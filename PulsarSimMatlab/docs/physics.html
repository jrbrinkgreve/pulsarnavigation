1. Pulsar timing and navigation

In short

A pulsar is a very stable clock in the sky. If you know where you are, you can predict when each pulse arrives. Measure the arrival time instead, and the difference tells you where you are: a pulse that is 1 µs early means you are 300 m closer to that pulsar.

A pulsar is a rotating neutron star. It turns with spin frequency f0 (here 100 Hz, so a period T of 10 ms) and slows down very slightly (F1). An ephemeris, taken from a pulsar catalogue, gives its rotational phase at any time:

\[ \phi(t) = \Phi_0 + f_0\,(t - T_\mathrm{ref}) + \tfrac{1}{2}\,F_1\,(t - T_\mathrm{ref})^2 \]
\(\phi(t)\)
rotational phase at time \(t\) [turns]; each whole number is one pulse
\(t\)
time at the receiver [s]
\(T_\mathrm{ref}\)
reference epoch of the phase model [s] (ephem.TRef)
\(\Phi_0\)
phase at \(T_\mathrm{ref}\) [turns] (Phi0 of foldProfile, default 0)
\(f_0\)
spin frequency at \(T_\mathrm{ref}\) [Hz] (ephem.f0 = 1/T = 100 Hz)
\(F_1\)
spin-down: rate of change of the spin frequency [Hz/s] (ephem.F1, 0 in the simulation)
  1. Each whole number of phase is one pulse.
  2. A time of arrival (TOA) is the measured arrival time of one chosen point of the pulse (here: phase 0 of the template), at one chosen frequency (the reference frequency, 1.6 GHz).
  3. Subtract the predicted arrival time. The difference times the speed of light is a distance along the line of sight to the pulsar: 1 µs ≈ 300 m.
  4. One pulsar gives one direction. Pulsars in several well-separated directions give the full position, as the figure shows.
What this project is, and is not (yet)

The voltage pipeline produces TOAs with honest error bars for one pulsar at a receiver that does not move. The motion of the receiver (Doppler) and the barycentric corrections are in block D: see phase prediction and localisation, frames and relativity. The navigation solution is stage 10: a batch fit for a fixed site and a Kalman filter for a moving ship (navigation). Several pulsars at full scale and the array follow in block E (roadmap).

2. What a pulsar signal looks like

In short

A pulsar does not send a pulse shape. It sends random noise that gets louder once per turn. So the information is in the power of the signal, not in its voltage.

Pulsar radio emission comes from a huge number of independent emitters. Their sum is Gaussian noise (the central limit theorem). What makes a pulsar a pulsar is that the variance of that noise, its power, rises once per turn. The simulator models the voltage as

\[ x(t) = A\,\sqrt{G(t)}\;n(t), \qquad \mathrm{E}\!\left[x^2(t)\right] = A^2\,G(t) \]
\(x(t)\)
the pulsar's voltage at the antenna, before receiver noise [V]
\(A\)
amplitude: the standard deviation of \(x\) at the pulse peak [V] (A = 1)
\(G(t)\)
the power profile: one Gaussian per turn with peak 1, FWHM 5 % of the period (dutycycle), standard deviation \(\sigma_t\) = 212.3 µs
\(n(t)\)
white Gaussian noise with variance 1
\(\mathrm{E}[\cdot]\)
expected value: the average over many noise realizations

There is no fixed waveform: every turn is a new piece of noise, and only its loudness repeats.

Design choice: square first, then average

Alternative: correlate the voltage with a known waveform, the way radar and GNSS receivers do.

Why not: there is no known waveform. Averaged over turns the voltage is zero, pulse or not (panel b above). Only the power repeats. So the processing must at some point square the voltage (detection) and then average many turns. Every step that needs the phase of the voltage (dedispersion, RFI blanking, later beamforming) must therefore come before the squaring.

Self-noise

Estimating a variance from a limited number of samples gives a scattered answer. A detected power bin averages |z|² over about B·Δt independent samples, so even without receiver noise its relative scatter is 1/√(B·Δt) (section 5). When the pulsar is bright, that scatter follows the pulse itself: largest at the peak, zero off-pulse. This self-noise limits the SNR of a very bright pulse. At the weak signals this project targets (−54 dB) it is negligible, but the noise model includes it.

In the code: generatePulsarSignal; the self-noise term in expectedPowerModel.

3. Interstellar dispersion

In short

Free electrons between the pulsar and us delay low frequencies more than high ones. Across this band, at the simulation's DM of 25, the delay differs by 31.5 ms. That is sixty times the pulse width, and more than three rotation periods. The delay is known exactly from one number, the DM, so it can be undone exactly.

A cold plasma has a frequency-dependent refractive index, so the group delay depends on frequency:

\[ \tau(f) = \frac{K\,\mathrm{DM}}{f^2}, \qquad K = 4.148808\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{s\,MHz^2\,pc^{-1}\,cm^{3}} \]
\(\tau(f)\)
dispersion delay at radio frequency \(f\), relative to infinite frequency [s]
\(f\)
radio frequency [MHz in this formula]; the band is 1200–1600 MHz (fLow, fHigh)
\(\mathrm{DM}\)
dispersion measure: the number of free electrons along the line of sight [pc cm⁻³] (DM = 25)
\(K\)
dispersion constant (from the electron charge and mass)

For DM = 25 pc cm⁻³ the delay is 40.52 ms at 1.6 GHz and 72.03 ms at 1.2 GHz. So each pulse is smeared over 31.51 ms across the 400 MHz band. That is sixty times the 0.5 ms pulse width, and longer than three rotation periods.

Several figures on these pages are drawn at DM 5 instead, where the smear is 6.30 ms, because the sweeps then stay apart and are easier to see.

History

The default DM was 5 until 20 Oct 2026, and has been 25 since.

Two properties make an exact correction possible:

  • It only changes phases. Dispersion is a linear filter with |H(f)| = 1: it moves no energy between frequencies. Its exact inverse is the conjugate filter, applied to the complex voltages (coherent dedispersion).
  • It commutes with the receiver bandpass. Both are linear time-invariant filters, so the order does not matter. The simulator can apply dispersion only inside the observed band and still be exact there.
Design choice: coherent dedispersion

Alternative: incoherent dedispersion. Split the band into channels, detect each, and shift the channels in time.

Why not: it leaves the smearing inside each channel (full-band page). Coherent dedispersion applies the inverse filter to the voltages and removes the delay exactly at every frequency. The channelized front end does both: it splits the band into 3.125 MHz channels and dedisperses each one coherently, so no smearing is left.

In the code: forward applyDispersionStream; inverse applyInverseDispersion and dedisperseChannels.

4. The receiver: noise, SNR and baseband

In short

The receiver adds its own noise, far stronger than the pulsar. The pulsar's strength is described by one number, ρ (snrDB in dB): the pulsar's power density at the pulse peak over the noise power density. The receiver then shifts the band down to around zero frequency and keeps complex samples, I and Q.

Receiver noise and the SNR definition

The receiver noise is described by the system-equivalent flux density, SEFD = 2kTsys/Aeff: the noise expressed as if it came from the sky. Signal strength is one number, used everywhere as snrDB:

\[ \rho = \frac{S_\mathrm{peak}}{\mathrm{SEFD}} = \frac{\text{pulsar power density at the pulse peak}}{\text{receiver-noise power density}}, \qquad \rho = 10^{\,\texttt{snrDB}/10} \]
\(\rho\)
signal strength: a ratio of power densities (per hertz) at the pulse peak, no unit (snrDB = 10 log₁₀ ρ)
\(S_\mathrm{peak}\)
the pulsar's flux density at the pulse peak [Jy]
\(\mathrm{SEFD}\)
system-equivalent flux density \(2kT_\mathrm{sys}/A_\mathrm{eff}\) [Jy]: the receiver noise expressed as a flux density, with \(k\) Boltzmann's constant, \(T_\mathrm{sys}\) the system temperature [K] and \(A_\mathrm{eff}\) the effective collecting area [m²]

In the simulation \(\rho = A^2/\sigma_n^2\), with \(A\) the pulsar amplitude of section 2 and \(\sigma_n\) the standard deviation of the receiver noise [V]. Two values matter:

  • −5 dB is a bright pulsar on a large dish. Most validation runs use it, because short runs then give precise checks.
  • About −54 dB is J0437−4715 on the target 0.61 m² array. This is the worst-case design point (scenario).
Design choice: SNR as a ratio of densities at the pulse peak

Alternative: an SNR per sample, per time bin, or of the folded profile.

Why not: those depend on choices made later in the pipeline (bandwidth, bin width, fold length). ρ depends only on the sky and the antenna, so one number describes a scenario for every stage.

Do not confuse ρ with "the SNR"

ρ is a per-hertz ratio at the pulse peak. The SNR of one pulse after matched filtering is ρ·√(B·σt·√π) ≈ 388·ρ for this pulsar and band. The SNR of a folded sub-integration grows with √(turns) (section 6). See also the glossary entry SNR.

From RF to complex baseband (IQ)

  1. The receiver multiplies the RF signal with a local oscillator (LO) at fLO. This shifts every frequency down by fLO.
  2. A low-pass filter keeps the shifted band and removes the rest.
  3. The result is sampled as complex numbers z = I + iQ: the complex envelope.
\[ x(t) = \mathrm{Re}\!\left\{ z(t)\, e^{\,i 2\pi f_\mathrm{LO}\, t} \right\}, \qquad f_\mathrm{baseband} = f_\mathrm{RF} - f_\mathrm{LO} \]
\(x(t)\)
the real voltage at the receiver input: pulsar + receiver noise + RFI [V] (not only the pulsar, as in section 2)
\(z(t)\)
the complex envelope \(I + iQ\) [V]
\(f_\mathrm{LO}\)
local-oscillator frequency [Hz] (fLO = 1.3 GHz)
\(f_\mathrm{RF}\)
a frequency in the radio band, 1.2–1.6 GHz [Hz]
\(f_\mathrm{baseband}\)
where that frequency ends up after the shift, here −100…+300 MHz [Hz]
\(i\), \(\mathrm{Re}\)
the imaginary unit; the real part

Complex samples keep the phase, which coherent dedispersion needs. They also keep positive and negative frequencies apart.

The complex sample rate must be at least the bandwidth of interest, 400 MHz. Here it is 800 MHz, for two reasons. The LO sits off centre on purpose, so the band lands at −100…+300 MHz. And the IQ low-pass filter needs a margin: its cutoff is at 360 MHz. (The RF simulation before the receiver runs at 4 GHz.) The whole frequency plan is drawn on the parameters page.

In the code: addNoiseAndRFI, applyIQmodulation.

5. Square-law detection and the radiometer equation

In short

Squaring the voltage and averaging it over a short time bin gives the power. Its noise falls as 1/√(B·Δt): the square root of the number of independent samples in the bin. The bandwidth decides how many samples are independent, not the sample rate.

Detection forms |z|² = I² + Q² and averages it over time bins of length Δt (about 1 µs). For Gaussian voltages the averaged power estimates the variance, with relative noise

radiometer equation\[ \frac{\sigma_P}{P} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{B_n\,\Delta t}} \]
\(P\)
the mean detected power of a time bin: the expected value of \(|z|^2\) [V²]
\(\sigma_P\)
standard deviation of the bin-averaged power around \(P\), from bin to bin [V²]
\(B_n\)
noise-equivalent bandwidth [Hz] (391.6 MHz here; noiseBandwidth)
\(\Delta t\)
length of a time bin [s] (≈ 1 µs; binLen/fs in detectPower)
\(B_n\,\Delta t\)
the number of independent samples in one bin

Bn is the noise-equivalent bandwidth. For a flat band it is simply the bandwidth B. With the tapered band edges used here it is 391.6 MHz for receiver noise (exact formula below).

The noise bandwidth of a tapered band

For a band shaped by a taper W(f):

\[ B_n = \frac{\left(\int W^2(f)\,df\right)^2}{\int W^4(f)\,df} \]
\(W(f)\)
amplitude response of the band: 1 inside, raised-cosine (sin²) edges 8 MHz wide, 0 outside; \(W^2\) is the shape of the power spectrum
\(f\)
frequency [Hz]; both integrals run over the band

The mean power passes ∫W², but the variance depends on ∫W⁴. For a flat band Bn = B. With the 8 MHz raised-cosine edges used here, Bn ≈ 391.6 MHz for receiver noise.

Where the radiometer equation comes from

For Gaussian voltages, Isserlis' (Wick's) theorem says: the covariance of two power samples is the square of the correlation between their voltages, \(\operatorname{cov}\!\left(|z_a|^2, |z_b|^2\right) = P^2\,|R(a-b)|^2\). Adding this up over all pairs in a bin of \(n\) samples gives its variance:

\[ \frac{\operatorname{var}(\hat P)}{P^2} = \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{a=0}^{n-1} \sum_{b=0}^{n-1} \left|R(a-b)\right|^2 \]
\(z_a\)
complex voltage sample number \(a\) in the bin [V]
\(\hat P\)
detected power of one time bin: the mean of \(|z_a|^2\) over its \(n\) samples [V²]
\(P\)
its expected value [V²]
\(n\)
samples per time bin (binLen)
\(a, b\)
sample indices within the bin
\(R(k)\)
normalized autocorrelation of the complex voltage at a lag of \(k\) samples: the inverse Fourier transform of the power spectrum \(W^2\), with \(R(0) = 1\)

By Parseval this becomes the ∫W⁴ above (the lines in panel b are exactly these sums). This one formula is the root of the whole noise model. In narrow channels it predicts that neighbouring time bins are correlated, because a bin is not much longer than the channel's correlation time (powerCovariance). With blanking it gives the exact variance of each bin (blankingWeights).

Design choice: detect, then fold, then match the template

Alternative: other orders, for example matched-filtering each pulse and adding the results, or folding before squaring.

Why this order: for noise whose variance follows a profile, the best possible test statistic (Neyman–Pearson) is a profile-weighted sum of |z|². Detection, folding and template matching compute exactly that, in the cheapest order: detection cuts the data rate by ~800× (800 complex samples become one power value per µs), and folding turns hours of data into one short profile before the fit.

6. Folding

In short

At weak signal one pulse is invisible. Folding adds up all turns at the same rotational phase. The pulse adds up, the noise averages down, and the SNR grows as the square root of the number of turns.

  1. The ephemeris gives the phase φ(t) of every detected time bin.
  2. Each time bin is added into the phase bin for its phase (2048 phase bins per turn).
  3. A sub-integration (sub-int) is a group of consecutive turns folded together. Each sub-int gives one TOA.
\[ \mathrm{SNR}_\text{sub-int} = \mathrm{SNR}_\text{turn}\,\sqrt{N} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N = \left(\frac{\mathrm{SNR}_\text{target}}{\mathrm{SNR}_\text{turn}}\right)^{2} \]
\(N\)
number of turns folded into one sub-int (SubintPeriods of foldProfile)
\(\mathrm{SNR}_\text{turn}\)
matched-filter SNR of the profile from a single turn (≈ 373·ρ for the simulated pulsar)
\(\mathrm{SNR}_\text{sub-int}\)
matched-filter SNR of the profile folded from \(N\) turns
\(\mathrm{SNR}_\text{target}\)
the SNR wanted per sub-int (design rule: 6–7)

The validated design rule is SNR ≥ 6–7 per sub-int; below that, TOAs start to lock onto noise peaks (threshold sweep).

In the code: foldProfile.

7. From a profile to a TOA, and how precise it can be

In short

The TOA is found by sliding the template along the folded profile until it fits best. The best possible precision is about the pulse width divided by the SNR. The timing information sits on the flanks of the pulse, where it changes fastest.

The folded profile is modelled as baseline + amplitude × template shifted by τ + noise. The fitted shift τ (in turns) gives the TOA:

\[ \mathrm{TOA} = t_\mathrm{ref} + \frac{\tau}{f_\mathrm{ref}} \]
\(\mathrm{TOA}\)
arrival time of template phase 0 at the dedispersion reference frequency (1.6 GHz) [s] (toa.toa)
\(t_\mathrm{ref}\)
the time at which the phase model reaches the sub-int's reference turn [s] (fold.subint.tRef)
\(\tau\)
the fitted shift of the template [turns]; not the dispersion delay \(\tau(f)\) of section 3
\(f_\mathrm{ref}\)
the spin frequency at \(t_\mathrm{ref}\) [Hz] (fold.subint.fRef); it converts turns to seconds. Not the 1.6 GHz radio reference frequency

The fit uses FFTFIT (Taylor 1992): a cross-correlation in the Fourier domain that handles shifts smaller than a bin exactly.

The best possible precision (Cramér–Rao bound)

In the weak-signal limit (pulse much weaker than the noise, the target regime), for a Gaussian pulse of width σt:

\[ \mathrm{SNR} \approx \rho\,\sqrt{B\,\sigma_t\sqrt{\pi}} \approx 388\,\rho, \qquad \sigma_\mathrm{TOA} \approx \frac{\sqrt{2}\,\sigma_t}{\mathrm{SNR}} \]
\(\mathrm{SNR}\)
the best possible SNR of one pulse (matched filter)
\(\rho\)
signal strength (section 4)
\(B\)
bandwidth [Hz] (400 MHz: fHigh − fLow)
\(\sigma_t\)
standard deviation of the Gaussian power profile [s] (212.3 µs)
\(\sigma_\mathrm{TOA}\)
the smallest possible standard deviation of the TOA [s] (Cramér–Rao bound)
The general formulas (any signal strength)

Model each detected bin as Gaussian with mean \(m(t) = P_n\,(1 + \rho\,p(t))\) and variance \(m^2/(B\,\Delta t)\). The Fisher information then gives

\[ \mathrm{SNR}^2 = B \int \frac{\left(\rho\,p(t)\right)^2}{\left(1 + \rho\,p(t)\right)^2}\,dt, \qquad \frac{1}{\sigma_\mathrm{TOA}^2} = B \int \frac{\left(\rho\,p'(t)\right)^2}{\left(1 + \rho\,p(t)\right)^2}\,dt \]
\(m(t)\)
expected detected power of a bin at time \(t\) [V²]
\(P_n\)
receiver-noise power [V²]
\(p(t)\)
pulse power profile with peak 1 (one turn of \(G(t)\))
\(p'(t)\)
its time derivative \(dp/dt\) [1/s]
\(B\), \(\Delta t\)
bandwidth [Hz]; time-bin length [s]
\(\int \ldots dt\)
over one turn

For ρp ≪ 1 the denominators are 1 and the weak-signal results above follow. When the pulse is strong, the (1 + ρp)² term is self-noise and caps the SNR. See estimateTOA.

Design choice: a template fit (FFTFIT), not the pulse centroid

Alternative: the centroid of the pulse, or the position of its highest bin.

Why not: the centroid also weights the noisy wings, which have long lever arms; it was measured to be about 2× noisier than FFTFIT (validation record). The highest bin is limited to the bin width and jumps with the noise. FFTFIT fits continuous shifts, is fast, and is the maximum-likelihood fit for white noise.

Honest error bars

The fit's stated σ follows from error propagation through the fit, using the observer's own noise model (fitted baseline and amplitude, radiometer equation, exact correlations between phase bins). Validation checks that the actual scatter of (TOA − truth) matches the stated σ: their ratio should be 1 (validation record).

8. Is the pulsar there? Detection theory

In short

A fit always finds a best position, even in pure noise. A separate test asks whether the peak is real: compare the matched-filter output with a threshold set by the accepted false-alarm rate. Searching all phases needs a higher threshold than looking at one known phase.

The test compares two hypotheses: H0 (baseline + noise) and H1 (baseline + template-shaped pulse + noise). The Neyman–Pearson lemma says the best test at a fixed false-alarm probability PFA is a matched filter compared with a threshold η.

  • Known phase (tracking: you know where the pulse should be): one test, η = Φ⁻¹(1 − PFA) = 3.09 for PFA = 10⁻³.
  • Unknown phase (acquisition: the pulse could be anywhere): search all phases and take the maximum. Rice's formula for the maximum of a smooth Gaussian process gives η = 4.15 at 10⁻³. At that threshold the search costs as much as about 60 independent trials. The "Rice factor" of 5.5 in that formula is not a number of trials: it is the number of times per turn that the noise crosses its mean level upwards.

A second check, the reduced χ² of the fit residuals, asks whether what is left after the fit is just noise. It catches a wrong profile shape (a radar spike), which detection cannot. Pictures of both tests: Detection and good TOAs.

Design choice: a good TOA = fitted, detected at unknown phase, and χ² ok

Alternative: accept every fitted TOA, or test detection only at the known phase.

Why not: FFTFIT searches all phases, so the fair question is "is the best peak anywhere real?", which is the unknown-phase test. And a TOA can pass detection while the profile is wrong; χ² catches that. In the threshold sweep the good TOAs had no outliers at any SNR (results).

9. Radio-frequency interference

In short

Man-made signals in the band are far stronger than the pulsar. Steady ones only cost some SNR. Short, strong pulses (radar) ruin TOAs. They must be found and blanked on the channel voltages, before dedispersion.

The simulator has five types:

TypeExampleWhat it looks likeEffect without excision
Carrier (cw)spurious toneone frequency, always onlowers SNR; TOAs stay valid
Spread spectrum (bpsk)GNSS L1, L21–20 MHz wide, always on, constant envelopelowers SNR; TOAs stay valid
PulsedL-band radar2 µs pulses at a repetition frequency, +20 dBdestroys TOAs with confident error bars
Impulsebroadband bursts200 ns bursts across the whole bandharmless after coherent dedispersion (spread over the 31.5 ms sweep)
Noise-like (noise)LTE / OFDM, Inmarsatband-limited Gaussian noise, indistinguishable from receiver noiseextra noise in its channels
Design choice: blank, do not subtract, and do it before dedispersion

Alternative: estimate the interference and subtract it, or remove it after dedispersion.

Why not: at −54 dB one radar pulse carries the energy of about 80,000 pulsar pulses. Even 99.9 % suppression would leave more interference than pulsar. So samples with RFI are blanked: set to zero, with weight 0.

And before dedispersion, a radar pulse is a 2 µs blip in a few channels. After dedispersion it is smeared over milliseconds and mixed with good data. So excision works on the channel voltages, before dedispersion.

10. Other propagation effects

In short

Scattering, the pulsar's spectrum, the ionosphere and motion are not in the simulator yet. Scintillation is in the fast simulator only. Motion matters most: the drift it causes is the navigation signal itself. How big each effect is: Limitations.

  • Scintillation. The received flux of a nearby pulsar fades and brightens by order 100 % over minutes to hours (below). The fast simulator includes it; the voltage simulation does not.
  • Scattering. The same multipath blurs the pulse with an exponential tail τd: ~0.4 ns at DM 5 (irrelevant), µs at DM ≳ 100.
  • Pulsar spectrum. Real pulsars are brighter at low frequencies (S ∝ f−1.6 on average); the simulator is white.
  • Ionosphere. The same 1/f² law as interstellar dispersion, but ~3–70 ns and changing with time; also Faraday rotation and ionospheric scintillation.
  • Motion. Earth's orbital speed (v/c ≈ 10⁻⁴) drifts the pulse phase by ~100 µs per second at 100 Hz. That drift is the navigation signal; following it needs a barycentric phase model (block D).

Interstellar scintillation

In short

The interstellar plasma is turbulent, so the pulsar's radio waves reach us along many slightly different paths that interfere. As the Earth, the pulsar and the plasma move, we drift through that interference pattern: the received flux fades and brightens, and differently at different frequencies. For J0437 at 1.4 GHz the bright patches ("scintles") are ~740 MHz wide and last ~40 minutes, so our whole band fades together, fully, a few times per hour. That changes how long a TOA takes, not where it is.

Where it comes from. Free electrons in the interstellar medium have a refractive index that varies with their density. Turbulence makes that density lumpy on many scales, so the plasma acts like a randomly corrugated lens, a "phase screen". A point source seen through it is split into many images whose waves add with random phases. Where they add up we see a bright scintle; where they cancel, almost nothing.

Two scales describe the pattern.

  • Scintle bandwidth Δνd: the waves' path differences are a fixed delay, so their relative phases change with frequency; two frequencies further apart than Δνd see independent patterns. It grows steeply with frequency (∝ ν4.4 for Kolmogorov turbulence) and shrinks with distance and DM.
  • Scintle time Δtd: the time to cross one scintle as the line of sight moves through the pattern (relative speed of pulsar, Earth and plasma). It grows with frequency (∝ ν1.2).

Statistics. When many paths with random phases add up, the field is complex Gaussian, so the intensity is exponentially distributed: mean 1 (the catalogue flux), but 10 % of the time below 0.1 and 5 % of the time above 3. Averaging over many scintles (a band much wider than Δνd, or a time much longer than Δtd) smooths this out; within one scintle there is no averaging.

J0437−4715 at 1.4 GHz (DM 2.6, 156 pc) has Δνd ≈ 740 MHz and Δtd ≈ 2290 s. These values come from the IAR timing campaign, rescaled from Keith et al. 2013. Observations at 327 MHz, rescaled, give two time scales instead: ~500 s and ~5700 s (Gwinn et al. 2006). The scales also vary noticeably from epoch to epoch, by factors of 2–3 (refractive scintillation).

Δνd is larger than our 400 MHz band, so the band holds about one scintle. There is little averaging over frequency, and the band-averaged flux is fully modulated in time.

What it does to a navigation receiver.

  • SNR budget: the SNR per sub-int follows the flux, so the time to reach SNR ~7 per TOA varies by large factors from one hour to the next. During a fade a sub-int may give no TOA at all; during a bright scintle a TOA comes quickly. That is why the sub-int length should adapt to the measured SNR (the fast simulator's adaptive sub-ints).
  • Across the band: scintles can be narrower than the band, for more distant pulsars or at lower frequencies (panel b). The pulse is then stronger in some channels than in others. The channel path's fit assumes equal strength in every channel (ChannelGain 1). So it weights the channels sub-optimally, and its χ² rises. The same happens in the edge channels, where the band taper weakens the pulse. For J0437 at 1.4 GHz the effect is a mild slope across the band, not a pattern.
  • Timing: diffractive scintillation changes the amplitude, not the arrival time (at DM 2.6 the scattering delay is a fraction of a nanosecond). So it costs observing time, not accuracy.

In the simulator (simulateFold, option 'Scintillation'): the pulse is multiplied by a flux factor F = |g|² per sub-int and channel. Here g(t, ν) is a complex Gaussian field with the two scales above: its intensity correlation drops to 1/e at Δtd and to ½ at Δνd. testSimulateFold 11 checks the exponential statistics and both correlation scales. Refractive variation, which is slow (weeks), is not included. More on the simulator page.
