1. Pulsar timing and navigation

A pulsar rotates with spin frequency f0 (here 100 Hz, period T = 10 ms) and a slow spin-down F1. An ephemeris (from a pulsar catalogue) gives the rotational phase at any time:

φ(t) = Φ0 + f0·(t − Tref) + ½·F1·(t − Tref)²

Each integer phase is a pulse. A time of arrival (TOA) is the measured arrival time of a chosen point of the pulse (here: the template's phase 0) at a chosen reference frequency. The difference between a measured TOA and the predicted one, times the speed of light, is a distance along the line of sight to the pulsar: 1 µs ≈ 300 m. With four or more pulsars in well-separated directions you solve for 3-D position and receiver clock offset, as with GNSS.

What this project is, and is not (yet)

The pipeline produces TOAs with uncertainties for one pulsar at a fixed receiver. Motion (Doppler), barycentric corrections and the navigation solution are later blocks (D and E, see roadmap).

2. What a pulsar signal looks like

Pulsar radio emission comes from a huge number of independent emitters. Their sum is, by the central limit theorem, Gaussian noise. What makes a pulsar a pulsar is that the variance (power) of that noise rises once per turn. The simulator models the voltage as

x(t) = A·√G(t)·n(t),    E[x²] = A²·G(t)

with n white unit-variance noise and G a periodic train of unit-peak Gaussians (the power profile).

Why this matters for the processing

Because the signal is noise, you cannot correlate the voltage with a known waveform. The information is in the power. So the processing must at some point square the voltage (detection) and then average many turns. Linear operations that need the phase (dedispersion, RFI blanking, beamforming) must happen before squaring.

Self-noise

Estimating a variance from a finite number of samples has scatter. A detected power bin averages |z|² over about B·Δt independent samples, so even with no receiver noise its relative scatter is 1/√(B·Δt). When the pulsar is bright, this scatter is proportional to the pulse itself: largest at the peak, zero off-pulse. This self-noise limits the SNR of a very bright pulse. At the weak signals this project targets (−54 dB) it is negligible, but the noise model includes it.

In the code: generatePulsarSignal; the self-noise term in expectedPowerModel.

3. Interstellar dispersion

Between the pulsar and us are free electrons. A cold plasma has a frequency-dependent refractive index, so the group delay depends on frequency:

τ(f) = K·DM / f²,    K = 4.148808×10³ s·MHz²·pc⁻¹·cm³

DM (dispersion measure) is the electron column density along the line of sight. For DM = 5 pc cm⁻³ the delay is 8.103 ms at 1.6 GHz and 14.406 ms at 1.2 GHz: each pulse is smeared over 6.30 ms across the 400 MHz band, which is more than ten times the 0.5 ms pulse width.

Two properties make exact correction possible:

  • Phase-only. Dispersion is a linear filter with |H(f)| = 1: it moves no energy between frequencies. Its exact inverse is the conjugate filter (coherent dedispersion), applied to the complex voltages.
  • It commutes with the receiver bandpass (both are linear time-invariant filters), so the simulator can apply dispersion only inside the observed band and still be exact there.
Why coherent and not incoherent dedispersion

Incoherent dedispersion splits the band into channels, detects each, and shifts channels in time. It leaves smearing inside each channel. Coherent dedispersion applies the inverse chirp to the voltages and removes the delay exactly at every frequency. The channelized front end does both: it splits into 3.125 MHz channels and dedisperses each one coherently, so there is no residual smearing either.

In the code: forward applyDispersionStream; inverse applyInverseDispersion and dedisperseChannels.

4. The receiver: noise, SNR and baseband

Receiver noise and the SNR definition

The receiver adds its own Gaussian noise, characterized by the system-equivalent flux density SEFD = 2kTsys/Aeff. This project describes signal strength with one number, used everywhere as snrDB:

ρ = Speak / SEFD = pulsar power spectral density at the pulse peak / receiver-noise power spectral density

It is independent of bandwidth, time resolution and dispersion, which is why it was chosen. In the simulation ρ = A²/σn². Values: −5 dB is a bright pulsar on a large dish (used for most validation runs); about −54 dB is J0437−4715 on the target 0.61 m² array (worst-case design point).

Do not confuse ρ with "the SNR"

ρ is a per-hertz power ratio at the pulse peak. The SNR of a pulse after matched filtering is ρ·√(B·σt·√π) ≈ 388·ρ for this pulsar and band, and the SNR of a folded sub-integration grows with √(turns). See section 7 and the glossary entry SNR.

From RF to complex baseband (IQ)

A receiver mixes the RF signal with a local oscillator (LO) at fLO, low-pass filters and samples. The result is the complex envelope z(t), with

x(t) = Re{z(t)·ei2πfLOt},    fbaseband = fRF − fLO

Complex samples keep the phase, which coherent dedispersion needs, at a sample rate equal to the bandwidth of interest (800 MHz here instead of the 4 GHz real RF simulation grid).

In the code: addNoiseAndRFI, applyIQmodulation.

5. Square-law detection and the radiometer equation

Detection forms |z|² = I² + Q² and averages it over time bins of length Δt (about 1 µs). For Gaussian voltages the averaged power is an estimate of the variance, with relative noise

σP / P = 1 / √(Bn·Δt) radiometer equation

where Bn is the noise-equivalent bandwidth. For a band shaped by a taper W(f):

Bn = (∫W²df)² / ∫W⁴df

The mean power passes ∫W², but the variance depends on ∫W⁴. For a flat band Bn = B. With the 8 MHz raised-cosine edges used here, Bn ≈ 391.6 MHz for receiver noise.

Where the radiometer equation comes from

For zero-mean complex Gaussian voltages, Isserlis' (Wick's) theorem gives the covariance of two power samples as the squared voltage correlation between them: cov(|za|², |zb|²) = |R(a−b)|². So the variance of a bin mean over n samples is

var(P̂) = (1/n²)·ΣaΣb |R(a − b)|²

which by Parseval becomes the ∫W⁴ above. This one formula is the root of the whole noise model. In narrow channels it predicts that neighbouring time bins are correlated (the bin is not much longer than the channel's correlation time); with blanking it gives the exact variance of each bin. See powerCovariance and blankingWeights.

Why detection, then folding, then template matching

For variance-modulated Gaussian noise, the optimal (Neyman–Pearson) statistic is a profile-weighted sum of |z|². Square-law detection followed by folding and a matched filter computes exactly that, in a cheap order.

6. Folding

One pulse at weak signal is buried in noise. Folding averages all turns at the same rotational phase: each detected time bin is assigned to a phase bin using the ephemeris phase φ(t). The pulse adds up coherently, the noise averages down, and the SNR grows as √(number of turns). A sub-integration (sub-int) is a group of consecutive turns folded together; each gives one TOA.

SNRsub-int = SNRturn·√N   ⇒   N = (SNRtarget / SNRturn)²

The validated design rule is SNR ≥ 6–7 per sub-int (below that, TOAs start to lock on noise peaks). At −54 dB a TOA of J0437 needs about 20 hours. See foldProfile and the threshold sweep.

7. From a profile to a TOA, and how precise it can be

The folded profile is modelled as baseline + amplitude × template shifted by τ + noise. The fitted shift τ (in turns) gives the TOA: TOA = tref + τ/fref. The fit uses FFTFIT (Taylor 1992), a cross-correlation in the Fourier domain that handles sub-bin shifts exactly.

The best possible precision (Cramér–Rao bound)

Model each detected bin as Gaussian with mean m(t) = Pn(1 + ρp(t)) and variance m²/(B·Δt). The Fisher information then gives

SNR² = B·∫ (ρp)² / (1 + ρp)² dt,    1/σTOA² = B·∫ (ρp′)² / (1 + ρp)² dt

In the weak-signal limit (ρp ≪ 1), for a Gaussian pulse of width σt:

SNR ≈ ρ·√(B·σt·√π) ≈ 388·ρ,    σTOA ≈ √2·σt / SNR

Timing information lives on the pulse flanks, where the profile changes fastest: a narrow pulse and a high SNR give a precise TOA. In the strong-signal limit self-noise caps the SNR. See estimateTOA.

Honest error bars

The fit's stated σ follows from first-order error propagation through the fit, using the observer's noise model (fitted baseline and amplitude, radiometer equation, exact correlations between phase bins). Validation checks that the actual scatter of (TOA − truth) matches the stated σ: their ratio should be 1. See validation record.

8. Is the pulsar there? Detection theory

A TOA fit always returns a peak, even on pure noise. A separate test asks whether the pulse is really there: H0 (baseline + noise) against H1 (baseline + template-shaped pulse + noise). The Neyman–Pearson lemma says the best test at a fixed false-alarm probability PFA is a matched filter compared with a threshold η.

  • Known phase (tracking: you know where the pulse should be): one test, η = Φ⁻¹(1 − PFA) = 3.09 for PFA = 10⁻³.
  • Unknown phase (acquisition: the pulse could be anywhere): search all phases and take the maximum. Rice's formula for the maximum of a smooth Gaussian process gives η = 4.15 at 10⁻³ (about 5.5 effective independent trials per turn).

A second check, the reduced χ² of the fit residuals, asks whether what is left after the fit is just noise. It catches something wrong in the profile (a radar spike), which detection cannot. See Detection and good TOAs.

9. Radio-frequency interference

Man-made signals in the 1.2–1.6 GHz band are far stronger than the pulsar. The simulator has five types:

TypeExampleWhat it looks likeEffect without excision
Carrier (cw)spurious toneone frequency, always onlowers SNR; TOAs stay valid
Spread spectrum (bpsk)GNSS L1, L21–20 MHz wide, always on, constant envelopelowers SNR; TOAs stay valid
PulsedL-band radar2 µs pulses at a repetition frequency, +20 dBdestroys TOAs with confident error bars
Impulsebroadband bursts200 ns bursts across the whole bandharmless after coherent dedispersion (spread over 6.3 ms)
(planned) noise-likeLTE / OFDMindistinguishable from noiseextra noise in its channels
Why blank, and why before dedispersion

At −54 dB one radar pulse carries the energy of about 80,000 pulsar pulses. Even 99.9 % suppression would leave more interference than pulsar, so samples with RFI must be blanked (set to zero, weight 0), not subtracted. Before dedispersion a radar pulse is a 2 µs blip in a few channels; after dedispersion it is smeared over milliseconds and mixed with good data. So excision works on the channel voltages, before dedispersion.

See rfiSource and RFI excision.

10. Other propagation effects (not yet simulated)

  • Scintillation. Turbulence in the interstellar plasma acts as a random phase screen: multipath. At low DM the interference pattern has bright patches ("scintles") 0.1–1 GHz wide, so the whole band fades by ~100 % on minutes-to-hours timescales. That affects the SNR budget, not the timing.
  • Scattering. The same multipath blurs the pulse with an exponential tail τd: ~0.4 ns at DM 5 (irrelevant), µs at DM ≳ 100.
  • Pulsar spectrum. Real pulsars are brighter at low frequencies (S ∝ f−1.6 on average); the simulator is white.
  • Ionosphere. The same 1/f² law as interstellar dispersion, but ~3–70 ns and time-variable; also Faraday rotation and ionospheric scintillation.
  • Motion. Earth's orbital speed (v/c ≈ 10⁻⁴) drifts the pulse phase by ~100 µs per second at 100 Hz. That drift is the navigation signal and needs a barycentric phase model (block D).
