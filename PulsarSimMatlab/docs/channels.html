Stages 5a–6b · Channelized front end

The default processing path. It splits the band into 128 channels of 3.125 MHz (5a), dedisperses each channel coherently to one common reference (5b), and detects each channel with an exact model of its noise (6b).

Why channels

The full-band path of stages 5–6 dedisperses the whole 400 MHz band at once. It was validated first. The channelized path splits the band before anything else, for four reasons.

The first and most important is that RFI excision needs it. At −54 dB, one radar pulse outweighs about 80,000 pulsar pulses. It must therefore be blanked on the voltages, before dedispersion spreads it out. In a 3.125 MHz channel, a 2 µs radar pulse is about 8 samples in about 2 channels: easy to find and cheap to remove. The full band has no place where it is that compact.

The second is memory at high DM. A full-band dedispersion filter grows with the sweep, to about 15 GB at DM 100. A channel's filter spans the sweep only at the channel rate of 4.17 MHz: about 10⁵ samples instead of 10⁸, one channel at a time (Section 5b.4). The third is a later use: arrival times per group of channels give a check of the DM from the data itself (planned; roadmap, longer term).

The fourth is that none of this costs precision. Each channel is still dedispersed coherently, on its voltage, so no smear is left inside a channel. The full-band page shows what shifting channels would leave. The full-band path stays as the validated reference: with all new options off, every changed function reproduces its old output bit for bit.

5a · channelizeIQ validated

info_chan = channelizeIQ(inFile, outBase, fs, fLO, fLow, fHigh, 'ChanWidth', 3.125e6, 'T0', info_IQ.t0)

Full interface and help text of channelizeIQ →

Stage 5a splits the 800 MHz IQ stream into 128 channels that tile 1.2–1.6 GHz exactly. Each channel is a complex voltage stream at 4.17 MHz, written to its own file.

5a.1 What a channel is

A channel is the output of a narrow receiver tuned to one slice of the band. The receiver mixes the slice down to 0 Hz, filters it with a low-pass and keeps every D-th sample:

(5a.1)\[ y_j(m) = \sum_n h(mD - n)\; x(n)\; e^{-i 2\pi (f_{c,j} - f_\mathrm{LO})\, n / f_s} \]
\(y_j(m)\)
complex sample \(m\) of channel \(j\) [V]
\(x(n)\)
complex IQ input sample \(n\) [V], at rate \(f_s\) = 800 MHz
\(f_{c,j}\)
centre of channel \(j\): \(f_\mathrm{Low} + (j - \tfrac12)\cdot\)ChanWidth [Hz]
\(f_\mathrm{LO}\)
LO of the IQ input, 1.3 GHz
\(h\)
the prototype low-pass filter (zero phase, unit DC gain)
\(D\)
decimation factor, 192

The exponential is the mixer, h is the low-pass, and evaluating the sum only at mD is the decimation. Sample m lies at t0 + mD/fs, because the filter delay is removed. Each channel file is therefore an ordinary IQ file, with LO fc,j and rate fs/D. The unit DC gain keeps tone amplitudes and power densities, so the channels' useful bands add up to the IQ band power.

Evaluated directly for 128 channels, Eq. (5a.1) costs about 2700 multiplications per input sample. Sections 5a.2 to 5a.5 build, one step at a time, a filterbank that gives the same numbers for about 27. Section 5a.6 assembles it.

5a.2 The prototype filter

The filter h appears in every channel, so its shape is every channel's shape. Ideally it is a block in frequency. Gain 1 inside the slice lets the slice through unchanged. Gain 0 outside keeps the neighbours and RFI elsewhere out. A block with edges at ±fcut is a sinc in time:

(5a.2)\[ h(r) = \frac{\sin(2\pi f_\mathrm{cut}\, r / f_s)}{\pi r} \]

Its zero crossings lie fs/(2fcut) samples apart. With fcut = 2.083 MHz, that is every 192 samples (Figure 5a.1a). Section 5a.5 explains this cut-off.

An exact block needs a sinc that never ends. The code tapers the sinc to zero with a Kaiser window. In frequency, each vertical edge becomes a slope, and the ripples outside the block fall below −80 dB. The block width and the slope width Δf are separate settings. The block width sets the spacing of the zero crossings. The slope width sets the length, by the Kaiser rule \(M = \lceil (A-8)/(2.285\cdot 2\pi\,\Delta f/f_s) \rceil + 1\). A slope of 1.042 MHz at A = 80 dB needs 3853 taps, or 4.8 µs.

At the block edge, half of the window's spectrum falls inside the block, so the gain there is exactly ½. The long filter does not give a fine resolution. Of its weight, 94 % lies within ±192 samples, so it resolves only about 4 MHz. The long tails sharpen the edges, not the resolution.

The prototype keeps the slice around 0 Hz. The channels, however, lie from 98 MHz below to 298 MHz above the LO. The next section moves the filter to them.

5a.3 One filter for every channel

Multiplying a filter by a phasor that turns at fk shifts its response by fk:

(5a.3)\[ h_k(r) = h(r)\, e^{+i 2\pi f_k r / f_s} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad H_k(f) = H(f - f_k) \]

Every channel's band-pass filter is therefore the prototype times a phasor. Only the centre moves, which is why h is called the prototype.

One inverse DFT applies all these phasors at once. Output k of a K-point inverse DFT multiplies its input by \(e^{+i2\pi kr/K}\) and sums. That phasor turns at k·fs/K. Applied to a frame weighted by h, output k is the frame filtered by hk. With K = fs/ChanWidth = 256, the outputs lie one channel width apart. The frame is longer than K samples, and Section 5a.4 deals with that.

Filtering with a shifted prototype is the same as mixing first and filtering after. Take the frame that ends at input sample c, with the taps r counted back from c. Substituting n = c − r gives

(5a.4)\[ \sum_r h(r)\, e^{+i 2\pi f_k r/f_s}\, x(c - r) \;=\; e^{+i 2\pi f_k c/f_s} \sum_n \Big[ x(n)\, e^{-i 2\pi f_k n/f_s} \Big]\, h(c - n) \]

The bracket is the mixer of Eq. (5a.1), and the outer sum is the low-pass. Section 5a.6 removes the leftover rotation in front. Output k is therefore a receiver tuned to fk, and a channel's content comes out there for that reason.

The DFT outputs must also land on the channel centres. The LO sits on the edge between channels 32 and 33. The channel centres therefore lie half a step off the DFT grid: \(f_{c,j} - f_\mathrm{LO} = (k_j + \beta)\, f_s/K\), with β = ½. The DFT does the whole-number part kj. The code multiplies the common β into the prototype once, \(h_b(r) = h(r)\,e^{+i2\pi\beta r/K}\). Below the LO, kj is negative, and its phasor equals that of kj + 256. Those channels come from the last rows (Figure 5a.2a).

One loose end remains: the inverse DFT has 256 points, but the frame has 4096 samples.

5a.4 Folding the frame

A 4096-point DFT of the weighted frame would give bins every 0.195 MHz, but only every 16th bin is a channel centre. Split the weighted frame \(v(r) = h_b(r)\,x(c-r)\) into P = 16 blocks of K = 256 samples, with r = qK + p:

(5a.5)\[ \sum_{r=0}^{L-1} v(r)\, e^{+i 2\pi k r/K} \;=\; \sum_{p=0}^{K-1} e^{+i 2\pi k p/K} \underbrace{\sum_{q=0}^{P-1} v(qK + p)}_{u(p)} \]

For a whole number k, \(e^{i2\pi kq} = 1\), so every block sees the same 256 phasor values. The blocks can therefore be added first, which is the fold u(p), and transformed once with a 256-point DFT. Output k is fine bin 16k. The bins in between turn a fraction of a turn per block, so they would need different phasor values in each block. The fold has lost that information. Folding by 16 in time keeps every 16th bin in frequency.

Worked example: 8 samples folded into 4

The fold of x0 … x7 is (x0+x4, …, x3+x7). Bin 2 of the 8-point DFT has the phasors 1, i, −1, −i, 1, i, −1, −i. They repeat after 4 samples, so bin 2 equals output 1 of the folded 4-point DFT. Bin 1 has the phasors 1 and −1 at r = 0 and 4. It needs x0 − x4, which the fold has lost.

Nothing useful is lost by the fold. The weighted frame's spectrum is the input convolved with the 4 MHz block. It is smooth on the scale of a fine bin, and neighbouring fine bins correlate at 0.986 (Figure 5a.3). The frequency within a channel is not lost either. It shows from frame to frame: a tone 1 MHz above a channel centre makes that channel's output turn at 1 MHz. Stage 5b needs this frequency, because the dispersion delay changes across a channel. It resolves the frequency with long FFTs along the channel stream. One frame gives the channel, and the stream gives the frequency within it.

The fold also explains why β sits in hb. Its phasor turns only half a turn per block, so it must be applied before the fold.

One frame gives one sample of every channel. The next section asks how far the frame moves between samples.

5a.5 Sliding the frame

The next frame starts D = 192 input samples later, so every channel is sampled at fs/D = 4.17 MHz. Sampling makes the channel's spectrum repeat every 4.17 MHz, so its response gains copies (orange in Figure 5a.4). The copies must stay out of the useful band, which ends at ChanWidth/2 = 1.5625 MHz. The nearest copy must therefore already be in its stopband there. So the stopband must start at fs/D − 1.5625 MHz = 2.604 MHz. The slope fits between the end of the useful band and the start of the stopband:

(5a.6)\[ \Delta f = \frac{f_s}{D} - \mathrm{ChanWidth} = 1.042\ \mathrm{MHz}, \qquad f_\mathrm{cut} = \frac{f_s}{2D} = 2.083\ \mathrm{MHz} \]

These are the two numbers that Section 5a.2 took as given. With this cut-off, the prototype's zero crossings lie exactly D samples apart.

Sliding by K = 256 instead is called critical sampling. It makes Δf zero, so the filter would have to be infinitely long. With our prototype, the copies would reach 1 MHz into the useful band (Figure 5a.4b).

Second, the response is not flat out to ±2.083 MHz, so consecutive channel samples are slightly correlated. detectRFI and powerCovariance take this into account.

5a.6 The complete algorithm

Each output time m combines the four ingredients (Figure 5a.5).

  1. Weight the 4096 input samples that end at c = mD + G by hb. The prototype is then centred on sample mD (5a.2, 5a.3).
  2. Fold them into 256 bins (5a.4).
  3. Do one 256-point inverse FFT. Output k is tuned to (k + β)·3.125 MHz (5a.3).
  4. Correct each channel's phase, and keep the 128 rows inside the band.

The phase correction of step 4 removes the leftover rotation of Eq. (5a.4). The DFT measures its phasor from the frame's end, while a mixer turns with absolute time. The code multiplies by \(e^{-i2\pi(k+\beta)c/K}\), computed in double precision from the absolute index c. Without it, the phase of channel k would jump by 2π(k + β)·¾ from frame to frame.

The cost per output time is 4096 multiplications for the weights, about 1000 for the FFT and 128 for the phases. That is about 27 per input sample, against about 2700 for Eq. (5a.1) evaluated directly.

The exact formula

With fc,j − fLO = (kj + β)·fs/K (β = ½ here: band edges on the K-grid) and the prototype h(r) centred at G = (M−1)/2, zero-padded to L = P·K:

\[ \begin{gathered} y_j(m) = e^{-i 2\pi (k_j + \beta)(mD + G)/K} \sum_{p=0}^{K-1} e^{+i 2\pi k_j p / K}\, u_m(p) \\[4pt] u_m(p) = \sum_{q=0}^{P-1} h_b(p + qK)\; x(mD + G - p - qK) \end{gathered} \]
\[ h_b(r) = h(r)\, e^{+i 2\pi \beta r / K} \]

The result is 128 ordinary IQ streams at 4.17 MHz, as Section 5a.1 promised. RFI excision works on them first. Stage 5b then dedisperses each channel and trims it to its useful band.

Summary
  • A channel mixes, filters and decimates (Eq. 5a.1).
  • The prototype is a tapered sinc. Its block edge at 2.083 MHz and its 1.04 MHz slope both follow from the slide D.
  • The inverse DFT shifts the prototype to every channel and mixes each channel down to 0 Hz.
  • Folding 4096 samples into 256 keeps every 16th fine bin, which are the channel centres.
  • Sliding by 192 samples each channel at 4.17 MHz, and the copies stay below −80 dB in the useful band.

Design choices

One number was taken as given throughout: the channel width.

128 channels of 3.125 MHz

The channel width is a trade-off. Narrower channels blur a short RFI pulse in time, because a channel of width B cannot show anything shorter than about 1/B. They also leave fewer independent samples per detected bin (Section 6b.2). Wider channels make a carrier or a GNSS signal cost more of the band once its channel is down-weighted, and they lengthen each channel's dedispersion filter. At 3.125 MHz, a 2 µs radar pulse is about 8 samples in about 2 channels, and a carrier costs 1/128 of the band. And 800 MHz / 3.125 MHz gives a whole number of DFT slots: 256.

Tests (tests/testChannelizeIQ.m)

The tests check Eq. (5a.1) sample by sample, and the properties of Section 5a.1.

  • Tones at, beside and far from channel centres. Every sample equals the exact prediction to ≤ 2.7·10⁻⁷, the single-precision limit. This one test pins down frequency mapping, LO phase, timing and gain at once. The passband is flat to |H| = 1 ± 1.2·10⁻⁴, and the stopband is at −80.2 dB.
  • White noise. The channel power divided by Σh² is 1.0007. The passband level is 1.0004 of the expected value, and flat within ±3 %.
  • Real data (seed 43, −5 dB). The channel band powers add up to the IQ band power, to 1.00007. So there are no gaps and no double counting (Parseval). 0.1 s of data becomes 128 × 416,667 samples in 4.1 s (427 MB).
  • Other LOs. With the LO on a channel centre (β = 0) or off the 3.125 MHz grid (β = 0.18), the tones still match to 2.9·10⁻⁷. The channel files equal those with today's LO to 1.4·10⁻⁷: a channel depends only on the RF signal, not on where the LO sits.
  • Oversampling 2 (the 3.125 MHz channels sampled at 6.25 MHz, 1285 taps). The passband is flat to 1.6·10⁻⁴, and the stopband is at −79.6 dB. Tones match to 2.5·10⁻⁷. A tone 4 MHz from a channel centre aliases to −2.25 MHz, outside the useful ±1.5625 MHz.
  • Block size. Four values of BlockOut, down to one output sample per block, give every channel file byte for byte.
  • Bad grids, bands and files are refused, each with its own error id.

Each channel is now an ordinary IQ file at 4.17 MHz. The pulse in it is still dispersed, both across the channel and relative to the other channels. Stage 5b removes both.

5b · dedisperseChannels validated

info_dc = dedisperseChannels(info_chan, outBase, DM, 'RefFreq', refFreq)

Full interface and help text of dedisperseChannels →

Stage 5b dedisperses every channel coherently, and brings all channels to one common reference frequency (1.6 GHz, the top of the band). Afterwards a pulse arrives at the same time in every channel.

5b.1 One validated filter, run per channel

Because each channel is an ordinary IQ file, the validated applyInverseDispersion of stage 5 runs on it unchanged, one channel at a time. Its band is set to the channel centre ± ChanWidth/2. The inverse chirp is zero outside that band, which also removes the channelizer's slopes, so the channels tile the band exactly.

Reusing this function keeps one validated kernel for two front ends. It already had its leakage check and its fully supported range. The only change was the option AllowRefOutsideBand of Section 5b.2. With that option off, the full-band output is bit-identical.

5b.2 One reference for all channels

Run per channel, the filter still needs a reference frequency. Every channel uses RefFreq = 1.6 GHz, with AllowRefOutsideBand. The inverse chirp is exact for any reference: its phase is the dispersion term, plus a pure delay and a constant. With a common reference, each channel's delay to 1.6 GHz is part of the same filter. All channels therefore come out aligned.

The alternative is to dedisperse each channel to its own centre and then shift it by its delay to 1.6 GHz. That shift is a whole number of samples plus a fractional delay, which costs an extra FFT pass per channel. The common reference costs nothing extra and needs no interpolation.

5b.3 A taper inside each channel

Trimming each channel to its useful band leaves the shape of its edges to choose. Each channel's band gets a taper inside its own edges (EdgeFrac 0.02 of the channel: 62.5 kHz). Neighbouring channels then do not overlap, so their noise is exactly independent. Overlapping, power-complementary tapers would keep the summed band flat at the boundaries, but neighbours would share noise, and every later step would have to carry that correlation.

The price is a small notch at each of the 127 boundaries. Together these cost 2.5 % of band power, the same as the full band's two edges. In total ∫W² = 390.0 MHz, as in the full-band path. The noise bandwidth is BnoiseChan = 3.0596 MHz per channel, and 128 × that = 391.6 MHz in total (BnoiseTotal).

5b.4 Memory per channel

The per-channel filter also solves the memory problem that was the second reason for channels. A channel's filter spans that channel's sweep to the reference, at 4.17 MHz. For the bottom channel at DM 100 that is about 5·10⁵ samples, or about 120 MB. A full-band filter at the same DM needs about 15 GB (Figure 5b.1). At DM 5 the FFT sizes are 2¹⁴…2¹⁸, the largest leakage is 3·10⁻¹² (2·10⁻¹² at DM 25), and all 128 channels take 1.7 s.

Tests (tests/testDedisperseChannels.m)

Four tests check the stage.

  1. Regression: the full-band applyInverseDispersion with defaults is bit-identical to test_IQ_dedispersed.dat.
  2. Commutation, the precise test. It compares channelize(full-band dedispersed) with dedisperseChannels(channelize(IQ)). Both are linear filters, so the order must not matter. In channels 4–125 the correlation is ≥ 0.999993, the phase difference ≤ 1.2·10⁻⁵ rad, and the power ratio 1 ± 3·10⁻⁵. This proves that the per-channel chirps, the delays between channels and the phases are exact.
  3. End to end. The TOAs of the channel path and of the full-band path on the same data (seed 43, −5 dB) agree: over 8 TOAs the mean difference is +0.19 µs and the rms 0.51 µs (0.18σ). The two paths keep slightly different parts of the same noise (correlation 0.979), so their differences should scatter by σ·√(2(1−ρ)) = 0.205σ. χ² is 5.98 for 8 TOAs: consistent.
  4. Time-bin noise of the summed channel power. The variance is 0.884 of the radiometer value (predicted 0.888 ± 0.005), and the lag-1 correlation 0.054 (predicted 0.052 ± 0.004). This test revealed the narrow-channel effect of Section 6b.2.

After 5b, a pulse arrives at the same time in every channel. What remains is to turn the voltages into power.

6b · detectChannels validated

info_det = detectChannels(info_dc, outFile, f_out)

Full interface and help text of detectChannels →

Stage 6b squares and bins every dedispersed channel into one file of [128 channels × time bins], the layout foldProfile reads. It also attaches the exact noise model of the bins.

6b.1 Square and bin each channel

detectChannels runs the validated detectPower of stage 6 on every channel. It interleaves the results into one float32 file and deletes the temporary files. For 0.1 s of 128 channels this takes 0.5 s.

A bin holds a whole number of channel samples: the bin rate is fs/round(fs/fout). Here that is 4 samples, 0.96 µs, or 1.0417 MHz. The function warns once if this differs from the requested 1 MHz. A bin boundary inside a sample would need interpolation and would break the exact noise model. And 0.96 µs is close to the 1 µs of the full-band path.

The info carries detectPower's fields that the fold uses (file, nChan, N, binTime0, binDt, fullySupportedBins, byteOrder, chanFreqs, …). It adds chanWidth, chanMeanPower, BnoiseChan, BnoiseTotal and noise: the powerCovariance struct of Section 6b.3, the same for every channel and bin without blanking.

6b.2 Narrow-channel bins are correlated

noise = powerCovariance(chanWidth, edgeWidth, fs, nPerBin, ...)

validated · Full interface and help text of powerCovariance →

A separate noise model is needed because the simple one fails in narrow channels. The radiometer equation, var = m²/(B·Δt), treats time bins as independent. That needs B·Δt ≫ 1. In the full band, B·Δt ≈ 400. In a 3.125 MHz channel with 0.96 µs bins it is only about 3. The detected power stays correlated for about 1/Bch ≈ 0.3 µs, which is a good part of a bin.

Two things follow (Figure 6b.1). Each bin has less variance than the formula says: 0.888 of it. And neighbouring bins are correlated: +0.046 at lag 1.

6b.3 The exact noise model

Both numbers follow exactly from the channel's spectrum. A bin is the mean of |y|² over n samples. For a Gaussian y with normalized autocorrelation R, Isserlis' theorem (see physics) gives the bin's variance and covariances, relative to the radiometer value:

(6b.1)\[ V = \frac{C(0)}{\mathrm{rad}}, \qquad X_L = \frac{C(L)}{\mathrm{rad}}, \qquad C(L) = \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{a=0}^{n-1} \sum_{b=0}^{n-1} \left|R(a - b + Ln)\right|^2, \qquad \mathrm{rad} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{l} \left|R(l)\right|^2 \]
\(V\)
variance of one detected time bin, relative to the radiometer value \(m^2/(B\,\Delta t)\)
\(X_L\)
covariance between a bin and the bin \(L\) further, on the same relative scale
\(L\)
lag in time bins, 1 … Lmax (not the frame length \(L\) of 5a)
\(C(L)\)
the Isserlis sum for bins \(L\) apart (relative to \(m^2\))
\(\mathrm{rad}\)
the radiometer value \(1/(B\,\Delta t)\) written as a sum over \(R\)
\(n\)
channel samples per time bin (4 at 0.96 µs)
\(a, b\)
sample indices within a bin
\(R(l)\)
normalized autocorrelation of the channel voltage \(y\) at lag \(l\) samples, \(R(0) = 1\)
\(m\), \(B\), \(\Delta t\)
mean bin power; bandwidth \((\int S)^2/\int S^2\) of the channel spectrum \(S = W^2\); bin length

The spectrum is W² of the per-channel taper of Section 5b.3; the chirp is all-pass and drops out. Summed over all lags, V + 2ΣXL = 1 exactly: the independent-bin model is right for long sums, but wrong bin by bin. The tail falls as 1/L², because the channel edges are sharp. Lmax is the smallest lag with V + 2Σ≤LX ≥ 1 − 0.002.

Carrying this exactly may look excessive, because within one phase bin the two effects nearly cancel: the variance is 0.987 of the simple model. The effect looks negligible, but there are three reasons to carry it. Error bars and false-alarm thresholds must be exact at weak signal. Phase bins can be shorter for other pulsars. And the same machinery is needed for blanking anyway, where the effect is large (±45 %). So V and X(L) travel with the data through the fold (NoiseCoeffs).

Summary
  • Stage 5b runs the validated full-band dedispersion on every channel, with one common reference, so the channels come out aligned.
  • A taper inside each channel keeps neighbouring channels independent, at the same 2.5 % of band power as the full band.

On the channel path the reduced χ² is 1.008, all sub-ints are detected, and the noise ratio is 0.990. Per sub-int, the two paths differ by 0.26σ rms. Two differences remain, and both are understood. The channel path has one TOA fewer, because the per-channel dedispersion guard (20/62.5 kHz = 320 µs) leaves out the first 0.32 ms of the file. The file starts exactly on a turn boundary, so turn 0 is then incomplete. Both paths lose the last turn to the sweep, which is 6.3 ms at the DM 5 of this run (timeline on the full-band page). Its error bars are 2.2 % larger, because the simulator tapers only the pulsar at the band edges, not the receiver noise (see limitations). With a realistic receiver the difference would be 0.37 %.
