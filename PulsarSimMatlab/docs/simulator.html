Stages 1–7 in one step · Fast simulator: simulateFold validated

The fast simulator draws the folded profiles directly from their known statistics, instead of simulating every voltage sample. Hours of observation then take seconds. Everything after the fold (foldToTOA: TOAs, detection, good TOAs) runs unchanged on the result. It stands in for stages 1–7 when a run lasts hours to days. For a moving receiver with a real clock it also places each pulse where the true trajectory and clock put it.

In short

A J0437 TOA needs 16 hours of data at nominal flux, and 82–95 hours in the worst case (scenario). The voltage chain writes about 72 GB per second on the default path, so hours are out of reach.

But after the fold, everything is described by a few validated numbers per phase bin: the mean (baseline plus the smoothed pulse), the variance (the radiometer equation), and the correlation with the neighbouring bins. simulateFold draws exactly that. 20 hours in 60 s sub-ints take 0.05 s.

It reproduces the voltage chain within its statistics, in three settings: the full band from the detection threshold to +20 dB; the channel path, including the slightly high χ² of the edge channels; and realistic RFI after excision, using profiles measured on the voltage chain.

For a moving receiver, trueResiduals gives each sub-int's true pulse phase against the observer's prediction. With it the scenario runs carry a site error and a real clock.

The idea

The fold of one sub-int sums many detected time bins in each phase bin: about 5 per turn at 100 Hz, 2048 bins and 1 µs, times the number of turns. By then each phase bin is Gaussian. Its mean, its variance and its covariance with the neighbouring bins follow from the noise model that estimateTOA and detectPulsar already use. The voltage chain validated that model end to end (the multi-seed Monte Carlo and the long noise-only run runH0).

So the simulator draws the fold struct straight from that model, with the same fields and conventions as foldProfile. The true values go into a separate truth struct: the true pulse phase of every sub-int, ρ, the gains and the RFI. The observer gets only fold and info, which hold nothing that foldProfile's would not. That is the ground-truth rule.

How a fold is drawn

  1. Mean of each phase bin. The baseline is 1, the receiver-noise power. On top comes the pulse: by default the generator's periodic Gaussian with peak 1 and FWHM 5 %. With 'Components' it is a sum of Gaussians scaled to peak 1, such as B0329+54's five components; with them the simulator matches the voltage chain within about 1 % (expComponentsChain). The pulse is smoothed the way the fold smooths it: averaged over one time bin, and spread over ±1 phase bin by the linear assignment. Then it is shifted to the true pulse phase. All of this is computed exactly, in Fourier space.
  2. Weights. If the time bins land evenly in phase, a phase bin collects n time bins per sub-int. Then weight = n, weight2 = 2n/3 and weightX = n/6: the exact averages of the linear split. Neighbouring bins then share noise with correlation ¼, the value runH0 measures. For validation, the weights of a real fold can be copied instead.
  3. Noise. Gaussian, with the variance of the radiometer equation, m²/(Bn·binDt)·weight2/weight², and the neighbour correlation. It uses m² rather than 1², so the pulsar's own noise is included. The correlation matrix of a sub-int is circulant, so the noise is drawn exactly with two FFTs.
  4. Bookkeeping. The sub-int times (tRef, fRef, turnRef) are made as foldProfile makes them. The truth gets each sub-int's true arrival time (pulseCenters), so validateTOA reads it directly.

Channels

The default path folds 128 channels separately. Three things differ from the full band:

  1. Correlated time bins. In a 3.125 MHz channel, consecutive 0.96 µs detected bins are correlated: V 0.888, X(1) 0.046, and so on up to 7 lags ('NoiseCoeffs', from detectChannels). For time bins spread evenly in phase, the weights have a closed form: \( W_2 = n\sum_L c(L)\,K(L\delta) \), \( W_X(d) = n\sum_L c(L)\,K(d - L\delta) \), with \(c(0) = V\), \(c(\pm L) = X(L)\), δ = f0·binDt·N the phase-bin step between time bins and K(s) the overlap of two assignment triangles s bins apart (2/3 − s² + |s|³/2 below 1, (2 − |s|)³/6 up to 2). It matches foldProfile to 2·10⁻⁵.
  2. The true pulse fraction per channel ('ChannelGain', part of the truth). The simulated pulsar passed an 8 MHz band-edge taper that the noise did not. So channels 1 and 128 hold 0.023 of a normal channel's pulse, channels 2 and 127 hold 0.417, and channels 3 and 126 hold 0.960 (roadmap item 3). The observer still fits with gain 1, as main does.
  3. Per-channel noise, independent between channels, each drawn by FFT.

RFI after excision

Excision acts on voltages, which the simulator never makes. So what excision leaves in each channel is measured instead, once. The measurement is a short voltage-chain run without a pulsar: zero sky, receiver noise and RFI, through processIQ with excision (runRFIProfile.m). rfiProfile then measures three things from the detected power of every channel:

  • the level Lc: the mean power, relative to the receiver-noise level. Constant RFI that excision leaves in raises it; LTE reaches 31× in its channels.
  • the true noise coefficients Vc and Xc(L). A channel partly covered by noise-like RFI has a narrower spectrum, and is noisier than the observer's flat model. A channel with a strong carrier is quieter relative to its mean: (1 + 2I)/(1 + I)², which is 0.36 for the local carrier at I ≈ 4.
  • the valid fraction: 1 − the blanked fraction. In the realistic scenario 0.29 % is blanked.

With 'RFI', channel c's mean is Lc + the pulse, and its weights are scaled by the valid fraction. Its noise is drawn with the measured coefficients, while the fold carries the observer's clean model. So the observer's fit makes exactly the mistakes it makes on real data. Optimal weighting down-weights the loud channels, which costs SNR. And the error bars are too small in the partly covered channels.

These are time-domain numbers, so a profile transfers to any pulsar, phase-bin count and sub-int length. What the model assumes, and how it is checked

The profile is a statistical model, not a recording. The simulator draws fresh noise with the measured statistics in every sub-int. That rests on four assumptions:

  1. The RFI is stationary. Its 1 s average holds every hour, every day.
  2. Time bins are correlated only over the measured lags (Lmax = 7, 6.7 µs), so their second moments fix the folded noise. RFI narrower than about 1 MHz correlates further. A 200 kHz carrier's power has a sidelobe at lags 7–20 that holds 5–7 % of its long-run noise. With 7 lags, the neighbour-bin correlation of short phase bins, and the noise of the scenario's long phase bins, come out about 5–7 % low in such channels. So rfiProfile can measure more lags ('Lmax'), and runRFIProfile uses 30 (testRFIModel 6).
  3. Folded profiles are Gaussian.
  4. Blanking is not locked to the pulse phase, and does not depend on the data. False flags on strong continuous RFI break this. detectRFI cut the power peaks of narrow carriers, and phase bins with blanking then came out 4–9 % noisier than modelled (the narrow-Inmarsat case). The observer's noise calibration absorbs this. The per-channel detectRFI thresholds now remove those false flags (excision). With them and 30 lags, testRFIChain passes in the narrow-Inmarsat case too: noise relative to the model 1.3286 real against 1.3292 simulated.

Three checks test these assumptions:

  1. testRFIModel uses synthetic data with a closed-form truth. rfiProfile recovers the level, the noise coefficients and the valid fraction of a carrier, of noise-like narrowband RFI and of blanked impulses. Folds of that data by foldProfile, and by simulateFold with the measured profile, agree in level, weight, noise relative to the observer's model, neighbour correlation and TOA statistics. And RFI correlated beyond the measured lags is detected: the real fold is 3.3× noisier than the model.
  2. expSimRFI part 1 compares with runRFITest on the voltage chain: they agree within about 1 %.
  3. testRFIChain (slow) makes the same fold comparison on a voltage-chain run, with the chain's real RFI and excision: the profile comes from the first half, the fold from the second half. It tests (b)–(d). On the current code (per-channel thresholds, 30 lags) both cases pass:
    • (b) holds. The noise relative to the observer's model is 1.0729 real against 1.0729 simulated at 1 turn in the realistic case, and 1.3286 against 1.3292 with narrow carriers. So the measured lags carry all the correlation. Level and flatness agree too.
    • (c) holds for sub-ints of minutes. A phase bin of a one-turn sub-int averages only about 30 complex samples, so real folds are slightly non-Gaussian: an excess kurtosis of about 0.18. The partly covered channels and, unexpectedly, the carrier channel are more non-Gaussian. The test therefore merges the one-turn fold in groups of 4 turns and checks that the kurtosis falls to a quarter, as for independent turns. It does in every channel: 0.0514 against the expected 0.0537 in the realistic case. So the extra non-Gaussianity averages out, and sub-ints of minutes are Gaussian.

Observing schedule

Long observations come in passes: the pulsar is above the horizon for some hours a day. Two options describe this. 'Passes' gives the start and end of each pass, in seconds. 'SubintTime' gives the sub-int length in seconds, rounded to whole turns as in foldProfile.

The simulator then makes complete sub-ints only inside passes, from the complete turns that fit. The gaps hold no data. Times are absolute (tRef = TRef + turnRef/f0), so TOAs from different passes line up on one time axis. truth.pass says which pass each sub-int belongs to. One pass covering turns 0 … n−1 gives exactly the fold of the plain nTurns interface.

Until 21 Oct 2026 the J0437 runs used a single Gaussian: power-profile FWHM 0.141 ms = 0.0245 turns, NBin 512 (11.2 µs bins, FWHM/12.5), and ρ = −51.0 dB (worst case) or −47.4 dB (nominal). 20 h × 128 channels in 60 s sub-ints was then about 1.3 GB of folds. The example below keeps those settings.

Dual polarization ('NPol'). The array receives two polarizations, and the processing adds their detected powers into total intensity. The simulated fold is then the average of NPol independent polarizations. An unpolarized pulsar has the same ρ in each, so the baseline and the pulse stay as they are. The receiver noise is independent between the polarizations, so its variance drops by a factor NPol. That is the same as an NPol times larger noise bandwidth, which is what the observer passes to foldToTOA.

Unlike raising 'Bnoise', this also works with an RFI profile, whose bandwidth is fixed by the calibration run. The fluctuations that RFI leaves are taken as independent between the polarizations too. NPol 1 (the default) is the single stream of the voltage chain, bit for bit (testSimulateFold 13).

Scintillation

The pulsar's flux fades and brightens through interstellar scintillation (physics). So each sub-int and channel gets a true flux factor F that multiplies its pulse. F is either given ('FluxScale', [nSub × 1] or [nSub × NChan]), or drawn ('Scintillation', with the scintle bandwidth and time):

  1. White complex noise on a time × frequency grid (steps of a tenth of the scintle time; 64 or more points across the band and a frequency step of at most a quarter of the scintle bandwidth, padded).
  2. Smoothed with Gaussian kernels along both axes, so the field g has the right correlation lengths and unit mean power.
  3. F = |g|²: exponential statistics, intensity correlation 1/e at the scintle time and ½ at the scintle bandwidth.
  4. Taken at each sub-int's mid time, and averaged over each channel's frequencies (with one channel: the band average). A channel's power integrates its whole width. So when the scintles are not much wider than a channel, the channel fades less than a single frequency does: 2 MHz scintles in 3.125 MHz channels give a variance of 0.79, not 1 (testSimulateFold 11). For B0329+54's ~7 MHz scintles this matters at the 2 % level, and more for narrower ones.

Switch: off by default. Without the option, or with [], the output is bit for bit what it was before scintillation was added, so every validation stays as it was. pipelineParams has scintOn (false), the J0437 values in scint, and scintSim to pass on ([] while off). The voltage chain ignores them.

Scintillation has its own random stream, so the noise draws are the same with and without it. Sub-ints should be short compared with the scintle time; there is a warning above a fifth of it. Long integrations are made by merging short sub-ints (next section). J0437 at 1.4 GHz is struct('bandwidth', 740e6, 'timescale', 2290).

The observer does not know F. Its fit has one amplitude per sub-int, which absorbs the fade. And it assumes equal channel gains, so structure across frequency costs it efficiency.

Adaptive sub-int length (observer side)

With scintillation, the SNR per sub-int changes by large factors from hour to hour. So no fixed sub-int length fits: it is too long when the pulsar is bright, and too short when it is faint. Two observer-side functions solve this. They work on real folds as well as simulated ones:

  1. mergeSubints(fold, info_fold, group) merges runs of consecutive sub-ints exactly: their sums and weights add. Only the noise covariance across an internal boundary is missing, as in foldProfile itself; with independent time bins it is exact. A merged sub-int's reference time is the time of its middle turn. For a fold made with a predictor, that time comes from the member sub-int holding the middle turn, because the f0 model does not hold there (23 Oct; folding page, test 6).
  2. adaptiveSubints(fold, info_fold, template, Bnoise) walks through short sub-ints and adds them one at a time. It stops when the merged profile's matched-filter statistic T0 at the predicted phase reaches the target: by default 7, the design rule. Then it starts a new group.
    By default it never merges across a gap between passes. What is left at the end of a pass becomes a last group below the target, for the good-TOA rule to judge. With 'AcrossGaps', true a group continues in the next pass. That is needed when one TOA takes longer than a pass: J0437 in the worst case needs 82–95 h of data, against passes of 8–15 h. It assumes a phase model that stays coherent over the gap. On real data that is the observer's predictor, with motion and the barycentric correction (stage 7a). In the simulator without motion the ephemeris is the truth, so it is exact there; with motion each sub-int's pulse is shifted by what the predictor misses (below).
    With 'TargetSNR', Inf and 'MaxSubints' it forms fixed groups instead, for comparison.

trueResiduals: how late the pulse really is validated

res = trueResiduals(t, psr, trajTrue, trajAssumed, ...)

Full interface and help text of trueResiduals →

In short

The observer folds with a predictor made from the trajectory it assumes. The pulses arrive where the true trajectory puts them. trueResiduals computes how late each pulse is against the prediction, and where it therefore sits in the observer's fold. That lateness is the signal navigation measures. Only the simulator calls this function, because it needs the true trajectory.

How it works

  1. The true arrival: a pulse that passes the barycentre at time S reaches the receiver at the true time t, with t + ΔT(t) = S. ΔT is delayModel on the true trajectory.
  2. The predicted arrival: the observer expects the same pulse at tA, with tA + ΔA(tA) = S. ΔA is delayModel on the assumed trajectory.
  3. The clock: the observer's clock reads c = t + ε(t) when the pulse arrives. ε is the clock error. The observer corrects the reading with its own estimate ε̂(c), the same one its predictor uses.
  4. The residual: r = c − ε̂(c) − tA. Only the part of the clock error that the observer has not estimated shifts the pulse. A positive residual means the pulse is late.
  5. In the fold: the pulse sits at phase f·r turns. f is the observed spin frequency of the observer's predictor.

Without the clock, the residual is the difference of the two delays, divided by one plus the Doppler factor. A site 100 km east of the assumed one gives B0329+54 a daily sine of 193.33 µs. That is 2.7·10⁻⁴ of a turn. A true receiver closer to the pulsar than assumed gives a negative residual, because the pulse arrives early.

The exact solution

r = x + ε(t) − ε̂(c), with x = t − tA = (ΔA − ΔT)/(1 + dΔA/dt) to first order. On the Earth the Doppler factor dΔA/dt is below 10⁻⁴, so the division changes 193 µs by at most 19 ns. The function solves x exactly, with Newton steps on ΔA(t − x) − ΔT(t) − x = 0. It works with small differences only, because absolute times of about 8·10⁸ s carry 1.2·10⁻⁷ s of rounding. The phase uses f = fspin·(1 + dΔA/dt), the frequency of the observer's predictor.

Half a turn is the limit

The fold cannot tell a pulse that is f·r turns late from one that is a whole turn later. A TOA is therefore unambiguous only while |f·r| stays below half a turn. For B0329+54 that allows a position error of ±107,000 km. For J0437 it allows only ±863 km. Beyond half a turn trueResiduals warns.

Motion and the clock in the scenario runs written

Checked in Claude's runs; the functions it calls are validated.

The fast simulator shifts each sub-integration's pulse by the phase offset from trueResiduals: runScenario with motion.on (run scripts). For a 100 km offset the residual changes by 1.4·10⁻⁸ s per second. Over a 120 s sub-integration that is 1.7 ns, far below a phase bin. One offset per sub-integration is therefore enough for a position error.

A clock rate is different. At the 2 ppm of a commercial SDR the pulse moves by 240 µs within a 120 s sub-integration. For B0329+54 that is 5 % of its 5 ms core. For J0437 a 300 s sub-integration moves it by 600 µs, four times its pulse width. The observer must therefore fold with its own estimate of the clock rate, as it does with the Doppler shift. The predictor takes that estimate (stage 7a). What is left for the simulator is the clock error the observer has not estimated.

A first check over two days reproduces the 100 km daily sine to 1.6·10⁻⁸ s. It also shows how much a free-running crystal wanders: about 76 µs in a day, and 13 ms in a month. The clock must therefore be part of the navigation fit.

With an SDR clock the observer tracks it day by day (motion.track "daily"). After each day it fits all its TOAs so far with navFit and the clock model, and folds the next day with the predicted clock. Its prediction one day ahead is then off by a few hundred µs: harmless for B0329+54's 6.6 ms pulse, too much for J0437's 0.14 ms (run scripts).

A TOA merges sub-ints, often over hours and sometimes over two passes a day apart. So it is a time-average. Its true parts and the clock estimate the observer used are averaged over its sub-ints. Its time tag is the time nearest its mean time whose sidereal phase is the circular mean of its sub-ints' (sideTag), and it carries its sub-ints' times (tSub) for the navigation fit's averaged rows (navigation).

Which ρ

The simulator's ρ, not the scenario page's

rhoDB is the simulator's snrDB: the peak of the Gaussian pulse over the receiver noise. So a simulated fold at −20 dB compares one to one with a voltage-chain run at −20 dB.

The reference scenario defines ρ differently. It uses a pulse width Weq = 2·W50 as a margin, which makes its ρ 2.7 dB lower for the same pulsar. For J0437 as a single Gaussian in the worst case that is −53.7 dB there, and −51.0 dB here.

runScenario converts the catalogue flux to the simulator's convention, with the true profile: ρ = S1400/(SEFD·Weq). Weq is the pulse area of the unit-peak profile, in turns. The SEFD is 3.2·10⁵ Jy nominal and 7.4·10⁵ Jy in the worst case. For J0437's measured profile that gives −51.2 dB and −54.8 dB.

Design choices

Draw the fold, not the voltages

Alternative: a faster voltage simulator.

Why not: even a perfect one makes about 72 GB per second on the default path (61 GB on the full band), so hours are out of reach. Everything after the fold depends on the voltages only through the fold's statistics, and those are validated end to end. So drawing the fold loses nothing the observer could see. It also keeps foldToTOA identical for simulated and real folds.

One channel first, channels second

In the weak regime, the channel path's optimally weighted fit is the same estimator as the full band's, and only about 2 % less efficient (roadmap item 3). A single channel kept the first version simple and exactly checkable. The channels came once that was validated.

Jasper's decision, 20 Oct 2026.

RFI from a measured profile, not from formulas

Alternative: compute each channel's level and excess noise from the source list (INR, bandwidth, type).

Why not: that needs a formula per RFI type (noise, carrier, BPSK, pulsed with excision), and each formula is an approximation that has to be validated anyway. A short voltage run measures exactly what the real excision leaves. A 1 s run makes the per-channel noise ratios about 3× more precise than 0.1 s: ±0.7 % instead of ±2 %.

The fold carries the observer's model; the noise follows the truth

With RFI, the stored weight2 and weightX are what the observer would compute, from the clean coefficients. The drawn noise uses the measured ones. Otherwise the simulation would hide exactly the error-bar mistakes it should show.

Not (yet) in the simulator

Block C was closed with these items parked: none of them is on the critical path of the navigation work.

  • Time structure: the time variation of rotating radars (step C3d), and refractive (slow) scintillation.
  • Motion within a sub-int: 'PhaseOffset' takes one value per sub-int, or a function of time that is evaluated once per sub-int. A position error moves the pulse by only nanoseconds within a sub-int. An unestimated clock rate can move it by hundreds of microseconds, so the observer folds with its clock estimate (above).
  • Excision of bright pulses (the chain clips pulse peaks above ~−5 dB) and RFI locked to the pulsar's period.
  • Memory: 128 channels × 2048 bins × 1200 sub-ints is 2.5 GB per array. For hours of data, channels with identical statistics will be merged into effective channels.
  • Sub-ints are independent (as foldProfile, which does not store the covariance across sub-int boundaries).
