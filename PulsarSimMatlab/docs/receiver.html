Stages 3–4 · Receiver: noise, RFI and IQ

The simulated receiver adds thermal noise and man-made interference at RF, then converts the real 4 GHz stream to complex baseband samples, as an analog I/Q receiver with an ADC would.

Full interface and help text of addNoiseAndRFI →

Purpose

Add white Gaussian receiver noise at a chosen SNR, plus any list of interference sources, to the dispersed sky signal. Print and store what the chosen SNR means in practice.

Physics: where noise enters

Noise and RFI are added at the receiver input (RF), after dispersion: they never travel through the interstellar medium. Dedispersion later applies the inverse chirp to them, exactly as with real data. A broadband impulse gets smeared into an inverse 6.3 ms sweep; a narrowband carrier is only shifted by its frequency's delay.

The SNR definition

snrDB = 10·log₁₀(Speak / N) = 10·log₁₀(A² / σn²)  ⇒  σn = A / √(10snrDB/10)

The pulsar's power spectral density at the pulse peak over the receiver-noise power spectral density, both in the band. In radio-astronomy terms Speak/SEFD. Inf means no noise.

Why this definition

It does not depend on bandwidth, time resolution or dispersion (each frequency still sees the full pulse height, only later), so it describes the sky and antenna, not a processing choice. A small-antenna design point translates directly into it: −54 dB for J0437 on the 0.61 m² array. All the "SNR per pulse / per sub-int" numbers follow from it through the radiometer equation.

Each source is a struct with the same fields, so a scenario is an array: rfi = [rfiSource(…), rfiSource(…)]. Power is the interference-to-noise ratio INRdB: the RFI power while it is on, relative to all receiver-noise power in the analysis band (0 dB = as much as the whole in-band noise).

Amplitudes: carrier-type sources a = √(2·INR·Pnb) (power a²/2); impulses σ = √INR·σn; with Pnb = σn²·B/(fs/2) the receiver-noise power in the band. Phases come from the absolute sample index; chips from mrg32k3a substreams per 65,536-chip chunk; impulse events are drawn once for the whole file, each burst from its own substream. So the result does not depend on the block size.

Radar edges: RiseTime (B5a, 8 Oct)

A real transmitter ramps its pulse up and down. The envelope rises and falls as a raised cosine of duration RiseTime, centred on the nominal edges, so PulseWidth stays the width between the 50 % points (the radar definition) and the energy is (PulseWidth − RiseTime/4) × the power while on.

Why it matters

With instant edges the radar's spectrum falls only as 1/f² away from the carrier: sidelobes over tens of MHz, in many channels, too weak per channel to be detected. With raised-cosine edges it falls as 1/f⁶ beyond ~1/(2·RiseTime) = 5 MHz. That difference decides how much radar energy survives excision (see locked radar). The default 0.1 µs is an assumed realistic value for a solid-state L-band surveillance radar (Jasper's choice; to be checked against ITU emission masks). RiseTime = 0 reproduces the pre-8-Oct output bit for bit.

Rotating antenna (B5c-1, 8 Oct) validated

Any source type can have a rotating antenna: ScanPeriod, BeamTime, BeamWidth, SidelobeDB. A surveillance radar's antenna turns (5–6 rpm: a scan of 10–12 s), so its main beam points at us once per scan and only its sidelobes the rest of the time. The source's power is multiplied by the antenna gain seen at time t:

g(t) = max(exp(−4 ln2·(dt/BeamWidth)²), 10SidelobeDB/10)

with dt the time from the nearest beam passage: a Gaussian main beam with −3 dB width BeamWidth in time (azimuth beamwidth / 360° × scan period; 1.4° at 10 s → 39 ms) on a flat sidelobe floor (default −30 dB). INRdB is then the main-beam peak. Default ScanPeriod = Inf: no rotation, bit for bit as before. Beamwidth and sidelobe levels of real L-band radars are assumptions.

Why model the rotation

Most of the time a surveillance radar reaches us only through its sidelobes: 25–35 dB weaker, so its pulses are close to the noise in their channels and per-pulse detection starts to miss them. Those missed pulses, not the bright main-beam passage, turn out to dominate what a rotating radar leaves behind (see excision: rotating radar).

Test (tests/testRfiGating.m, ~10 s): no rotation = the pre-B5a reference bit for bit; invalid rotations refused; radar pulses and a carrier through two beam passages (toy scan 1 ms, beam 100 µs, −25 dB floor): energy with/without rotation = g averaged over each pulse to 10⁻⁵.

Two deliberate choices in the scenario
  • Radar PRF 373 Hz, not 400 Hz. 400 Hz is a harmonic of the 100 Hz pulsar: the radar would fold coherently into the profile and bias the TOAs. That hazard is tested separately and on purpose (runLockedRadar.m); the default scenario uses an unrelated PRF.

Known simplification

Receiver noise does not pass the dispersion-stage taper Wf (real noise passes the same analog bandpass as the sky signal). Effect < 1 % in SNR; fixable by making the dispersion band slightly wider than the analysis band.

4 · applyIQmodulation validated

info = applyIQmodulation(inFile, outFile, f_in, fs, fLO, 'FilterOrder', 2048, 'Band', [fLow fHigh])

Full interface and help text of applyIQmodulation →

Purpose

Convert the real RF stream to complex baseband (I/Q) at rate fs: mix with a complex local oscillator, low-pass filter, decimate.

Physics

z(t) = LPF{2·x(t)·e−i2πfLOt},   so   x(t) = Re{z(t)·ei2πfLOt}
  • Frequency mapping fbb = fRF − fLO, no spectral inversion.
  • The mixer also makes a mirror image near −(fRF + fLO); it lies far outside the pass band and the low-pass removes it.
  • The factor 2 ('Gain','envelope', default) compensates the discarded image: a tone A·cos(2πft + φ) becomes A·eiφ·ei2π(f−fLO)t and mean|z|² = 2·mean(x²) in band.
Why the factor 2 does not matter physically (and why it is there anyway)

It multiplies signal and noise alike, so SNR, TOAs, error bars and detection are unchanged. The pipeline never assumes an absolute gain: the detector normalizes by the fitted baseline and the TOA fit uses the fitted model. It is the standard complex-envelope bookkeeping, and it matters only for absolute numbers (the noise baseline 1.233, the expected power model, the plot checks). 'unity' reproduces the old behaviour (amplitudes halved).

Why the LO is at 1.3 GHz, not in the middle of the band

With fLO = 1.4 GHz (band centre), 1.2–1.6 GHz maps to −200…+200 MHz, and its mirror image is the same interval. A sign error in the mapping (I and Q swapped, or a conjugated stream) would then be invisible: power, bandwidth and filter checks all pass. Worse, if both the IQ stage and dedispersion had the sign flipped, the simulation would be self-consistent and only real hardware would expose it. With fLO = 1.3 GHz the band sits at −100…+300 MHz and its mirror at −300…+100: a mirrored mapping would put half the band outside the dedispersion filter and fail visibly.

This needs fs = 800 MHz (decimation D = 5 from 4 GHz) so that +300 MHz fits inside the flat passband (cutoff 0.9·400 = 360 MHz). The original reference configuration was fLO = 1.4 GHz, fs = 500 MHz, D = 8.

Algorithm

  1. D = round(fin/fs); the achieved rate fin/D is info.actualFsOut.
  2. LO phase in cycles, mod(n0·r, 1) + (0:n−1)·r with r = fLO/fin, in double from the absolute start index (no drift).
  3. Low-pass: Kaiser-windowed sinc, odd length (2049 taps), unit DC gain, cutoff 0.9·fs/2, stopband 80 dB, transition ≈ 9.8 MHz. Checks: the stopband starts below the output Nyquist (no aliasing into the band); the band lies in the flat passband.
  4. Overlap-add FFT convolution (carry 2048 samples, automatic block size within MaxMemoryGB = 2).
  5. writeDecimated: remove the group delay G = 1024 input samples (256 ns) exactly, keep every D-th sample on the global grid → output sample k ↔ input sample k·D ↔ time k/fs. Output length ⌈N/D⌉.
Why odd length and exact delay removal

An odd-length linear-phase filter delays by an integer number of samples, which can be removed exactly. Without removal every TOA would be 256 ns late (this was a real bug once, #5).
