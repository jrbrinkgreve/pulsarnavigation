Stages 3–4 · Receiver: noise, RFI, IQ and clock

These two stages are the receiver. Stage 3 adds what the receiver contributes: its own thermal noise, and the man-made interference the antenna picks up. Stage 4 turns the real 4 GHz stream into complex samples at 800 MHz, as an analog I/Q receiver followed by an ADC would. The receiver's clock times every one of those samples.

Run by runStage.receiver · rerun this alone to change the SNR, the RFI or the noise seed.

In short

It adds white Gaussian receiver noise at a chosen SNR to the dispersed sky signal, plus any list of interference sources. It also prints what that SNR means in practice: how bright one pulse is, and how precise a TOA can be.

The physics

Where noise and RFI enter

Receiver noise and interference enter at the receiver, after the pulsar's signal has crossed the interstellar medium. So they are never dispersed. When the processing later removes dispersion, it applies the inverse chirp to them as well, exactly as it would with real data.

The SNR definition

\[ \texttt{snrDB} = 10\log_{10}\frac{S_\mathrm{peak}}{N} = 10\log_{10}\frac{A^2}{\sigma_n^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sigma_n = \frac{A}{\sqrt{\rho}}, \qquad \rho = 10^{\,\texttt{snrDB}/10} \]
\(S_\mathrm{peak}\)
power spectral density of the pulsar at the pulse peak [V²/Hz]
\(N\)

\(A\)
pulsar amplitude: its voltage standard deviation at the pulse peak [V] (A = 1)
\(\sigma_n\)
standard deviation of the receiver noise per RF sample [V]. Pulsar and noise are both generated white at the same sample rate (4 GHz) and dispersion does not change the in-band density, so the PSD ratio is \(A^2/\sigma_n^2\)
\(\rho\)
the same ratio on a linear scale, no unit

snrDB compares two power spectral densities in the band: the pulsar's at the peak of the pulse, and the receiver noise's. In radio-astronomy terms it is Speak/SEFD. In the simulation both are white, so the ratio is simply A²/σn², and the code sets the noise level from it. Inf means no noise.

How it works

  1. Noise level. σn = A/√ρ from snrDB. From it, the receiver-noise power in the band, Pband = σn²·B/(fs/2). All RFI strengths are relative to that.
  2. Set up each RFI source, once. Its amplitude from its INR, its own random stream, and what it needs for the whole file: the band-pass filter of a 'noise' source, the list of burst times of an 'impulse' source.
  3. Stream the file. For each block of 4 million samples: read the sky signal, add the noise, add every RFI source, write.

Every random number and every phase is tied to the absolute sample index, so the block size cannot change the output.

Exact formulas
  • Carrier-type sources (cw, bpsk, pulsed, noise): amplitude a = √(2·INR·Pband), so the power while on is a²/2 = INR·Pband.
  • Impulses: white bursts with σ = √INR·σn, so their in-band share has the same ratio.
  • Phases: cycles = mod(n·f/fs, 1) + …, in double from the absolute index n.
  • Random parts: noise from mt19937ar (seed noiseSeed); each RFI source from its own mrg32k3a stream (seed + 1000·source index), with substreams per 65,536-chip chunk (bpsk), per burst (impulse) or per 65,536-sample chunk (noise). Impulse times are drawn once for the whole file (Poisson).

Radar edges: RiseTime

A real transmitter ramps its pulse up and down. Here the envelope rises and falls as a raised cosine lasting RiseTime, centred on the nominal edges. So PulseWidth is still the width between the 50 % points (the radar definition), and the energy is (PulseWidth − RiseTime/4) × the power while on.

Why it matters

With instant edges the radar's spectrum falls only as 1/f² away from the carrier: weak sidelobes over tens of MHz, in many channels, too weak per channel to be detected. With raised-cosine edges it falls as 1/f⁶ beyond ~1/(2·RiseTime) = 5 MHz (figure below, a). That difference decides how much radar energy survives excision (see locked radar).

The default of 0.1 µs is an assumed realistic value for a solid-state L-band surveillance radar. It is still to be checked against ITU emission masks. RiseTime = 0 gives instant edges, and reproduces the output from before edges were modelled, bit for bit.

Rotating antenna validated

Any source can have a rotating antenna (ScanPeriod, BeamTime, BeamWidth, SidelobeDB). A surveillance radar's antenna turns at 5–6 rpm, a scan of 10–12 s. Its main beam points at us once per scan; the rest of the time only its sidelobes do. The source's power is multiplied by the antenna gain towards us at time t:

\[ g(t) = \max\!\left( \exp\!\left(-4\ln 2\,\left(\frac{\delta t}{\texttt{BeamWidth}}\right)^{2}\right),\; 10^{\,\texttt{SidelobeDB}/10} \right) \]
\(g(t)\)
antenna power gain towards us at time \(t\), relative to the main-beam peak (1 at the peak)
\(\delta t\)
the time since (or until) the nearest beam passage [s] (passages at BeamTime + whole ScanPeriods)
\(\texttt{BeamWidth}\)
−3 dB width of the main beam, in time [s]
\(\texttt{SidelobeDB}\)
sidelobe floor relative to the main-beam peak [dB] (default −30)
  • The main beam is a Gaussian: \(g = \tfrac12\) at \(\delta t = \pm\)BeamWidth/2. Its width in time is azimuth beamwidth / 360° × scan period, so 1.4° at 10 s gives 39 ms.
  • Outside the main beam: a flat sidelobe floor (default −30 dB). INRdB is the main-beam peak.
  • Default ScanPeriod = Inf: no rotation, bit for bit as before. The beamwidth and sidelobe levels of real L-band radars are assumptions.
Why model the rotation

Most of the time a surveillance radar reaches us only through its sidelobes, 25–35 dB weaker. Its pulses are then close to the noise in their channels, and per-pulse detection starts to miss them. Those missed pulses, not the bright main-beam passage, turn out to dominate what a rotating radar leaves behind (see excision: rotating radar).

Test (tests/testRfiGating.m, ~10 s). The rotation scales each pulse's energy exactly as the antenna gain says:

  • without rotation, the output equals the older reference (from before radar edges were added) bit for bit;
  • invalid rotation settings are refused;
  • radar pulses and a carrier pass through two beam passages (toy scan 1 ms, beam 100 µs, −25 dB floor). The energy with rotation, divided by the energy without, equals g averaged over each pulse, to 10⁻⁵.

Band-limited Gaussian noise: 'noise' validated

An LTE downlink is OFDM: hundreds of subcarriers carrying random data. By the central limit theorem its voltage is Gaussian, so it looks like extra receiver noise with a raised level in its band. The 'noise' type makes exactly that: white Gaussian noise through a Kaiser band-pass centred on Freq (−6 dB at Freq ± Bandwidth/2, transition 5 % of the bandwidth, 60 dB stopband).

How it stays exact and block-size independent

The white noise comes from counter-based random substreams indexed by absolute sample, also before the first sample, and the filter overlaps the block edges, so the result does not depend on the block size. Its power is set from the filter's own Σh², so the INR is exact.

The filtering runs at the full RF rate, so the filters are long: a 2 MHz source needs ~1.4·10⁵ taps, a 200 kHz carrier ~1.4·10⁶. Each source's filter spectrum is therefore computed once and kept for all full blocks. This costs 67–134 MB of memory per source.

What it shows

No power or kurtosis test can find Gaussian RFI. Its only signature is the higher level in its channels, which the optimal TOA fit already handles (weights 1/level²). In runRFITest (20 MHz at 1472 MHz) it costs 2–3 % SNR and is not flagged. But the two channels that the band only partly covers break the flat-spectrum assumption (see excision limits).

Test (tests/testRfiNoise.m, ~5 s). The source has the right power, the right band and Gaussian statistics, and it changes nothing else:

  • the other RFI types still give the older reference (from before radar edges were added) bit for bit;
  • an odd block size gives the same output as one single block, bit for bit;
  • the power is 0.9945 of INR × the in-band noise (σ 0.0045);
  • the spectrum is flat in band, and ≤ 2·10⁻⁷ out of band;
  • the kurtosis is 3.004 (σ 0.016), as for Gaussian noise (3).

Design choices

Why this stage is built this way. The reasons for individual RFI features (edges, rotation, the 'noise' type, the two scenario choices) are with each feature above.

Noise and RFI are added at RF, after dispersion

Alternatives: add them to the sky signal before dispersion, or later, to the baseband samples after stage 4.

Why not: before dispersion, an impulse made at the receiver would be dispersed like a pulsar, which never happens in reality. After stage 4, the I/Q filter would never see the noise and interference it has to handle in a real receiver. Adding them here follows the physical order (first figure on this page).

The SNR is a ratio of spectral densities at the pulse peak

Alternatives: an SNR per sample, per time bin, or after folding.

Why not: those depend on choices made later: the bandwidth, the time resolution, how long you fold. The ratio of spectral densities does not, and neither does dispersion (each frequency still sees the full pulse height, only later). So it describes the sky and antenna, not a processing choice. A small-antenna design point translates straight into it: −54 dB for J0437 on the 0.61 m² array. Every "SNR per pulse / per sub-int" follows from it through the radiometer equation (SNR figure above).

RFI strength relative to all the noise in the band

Alternative: relative to the noise per Hz, like the SNR.

Why not: a carrier puts all its power at one frequency; per Hz it would be infinitely strong, yet it touches only one channel. Total power is the one measure that means the same for a carrier, a 20 MHz LTE block and a 200 ns burst. It is also what a link budget gives, which is how the realistic scenario's levels were estimated.

A few simple RFI types, not recorded real RFI

Alternative: add a recording of a real radio environment.

Why not: a recording has no ground truth: you cannot know what part of a result was caused by which source. Each type here isolates one signature in time and frequency (RFI figure above). That is also how the processing has to deal with them: short, strong ones (pulsed, impulse) are found and blanked by excision; continuous ones (cw, bpsk, noise) cannot be blanked without losing the channel, and the TOA fit gives their channels less weight instead.

Every random number from its own stream, tied to the absolute sample

The noise has its own seed (noiseSeed = seed + 1 = 43), separate from the pulsar's. So a Monte Carlo can change the noise and keep the pulsar, and this stage can be rerun alone. Each RFI source has its own stream too, with substreams per chunk or burst, and phases come from the absolute sample index. So a change of block size, for example to save memory, can never change the data (see conventions §6).

Two RFI lists; the illustrative one is the default

The illustrative list has a few sources of different kinds at round levels. That makes it good for testing each kind on its own. The realistic list estimates what the reference hardware would actually see. The illustrative list stayed the default, so that main.m and all tests stayed unchanged.

Validation and limits

  • Noise: the −5 dB reference run (noise seed 43) and a Monte Carlo over 10 noise seeds: noise baseline 1.232 measured vs 1.233 predicted; error bars honest (validation record).
  • All five RFI types were observed and checked in the RFI test of 5 Oct 2026. The radar edges are checked by tests/testRadarEdges.m, and rotation and the 'noise' type by their tests above.
  • testAddNoiseAndRFI checks the noise level and the RFI powers against their formulas (unit tests). A peak-level pulsar's in-band power over the noise's equals ρ, within 1.3σ. The carrier matches its formula to 1.5·10⁻⁷ of its amplitude. The output is the same for any block size, bit for bit.
Known simplifications
  • Receiver noise does not pass the dispersion-stage taper W(f); real noise passes the same analog band-pass as the sky signal. On the full band this costs +0.4 % in σTOA. On the default channel path it costs +2.5 %, because the optimal fit weights the edge channels as if they held the full pulse (limitations, roadmap item 3). It is fixable by making the dispersion band slightly wider than the analysis band, by shaping the noise with the same taper, or by taking the channel gains from the observer's bandpass.
  • No ADC: no quantization and no clipping, so a very strong source cannot saturate the receiver (see limitations).

4 · applyIQmodulation validated

info = applyIQmodulation(inFile, outFile, f_in, fs, fLO, 'FilterOrder', 2048, 'Band', [fLow fHigh])

Full interface and help text of applyIQmodulation →

In short

It turns the real 4 GHz stream into complex (I/Q) samples at 800 MHz. It mixes with a 1.3 GHz local oscillator (LO), low-pass filters, and keeps every 5th sample. That is what an analog I/Q receiver followed by an ADC does.

The physics

\[ z(t) = \mathrm{LPF}\!\left\{ 2\,x(t)\, e^{-i 2\pi f_\mathrm{LO}\, t} \right\} \quad\text{so}\quad x(t) = \mathrm{Re}\!\left\{ z(t)\, e^{\,i 2\pi f_\mathrm{LO}\, t} \right\} \]
\(x(t)\)
the real RF voltage at 4 GHz sampling (f_in): pulsar + noise + RFI [V]
\(z(t)\)
the complex envelope \(I + iQ\), written at 800 MHz (fs) [V]
\(f_\mathrm{LO}\)
local-oscillator frequency [Hz] (fLO = 1.3 GHz)
\(\mathrm{LPF}\{\cdot\}\)
low-pass filter, one-sided cutoff 360 MHz (Cutoff, default 0.9·fs/2)
\(2\)
the envelope gain ('Gain','envelope'), explained below
  • Frequency mapping: RF frequency f lands at f − fLO. The band 1.2–1.6 GHz becomes −100…+300 MHz. There is no spectral inversion: higher RF stays higher.
  • Mirror image: the mixer also makes a copy of the band near −(f + fLO), far outside the pass band. The low-pass removes it.
  • The factor 2 ('Gain','envelope', default) makes up for the discarded mirror: a tone \(a\cos(2\pi f t + \varphi)\) with amplitude \(a\) and phase \(\varphi\) becomes \(a\,e^{i\varphi}\,e^{i 2\pi (f - f_\mathrm{LO}) t}\), and \(\overline{|z|^2} = 2\,\overline{x^2}\) in band (bar: time average).

How it works

  1. Mix. Multiply each block by the LO, e−i2πfLOt. The LO phase is computed in double from the absolute sample index, so it never drifts.
  2. Low-pass. Filter with a 2049-tap low-pass (cut-off 360 MHz), by FFT convolution with overlap-add, like stage 2.
  3. Keep every 5th sample (4 GHz / 5 = 800 MHz), after removing the filter's delay of exactly 1024 input samples (256 ns). Output sample k is then exactly input sample 5k, at time k/fs.
  4. Write interleaved I, Q as float32 (the cf32 format). Output length ⌈N/5⌉.
Exact formulas and numbers
  • Decimation D = round(fin/fs); the achieved rate fin/D is info.actualFsOut.
  • LO phase in cycles: mod(n0·r, 1) + (0:n−1)·r, r = fLO/fin, from the block's absolute start index n0.
  • Low-pass: Kaiser-windowed sinc, odd length M = 2049, unit DC gain, cut-off 0.9·fs/2 = 360 MHz, stopband 80 dB, transition ≈ (80 − 8)/(2.285·(M − 1))·fin/2π = 9.8 MHz. So the gain is flat to ±355 MHz and −80 dB beyond ±365 MHz.
  • Checks (warnings): the stopband must start below the output Nyquist frequency (no aliasing into the output), and the band must lie in the flat passband.
  • Overlap-add: carry 2048 samples, automatic block size within MaxMemoryGB = 2.
  • writeDecimated: removes the group delay G = (M − 1)/2 = 1024 input samples and keeps every D-th sample on the global grid.

Design choices

An analog I/Q receiver: mix, low-pass, keep every 5th sample

Alternative: an ideal conversion: one FFT of the whole file, keep the band, transform back at the lower rate.

Why not: that needs the whole file in memory (4·10⁹ samples per second of data), and it is not what the hardware does. Mixing, filtering and decimating streams with bounded memory, like every other stage. It also makes the receiver's timing conventions explicit: the LO phase, the filter delay and the sample grid. Those must be right for TOAs at the nanosecond level; an unremoved filter delay of 256 ns was once a real bug (#5).

The LO at 1.3 GHz, not in the middle of the band

With fLO = 1.4 GHz (the band centre), 1.2–1.6 GHz maps to −200…+200 MHz, and its mirror image is the same interval. A sign error in the mapping (I and Q swapped, or a conjugated stream) would then be invisible: power, bandwidth and filter checks all pass. Worse, if both this stage and dedispersion had the sign flipped, the simulation would be self-consistent, and only real hardware would expose it.

With fLO = 1.3 GHz the band sits at −100…+300 MHz and its mirror at −300…+100 MHz. A mirrored mapping would put half the band outside the dedispersion filter and fail visibly.

Jasper's choice, 1 Oct 2026.

800 MHz output (keep every 5th sample)

The off-centre band reaches +300 MHz, which must lie in the flat part of the low-pass, below the output Nyquist frequency fs/2. 800 MHz gives a Nyquist of 400 MHz and a cut-off of 0.9 × 400 = 360 MHz. The original configuration (fLO = 1.4 GHz, 500 MHz, D = 8) would not fit the off-centre band.

The low-pass: cut-off 360 MHz, 80 dB stopband, 2049 taps

The receiver noise is white over all frequencies (stage 3 does not band-limit it), so this filter is what limits it. After keeping every 5th sample, each in-band frequency also receives whatever sat 800 and 1600 MHz away, on both sides: four copies (low-pass figure above). Without the filter the noise would be five times higher. At −80 dB each copy adds about 10⁻⁸ of the in-band noise or less; all four together stay under 10⁻⁷: negligible. 2049 taps make the transition narrow (9.8 MHz), so the band sits well inside the flat part; with FFT convolution the length costs little.

Odd filter length; the delay removed exactly

An odd-length linear-phase filter delays everything by a whole number of samples, here 1024 at 4 GHz. A whole number can be removed exactly, and the kept samples stay on the global time grid. Without the removal every TOA would be 256 ns late. This was a real bug once (#5).

The factor 2 ('envelope' gain)

It multiplies signal and noise alike, so SNR, TOAs, error bars and detection do not change. The pipeline never assumes an absolute gain: the detector normalizes by the fitted baseline, and the TOA fit uses the fitted model. It is the standard complex-envelope bookkeeping, and it matters only for absolute numbers (the noise baseline 1.233, the expected power model, the plot checks). 'unity' reproduces the old behaviour (amplitudes halved).

4a · The receiver's clock: makeClock validated

clk = makeClock([t1 t2], ...)

Full interface and help text of makeClock → · the clock stamps the IQ samples of stage 4 · the observer estimates it in the predictor (stage 7a) and in navigation · block D

In short

Every time stamp comes from the receiver's oscillator, so the oscillator's error goes straight into every TOA. A commercial SDR's oscillator is specified to ±2 ppm. Left uncorrected, that is up to about 170 ms a day.

makeClock draws one realization of the clock's error over time, for the simulator's truth. The observer never sees it. Its navigation filter estimates the clock's offset and rate instead (stage 10).

In the voltage chain every time stamp is exact, and a run lasts seconds. The clock matters over hours and days, so the fast simulator applies it, through trueResiduals.

Offset, rate and wander of the clockShift every TOA.A temperature-compensated crystal (TCXO) drifts about 3.6 µs per hour, an oven-controlled one (OCXO) 4–36 ns. B0329+54's 734 µs TOAs tolerate a TCXO; J0437's 13 µs TOAs need an OCXO or better. The clock offset and rate become part of the navigation state.

How it works

  1. The fixed part: an offset at the start, a rate and an ageing drift. A commercial SDR's reference oscillator is specified to ±2 ppm. Its rate can therefore be up to 2·10⁻⁶, which adds 7.2 ms per hour.
  2. The noise: the oscillator's frequency also wanders at random. Its spectrum has three parts, each set by one coefficient: white frequency noise (h₀), flicker (h₋₁) and a random walk (h₋₂).
  3. The grid: the noise is drawn on a 60 s grid over the whole span and is linear in between.

An oscillator's stability is usually given as its Allan deviation σy(τ). It is the typical change of the average frequency from one interval of length τ to the next. White noise makes it fall with τ, flicker keeps it flat, and a random walk makes it grow.

The crystal preset's time error grows by τ·σy(τ): about 0.66 µs in an hour and 76 µs in a day, by its own formula. That is steadier than the TCXO estimate in the table above, 3.6 µs per hour. The noise of a commercial SDR's oscillator is not published. It is best measured on the device itself, against a better reference.

The formulas, and how each part is drawn

σy²(τ) = h₀/(2τ) + 2 ln2·h₋₁ + (2π)²/6·h₋₂·τ (the paper's eqs. 6–8).

  • White: the time error takes independent steps of variance h₀Δ/2 per grid step Δ. This is exact.
  • Random walk: the frequency and the time error form a two-state process with covariance q₂·[Δ³/3, Δ²/2; Δ²/2, Δ] per step, q₂ = 2π²h₋₂. This is exact, and it is the model the Kalman filter uses.
  • Flicker: no finite-state model is exact. The step-averaged frequency is synthesized by FFT with the spectrum h₋₁/f, over twice the span, so that the FFT never joins the two ends. It is then integrated.

Between grid nodes the left-out noise is a white-noise bridge of √(h₀Δ/8): 0.12 ns for the crystal at 60 s.

How the runs use the clock

Both scenario scripts give the receiver an SDR clock: the crystal preset's noise, and a rate drawn uniformly within ±2 ppm.

  • runScenario with motion.clock "sdr". The observer knows the clock from a port calibration to 1 ms in offset and 10⁻⁹ in rate. By default it then tracks the clock day by day: after each day it fits all its TOAs with navFit and the clock model, and folds the next day with the predicted clock. In the 30-day run (true rate −1.17·10⁻⁶) it keeps 300 of 302 TOAs, and the position is the same as with a perfect clock (navigation).
  • runShip. Each seed draws its own clock. The observer's filter carries the clock's offset and rate. With a calibrated start the port calibration's errors, 1 ms and 10⁻⁹, are drawn and given to the filter as 'ClockPrior' (navigation).

The clock the observer has not estimated then stays at a few hundred µs per day. That is harmless for B0329+54's 6.6 ms pulse, but too much for J0437's 0.14 ms.

Design choices

Flicker included, by FFT

Alternative: only white and random-walk noise, the two that a two-state Kalman model describes exactly.

Why not: for the paper's crystal, flicker is the largest of the three noises from 1 s to 100 s. After 200 s it is 42 % of the time error's variance. Leaving it out would make the clock look better than it is.
