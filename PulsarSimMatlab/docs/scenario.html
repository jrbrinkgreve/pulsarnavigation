Reference scenario

The real hardware and pulsars the pipeline is designed for. This is where the numbers "−54 dB" and "20 hours per TOA" come from, and why the project targets weak signals.

Drafted 6 October 2026 from Jasper's hardware; questions answered the same day. Pulsar data from the ATNF catalogue v2.6.5.

In short

The pipeline is designed for a small array (0.61 m², 1.2–1.6 GHz) and the best pulsar it can time, J0437−4715. At the worst-case design point the pulse peak is about 54 dB below the receiver noise, and one TOA takes about 20 hours of folding. That is why the project targets weak signals, why TOAs must be folded across several passes, and why a fast simulator is planned.

Receiver

  • 3×3 array, 0.61 m², dual polarization on all 9 elements, band 1.2–1.6 GHz, ground station, Tsys 60–80 K.
  • Site-independent: "should work anywhere". The station position is an input parameter; no pulsar is hard-coded; sources are chosen by visibility.
Design pointAeffTsysSEFD = 2kTsys/Aeff

For comparison: Parkes ~30 Jy, a 25 m dish ~1000 Jy. The array beam is wide (~15°), so Tsys also includes the beam-averaged sky temperature: ~3–5 K off the Galactic plane, ~5–20 K for plane sources such as Vela.

Design choice: design for the worst case

Alternative: design for the nominal area and system temperature.

Why not: the effective area is not known precisely, and the time per TOA scales as SEFD². So the worst case takes 5.3× longer. The pipeline must work there; nominal performance is the bonus. That is why the project targets the weak-signal regime, and why, for example, improvements to the estimator at high SNR have low priority. (Jasper's decision, 6 Oct 2026.)

Time per TOA

How the table below is computed:

  1. Take each pulsar's period P, pulse width W50 and mean flux Smean at 1.4 GHz from the catalogue.
  2. The radiometer equation gives the SNR of a folded profile after observing for a time t (dual polarization, 400 MHz).
  3. Solve for the t that gives SNR 7, the design rule from the threshold sweep.
  4. The TOA error at SNR 7 follows from the pulse width (physics).
\[ \mathrm{SNR} = \frac{S_\mathrm{mean}}{\mathrm{SEFD}}\, \sqrt{n_\mathrm{pol}\, B\, t}\; \sqrt{\frac{P - W_\mathrm{eq}}{W_\mathrm{eq}}}, \qquad \rho = \frac{S_\mathrm{peak}}{\mathrm{SEFD}}, \qquad \sigma_\mathrm{TOA} = \frac{\sqrt{2}\; W_{50}/2.355}{7} \]
\(\mathrm{SNR}\)
SNR of the folded profile after observing for a time \(t\)
\(S_\mathrm{mean}\)
the pulsar's mean flux density at 1.4 GHz, from the catalogue [Jy]
\(\mathrm{SEFD}\)
system-equivalent flux density of the receiving system [Jy]
\(n_\mathrm{pol}\)
number of polarizations (2)
\(B\)
bandwidth [Hz] (400 MHz)
\(t\)
observing time [s]
\(P\)
pulse period [s] (called \(T\) on the other pages)
\(W_{50}\)
pulse width at 50 % of the peak [s], from the catalogue
\(W_\mathrm{eq}\)
equivalent pulse width, \(2\,W_{50}\) (margin for wings and components) [s]
\(S_\mathrm{peak}\)
peak flux density, \(S_\mathrm{mean}\,P/W_\mathrm{eq}\) [Jy]
\(\sigma_\mathrm{TOA}\)
TOA error at SNR 7 [s]; \(W_{50}/2.355\) is the Gaussian standard deviation

Catalogue fluxes are averages; low-DM pulsars scintillate by a factor ~2 or more either way.

Two conventions for ρ

The ρ in this table uses Speak = Smean·P/Weq, with the margin Weq = 2·W50.

The simulator's snrDB is the peak of a Gaussian of FWHM W50: Speak = Smean·P/(1.0645·W50). That is 2.7 dB higher for the same pulsar. In snrDB terms J0437's worst case is about −51 dB (−47 dB nominal). So the design target of −54 dB carries a margin of about 3 dB.

Do not combine the two. The simulator's own pulsar (10 ms, 5 %, one polarization) at snrDB −54 needs about 62 h per TOA at SNR 7, not the 20 h of the J0437 row.

Who can see what

A pulsar can only be observed while it is well above the horizon. The scenario counts a pulsar as visible when it rises at least 20° above the horizon, and the hours per day it spends there turn integration hours into calendar days. The station position is an input, so this changes from site to site.

Conclusions

  • The figure of merit is σTOA·√t, in µs·√h; smaller is better. In the worst case it is 55 for J0437, 80 for Vela, 1160 for B0329+54, and 5400–11,500 for the other normal pulsars. The faint millisecond pulsars score well on paper (B1937+21 300, J1713+0747 3700, J2145−0750 4300), but a single TOA takes them months to years.
  • Vela is as good as J0437. It is bright, with a TOA in about 20 min. But it has a wider pulse, DM 67.8, glitches and timing noise.
  • J0437 is the only realistic millisecond pulsar. The time per TOA scales as S⁻², so a pulsar 15× fainter takes 225× longer. J0437 resembles the simulated pulsar (P 5.8 ms against 10 ms, DM 2.6 against 5), at ρ ≈ −54 dB in the worst case. It needs about 20 h per TOA, for σTOA ≈ 12 µs: 3.6 km along the line of sight.
  • Both good sources are southern; for northern sites B0329+54 is the only source with TOAs within a pass. From Europe and most of North America there is no feasible MSP.
  • Normal pulsars are needed for geometry (≥ 4 well-separated directions for 3-D position + clock) and visibility, at the cost of wide pulses (σTOA 0.3–0.7 ms at SNR 7) and timing noise.

Consequences for the pipeline

What the scenario forced
  1. Weak signal everywhere: ρ ≈ −54 dB, per-pulse SNR ≪ 1. Self-noise is negligible; FFTFIT is optimal; any phase-dependent bias must be ≪ 4·10⁻⁶ of the baseline.
  2. A 20 h TOA spans several passes → fold across passes with gaps → barycentric phase prediction (D) is a hard requirement.
  3. Hours of data cannot be simulated at voltage level → the fast simulator (C).
  4. DM target 100 (Vela 67.8, B1937+21 71.0, B0740−28 73.8, plus margin) → the channelized front end on the processing side.
  5. Dual polarization → √2 in SNR (2× in time); add the second polarization.
  6. Scintillation dominates the per-pass SNR of low-DM pulsars → weight passes by measured SNR; adaptive sub-int length.
  7. RFI is far stronger than the pulsar → excision must blank, before dedispersion (block B).

The ship and the Netherlands

The array will go on a ship. So the site moves, and a fixed flat installation is not an option. The simulations therefore assume a steered array, with constant gain over a pass. (Jasper's information, 20 Oct 2026.)

For now the location is the Netherlands, at 52° N. From there J0437 (maximum elevation −9°) and Vela (−7°) never rise. B0329+54 never sets, and is above 20° for 19.6 h a day. So the scenario runs use B0329+54 from 52° N, and J0437 from 30° S, where it is up 12.3 h a day: a ship that sailed south.

B0329+54 at 1.4 GHz:

  • P 714.5 ms, DM 26.8 (which is why the default DM is 25), W50 6.6 ms, S1400 203 mJy;
  • scintles of about 7 MHz × 13 min (ATA 2025, Wang et al. 2005);
  • five profile components, and an abnormal mode about 15 % of the time;
  • ρ in the simulator's convention: −41.9 dB nominal, −45.5 dB worst case.

Why NBin 2048. At NBin 1024 the narrowest component was only 5.6 bins wide. B0329+54 then gave 9.6 (nominal) and 1.8 (worst case) TOAs per day, at 2.0 and 11.0 h per TOA, with rms/pred 1.18 and 1.17. Most of that excess was bin resolution.

A rare low-SNR outlier remains. At NBin 2048, one TOA in each of two runs lands about one core width off: ±0.0064 turns (4.5 ms), |z| 6.4–6.9. That is about 0.3 % of ~700 TOAs, as in the pull test at SNR 7 (2 of 600). These TOAs have SNR 7.6–7.8 and χ² 1.00–1.02, so χ² cannot flag them.

The cause is the shape of the template. Its autocorrelation has no second peak there: it is 0.75, on a smooth shoulder, and the first side peak is at ±0.025 turns. A narrow core on a broad component lets noise move the maximum onto the shoulder now and then. The error bar comes from the curvature at the peak, so it does not cover this: it is the estimator's threshold effect. At SNR 10 there were none (0 of 600).

For navigation: use a robust (outlier-tolerant) position fit, or a target SNR of about 10 for B0329+54. J0437's 0.88 was statistical scatter: a second run gave 0.94, and adaptive and fixed groups on the same data both gave about 1.1. See limits.

J0437's real profile has wings: about 5× more data per TOA

The J0437 rows above use a single Gaussian with the catalogue W50 of 0.141 ms. But J0437's W10 is 1.02 ms, four times a Gaussian's. The measured Parkes profile at 1369 MHz (Dai et al. 2015, EPN database, CC BY 4.0) gives a Weq 2.4× the Gaussian's. So ρ is 3.75 dB lower: −51.2 dB nominal, −54.8 dB worst case. The wings add flux but little timing. And the core is sharper than a Gaussian, so the resolution rule needs NBin 1024.

The profile was fitted with 8 Gaussian components. Their W50, W10, Weq, spectral width and timing merit are within 1 % of the measured profile's. Simulated for 60 days, clean, at NBin 1024 (Claude's scratch run, 21 Oct 2026):

With realistic RFI: the scenario variants

In short

The realistic L-band RFI (LTE, GNSS, Inmarsat, radars, impulses; the measured 1 s profile after excision) costs about 20–25 % of the TOAs per day, and about 10–15 % in σ·√h.

Without the per-channel noise calibration, the error bars are only about 3 % too small. But χ² is 1.07, and the pChi2 test flags 89–100 % of the TOAs. With the calibration, χ² is 1.00 and about 5 % are flagged. That makes the calibration the setting for real data.

With its measured profile, J0437 gives 22 TOAs in 30 days at nominal flux, and only 2–4 in the worst case. The worst-case rows are therefore statistics of a handful of TOAs. The RFI still costs about 22 % of the TOAs, and the calibration still brings χ² to 1.00. The gain from scintillation is gone: each TOA now needs about 20 h of data, which averages over roughly 30 scintles of 40 min, so there are no bright spells left to ride.

  • RFI costs about 21 % of the TOAs for B0329+54, and 20–24 % for J0437. That is the SNR loss of the measured profile (×0.895, expSimRFI), squared.
  • Calibration brings χ² from 1.07 to 1.00, and the pChi2 flags from 89–100 % down to about 5 %. It costs no TOAs. The voltage chain shows the same: χ² 1.24 → 0.99. Without it, a TOA-quality test on real data would reject almost everything.
  • Scintillation. B0329+54's scintles (~7 MHz) average out over the 128 channels: no effect. J0437's (~740 MHz) make the whole band fade and brighten over about 40 min. Adaptive groups ride the bright spells, with 0.25–7.9 h of data per TOA. So σ·√h improves from 22.8 to 17.3 in the nominal case: information grows as the flux squared, and ⟨S²⟩ > ⟨S⟩². Fixed groups keep part of that gain (18.8), but some of their TOAs fall to SNR ~5. The worst case, with 16–17 TOAs, is inconclusive.
  • Outliers (B0329+54). With adaptive groups (SNR ≥ 7), 6 of ~1200 TOAs are 5–9σ off, at SNR 7.3–7.9, by one core width (0.005–0.009 turns). This is the threshold effect described above; all but one are positive. Fixed groups reach down to SNR ~5.5, and add a jump to the template's side peak: z −19.3, at −0.0259 turns (the autocorrelation's side peak is at ±0.0247). For navigation: use adaptive groups, a robust fit, or a higher target SNR for pulsars with several components.

100 days: the final budgets

In short

Over 100 days the budgets of the 30-day runs hold. B0329+54 gives about 10 good TOAs a day at nominal flux, and 7.7 with realistic RFI and scintillation. J0437 gives one about every 1.3 days. The error bars are honest in the core of the distribution. But 1.9 % of B0329+54's TOAs lie beyond 3σ, seven times more than a Gaussian would give, so the navigation fit must expect such a tail.
