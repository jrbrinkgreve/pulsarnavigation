Reference scenario

The real hardware and pulsars the pipeline is designed for. This is where the signal levels (−38 dB for B0329+54, −44 dB for J0437−4715) and the time per TOA come from, and why the project targets weak signals.

The pipeline is designed for a small array (about 3 m², 1.2–1.6 GHz, of which about 150 MHz is clean of RFI) and the pulsars it can time. At the array's design point the pulse peak of B0329+54 is 38.0 dB below the receiver noise, and that of J0437−4715 44.2 dB. Followed through its pass, B0329+54 needs 0.23 hours of data per TOA and gives 87 TOAs a day; J0437 needs about 1.7 hours and gives 7 a day (R7, R9). A fixed panel sees B0329+54 drift through its beam once a day, with an SNR of about 11.5 per pass (R7.4). That is why the project targets weak signals, why the weaker pulsars must be folded across several passes, and why the fast simulator was built. The array stands still at a site that is not known beforehand, so the method must work anywhere on Earth. The scenario runs time B0329+54 from 52° N, where the field test will probably be, and J0437 from 30° S, because J0437 never rises at 52° N (R6).

One design point, since 23 November 2026

Every table and figure on this page is for the array's design point: an SEFD of 6.44·10⁴ Jy and 150 MHz of bandwidth (R1). The runs are Claude's, of 23 November 2026; Jasper has not yet run them himself. Results for the receiver that was planned before that date (0.61 m², 400 MHz) remain only in the History boxes, as a dated record.

R1 · Receiver

Every number on this page starts from the receiver. It is an array of about 3 m² effective area, with dual polarization, in the band 1.2–1.6 GHz, at a static site (R6). Its system temperature is about 70 K, measured at the array's output. With the RFI, about 150 MHz of the band is clean at that 70 K. These are the antenna engineering team's figures, passed on by Jasper on 23 November 2026. Where the clean 150 MHz sits is not known; the runs take channels 33–80 of the 128, which is 1300–1450 MHz. The receiver must be site-independent ("should work anywhere"): the station position is an input parameter, no pulsar is hard-coded, and sources are chosen by visibility.

The antenna team specifies a receiver by its sensitivity and its SEFD, not by an SNR. Both describe the instrument alone, whereas an SNR also needs a pulsar, a bandwidth and an observing time. The sensitivity is Aeff/Tsys, and the SEFD follows from it; a lower SEFD is better (glossary):

This SEFD with the 150 MHz is the design point of every run on this page, and the default of runScenario.m, under the name "array3m2". It is the only design point: no worst case has been defined for this array. For comparison, Parkes has an SEFD of about 30 Jy and a 25 m dish about 1000 Jy. An SNR follows from the SEFD through Eq. (R.1) of the next section, for a named pulsar, bandwidth and time. The time per TOA goes as SEFD²/B, and a position error as SEFD/√B. The sky adds to Tsys: about 3–5 K off the Galactic plane, and 5–20 K for sources in the plane such as Vela. Whether the measured 70 K includes it is not known.

Even with this array the pulse peak is 38 to 44 dB below the receiver noise for the two best pulsars (R2). That is why the project targets the weak-signal regime, and why, for example, improvements to the estimator at high SNR have low priority.

History: the receiver planned until 23 November 2026

From 6 Oct to 23 Nov 2026 the receiver was planned as an array of 0.61 m² (3×3 elements, 4×4 on the same area since 20 Nov 2026), with a digital beamformer on its data streams (18, then 32), Tsys of 60–80 K, a beam of about 15°, about 22 dBi, and all 400 MHz in use. It had two design points: "nominal" (0.61 m², 70 K, SEFD 3.2·10⁵ Jy, 0.0087 m²/K, 3.8 dB/K) and "worst case" (0.3 m², 80 K, SEFD 7.4·10⁵ Jy), which was the one to design for (Jasper's decision, 6 Oct 2026). Every result dated before 23 November 2026 belongs to them, and runScenario.m keeps both under the names "nominal" and "worst". Against the nominal one, the design point of today has the time per TOA × 0.108, ρ + 6.96 dB, and a position error × 0.329. The record of the change is logs/2026-11-23_array-3m2-analog-beams.md.

R1.1 The array and its two beams

The array is a 4×4 grid of tiles, and each tile holds 4×4 dual-polarized bowtie elements: 256 elements in all. Its beams are formed in analogue hardware, by delay-and-sum in two stages: inside each tile, then across the 16 tiles. The delays are fixed for good, and they are hardwired to two directions:

Beam 1
(θ, φ) = (0°, 0°): the panel's normal
Beam 2
(θ, φ) = (20°, 45°): tilted 20° from the normal, along the grid's diagonal (θ is the angle from the normal, φ the direction in the panel's plane)
Outputs
four analogue signals for the whole array: two beams × two polarizations. They fit one RFSoC 4x2 board, which has four inputs

So this hardware has no digital beamforming and no electronic steering. A pulsar is seen only while it is inside one of the two beams, and pointing anywhere else needs the panel to move. Whether the panel is fixed or on a mount has not been decided. The team's goal is to detect a pulsar within a single pass through the beam. The two beams are taken to come from a power splitter, so that both are live all the time, each with the full area. That is Jasper's working assumption of 23 November 2026, not yet confirmed by the team.

The geometry is Claude's arithmetic from 3 m² and 256 elements, not the team's data. The element pitch is 0.108 m, half a wavelength at 1.4 GHz, and the directivity is 29.2 dBi. The beam is about 6° wide:

R1.2 Two figures of merit

Because the mount is not decided, this site reports two figures of merit, and never mixes them. The first is the integration time per TOA: the hours of data that one TOA at SNR 7 needs, with the TOAs per day and the σ per TOA that go with it. It applies when the pulsar is followed through its pass, which for this hardware means the panel on a tracking mount. R7 to R9 give it, from runScenario.m. The second is the SNR per pass: the SNR, and the TOA error, of the one TOA that a pulsar gives when it drifts once a day through a fixed beam. It applies to a fixed panel. R7.4 gives it, from a scratch copy of the script, because the repository's script has no drift through a beam. Neither is converted into the other, and every position result says which case it belongs to.

R2 · Time per TOA

The SEFD and a pulsar's flux decide how long a TOA takes. For each pulsar, the catalogue gives its period P, pulse width W50 and mean flux Smean at 1.4 GHz. The radiometer equation gives the SNR of a folded profile after observing for a time t (dual polarization, 150 MHz); solving for SNR 7, the design rule from the threshold sweep, gives the time per TOA, and the TOA error at SNR 7 follows from the pulse width (physics):

Two conventions for ρ

The ρ in this table uses Speak = Smean·P/Weq, with the margin Weq = 2·W50. The simulator's snrDB is the peak of a Gaussian of FWHM W50: Speak = Smean·P/(1.0645·W50), 2.7 dB higher for the same pulsar. In snrDB terms a single Gaussian with J0437's W50 would be at about −40.5 dB; the runs use its measured profile, which is at −44.2 dB (R7.3). Do not combine the two: the simulator's own pulsar (10 ms, 5 %, one polarization, 400 MHz) at snrDB −44.2 needs about 0.7 h per TOA at SNR 7, not the 0.42 h of the J0437 row.

R3 · Who can see what

The table gives hours of integration, not of calendar time, because a pulsar can only be observed while it is well above the horizon. The scenario counts a pulsar as visible when it rises at least 20° above the horizon, and the hours per day it spends there turn integration hours into calendar days. The station position is an input, so this changes from site to site (Figure R.2).

R4 · Conclusions

To compare pulsars, the time and the precision of a TOA are combined into one figure of merit, σTOA·√t, in µs·√h; smaller is better. At the design point it is 8 for J0437, 12 for Vela, 170 for B0329+54, and 770–1630 for the other normal pulsars. The faint millisecond pulsars score well on paper (B1937+21 42, J1713+0747 520, J2145−0750 610), but a single TOA takes them tens to hundreds of hours of data.

By that measure Vela is as good as J0437. It is bright, with a TOA in under a minute, but it has a wider pulse, DM 67.8, glitches and timing noise. J0437 is by far the fastest millisecond pulsar to time, because the time per TOA scales as S⁻², so a pulsar 15× fainter takes 225× longer. J0437 resembles the voltage chain's simulated pulsar (P 5.8 ms against 10 ms; DM 2.6 against the default 25). This estimate gives it about 0.4 h per TOA, for σTOA ≈ 12 µs: 3.6 km along the line of sight. With its measured profile, at ρ = −44.2 dB, the fast simulator gives 1.7 h and 12.8 µs (R9).

Both good sources are southern, however. For northern sites B0329+54 is by far the fastest source: 0.09 h per TOA in this estimate, against 1.3 h for B0950+08, the next. From Europe and most of North America a millisecond pulsar's TOA takes very long: B1937+21, the fastest there, needs about 72 h of data. Normal pulsars are needed anyway, for geometry (≥ 4 well-separated directions for 3-D position + clock) and for visibility, at the cost of wide pulses (σTOA 0.3–0.7 ms at SNR 7) and timing noise.

R5 · Consequences for the pipeline

These conclusions forced seven things on the design. The first is that the signal is weak everywhere: ρ is −38 dB for B0329+54 and −44 dB for J0437, so the per-pulse SNR is ≪ 1. Self-noise is then negligible and FFTFIT is optimal, but any phase-dependent bias must be ≪ 4·10⁻⁵ of the baseline for J0437. A weak signal takes time, and that has two more consequences. A TOA of a weaker pulsar needs more data than one pass holds: B1929+10 needs 11.5 h (R2), and with a fixed panel even B0329+54 gets only a pass worth about half an hour a day (R7.4). So the fold must run across passes with gaps, which makes barycentric phase prediction (D, stage 7a) a hard requirement. And a position needs weeks of TOAs, which cannot be simulated at voltage level; that is why the fast simulator (C) exists.

The pulsars themselves set two more. The DM target is 100 (Vela 67.8, B1937+21 71.0, B0740−28 73.8, plus margin), which calls for the channelized front end on the processing side. Dual polarization gives √2 in SNR (2× in time), so the second polarization must be added.

The last two come from the environment. Scintillation dominates the per-pass SNR of low-DM pulsars, so passes are weighted by their measured SNR and the sub-int length adapts. RFI is far stronger than the pulsar, so excision must blank, before dedispersion (block B).

R6 · The site

Where the array observes from matters as much as what it observes. The array stands still, but its site is not known beforehand, so no latitude can be built into the method. The scenario runs still need one, and use two. The first is 52° N, in the Netherlands, where the field test will probably be. From there J0437 (maximum elevation −9°) and Vela (−7°) never rise. B0329+54 never sets, and is above 20° for 19.6 h a day. The second is 30° S, where J0437 is up 12.3 h a day. The runs of R7 to R9 follow the pulsar through that time with constant gain: the "followed" case of R1.2, the array on a tracking mount. A fixed panel sees one short pass a day through each beam (R7.4).

History

Jasper's information, 20 Oct 2026: the array would go on a ship, with the Netherlands as its location; the 30° S runs stood for a ship that sailed south. The receiver had first been drafted, on 6 Oct, as a ground station. Since 2 Nov 2026 the goal is a static array at a site that is not known beforehand.

That makes B0329+54 the scenario's northern pulsar, so its own properties matter. At 1.4 GHz it has P 714.5 ms, DM 26.8 (which is why the default DM is 25), W50 6.6 ms and S1400 203 mJy; scintles of about 7 MHz × 13 min (ATA 2025, Wang et al. 2005); five profile components, and an abnormal mode about 15 % of the time. Its ρ in the simulator's convention, with the five components, is −38.0 dB at the design point.

R7 · Simulated TOA budget (fast simulator)

The catalogue estimates of R2 are checked with the fast simulator, which uses the pulsars' real profiles: B0329+54 with its five components (R7.2) and J0437 with its measured eight-component profile (R7.3). All runs use runScenario.m at its design point "array3m2": an SEFD of 6.44·10⁴ Jy, channels 33–80 of the 128 (1300–1450 MHz), and dual polarization. The pulsar is followed through its daily pass above 20° with constant gain, so R7.1 to R7.3, R8 and R9 give the first figure of merit of R1.2, the integration time per TOA. Each case covers 30 simulated days, with receiver noise only ("clean"; RFI and scintillation follow in R8). TOAs are formed by merging 120 s or 300 s sub-ints until SNR ~7, also over the gap to the next pass; the true pulse is 0.1 FWHM away from the prediction, and there is no motion.

R7.2 B0329+54's real profile (five components)

B0329+54's five components at 1410 MHz spread the same flux over twice the pulse area of a single Gaussian with the catalogue W50. The peak is therefore 3 dB lower, and a TOA needs about 3× more data. The runs are at NBin 2048, where the narrowest component is 11 bins wide. At NBin 1024 it was only 5.6 bins wide, and the error bars came out too small (History).

Even at NBin 2048 a rare low-SNR outlier remains: a TOA that lands about one core width off, ±0.0064 turns (4.5 ms). In the runs at the design point 0.1 % of B0329+54's TOAs lie more than 5σ off. The first runs showed what they are (20–21 Oct 2026, History): TOAs at |z| 6.4–6.9 with SNR 7.6–7.8 and χ² 1.00–1.02, so χ² cannot flag them; the pull test at SNR 7 gave the same kind (2 of 600). The cause is the shape of the template: its autocorrelation has no second peak there, it is 0.75 on a smooth shoulder, and the first side peak is at ±0.025 turns. A narrow core on a broad component lets noise move the maximum onto the shoulder now and then. The error bar comes from the curvature at the peak, so it does not cover this: it is the estimator's threshold effect. At SNR 10 there were none (0 of 600). For navigation: use a robust (outlier-tolerant) position fit, or a target SNR of about 10 for B0329+54 (limits).

R7.3 J0437's real profile has wings: about 5× more data per TOA

A single Gaussian with the catalogue W50 of 0.141 ms is not what J0437 looks like: its W10 is 1.02 ms, four times a Gaussian's. The measured Parkes profile at 1369 MHz (Dai et al. 2015, EPN database, CC BY 4.0) gives a Weq 2.4× the Gaussian's, so ρ is 3.75 dB lower: −44.2 dB at the design point. The wings add flux but little timing, and the core is sharper than a Gaussian, so the resolution rule needs NBin 1024. The profile was fitted with 8 Gaussian components, whose W50, W10, Weq, spectral width and timing merit are within 1 % of the measured profile's. Against the single Gaussian, the first runs with this profile needed 5.3× the data per TOA and had 2.5× the σ·√h; the white-noise estimate predicted 5.1× and 2.53× (History).

One thing is open for J0437: its error bars are too small in the clean runs. The rms/pred is 1.33 over 30 days and 1.23 over 100 days (R9), where 1 is honest. This has not been looked into.

R7.4 A single pass through a fixed beam Claude's run

This is the second figure of merit of R1.2: the SNR of one pass, for a fixed panel. B0329+54 drifts through a fixed beam once a day, and gives one TOA per pass. The fast simulator has no gain that changes within a pass. A pass is therefore simulated as a window at full gain that carries the same SNR² as a drift through a Gaussian beam of the stated width: 0.753 × the beam width, divided by the drift rate of 15°/h × cos δ. The runs are scratch runs with a copy of the scenario script, at the design point, clean, over 120 passes through the beam centre:

The two rows are alternatives, not a range. An area of 3 m² implies a beam of about 6° (R1.1); Jasper named a beam of 15°. A 15° beam and 3 m² do not belong to one filled aperture, so both were run, and the team's measured beam width decides which row holds. Two things lower the SNR of a pass, and both are scaled from the 6.3° row, not simulated. A pass off the beam centre loses gain: a zenith-facing panel has B0329+54 2.6° off centre, which gives SNR × 0.62. And on a low-flux day (the 5th percentile, 0.45 of the mean flux, from the literature) the SNR is about 5.

A TOA per day is not yet a position. A pulsar that is seen every day at the same hour angle gives no position at all: the site's delay at that hour angle is a constant, and it merges with the pulsar's own phase constant. R8 · With realistic RFI: the scenario variants

Inside the clean 150 MHz the realistic L-band RFI costs little: about 2 % of the TOAs per day. The RFI is the measured 1 s profile after excision, cut to the design point's channels. Of the model's sources, channels 33–80 hold the radar at 1332.9 MHz, the carrier at 1388.9 MHz and the broadband impulses; LTE, GNSS and Inmarsat lie outside them. Each variant has its own effect. The RFI costs B0329+54 about 2 % of its TOAs; J0437's rows, with about 200 TOAs each, cannot resolve so small a loss. Without the per-channel noise calibration χ² is 0.985, and the pChi2 test flags too few TOAs: 0.7 % and none, where 5 % is expected. With the calibration χ² is 1.00 and 3–4 % are flagged, at no cost in TOAs. The calibration therefore stays the setting for real data, although inside the clean band it changes little.

Scintillation depends on the scintle size. B0329+54's scintles (~7 MHz) are much narrower than the band. They widen the spread of the time per TOA, from 0.17–0.30 h to 0.10–0.40 h, and B0329+54 gives 91 TOAs a day instead of 85 (88 over 100 days, R9). With scintillation 10–11 % of its TOAs have a pChi2 below 0.05, where 5 % is expected; this has not been looked into. J0437's scintles (~740 MHz) make the whole band fade and brighten over about 40 min, and a TOA of 1.75 h is short enough for adaptive groups to ride the bright spells: 10.7 TOAs a day instead of 6.8, with 0.08–3.42 h of data per TOA, because information grows as the flux squared, and ⟨S²⟩ > ⟨S⟩².

Fixed groups are not safe. Their rms/pred is 5.54 for B0329+54 and 2.55 for J0437, and 0.4 % and 1.8 % of their TOAs lie more than 5σ off. Fixed groups reach down to a lower SNR than adaptive ones, where a TOA can jump to the template's side peak (the first run of this kind, on 21 Oct 2026, had one at −0.0259 turns; the autocorrelation's side peak is at ±0.0247). For navigation: use adaptive groups, a robust fit, or a higher target SNR for pulsars with several components.

History: the variants over the full band, until 23 November 2026

At the design points before 23 November 2026 (0.61 m², all 128 channels over 400 MHz; Claude's runs of 23 Oct 2026) the same RFI model cost much more, in a band that also holds the model's LTE, GNSS and Inmarsat sources: about 20–25 % of the TOAs per day and 10–15 % in σ·√h, the SNR loss of the measured profile squared (×0.895, expSimRFI). Without the calibration χ² was 1.07–1.08 and the pChi2 test flagged 89–100 % of the TOAs; with it χ² was 1.00 and at most 7 % were flagged. The voltage chain shows the same: χ² 1.24 → 0.99. B0329+54 gave 9.97 TOAs a day at nominal flux (clean) and 7.9 with RFI, at 734–746 µs. J0437 gave 0.73 a day at nominal flux and 0.13 in the worst case, with 15.5 h and 87 h of data per TOA; a TOA of about 20 h averaged over roughly 30 scintles of 40 min, so scintillation brought no gain then. The RFI profiles of those runs (per-channel detectRFI thresholds, 30 lags) are the ones in use now; the first profiles of 20 Oct are kept in data/rfi/profile_20oct/.

R9 · 100 days: the final budgets Claude's run

Over 100 days the budgets of the 30-day runs hold. The tail of the distribution matters for the navigation fit. In these runs 0.1 % of B0329+54's TOAs lie more than 5σ off. The fractions beyond 3σ and 4σ have not been taken from the new runs. The 100-day runs of 21–22 Oct 2026, at the design points before 23 November 2026 (978 TOAs at nominal flux), gave 1.9 % of B0329+54's TOAs beyond 3σ, against 0.27 % for a Gaussian, and 0.3–0.7 % beyond 4σ, while the central part of the pulls had a width of 1.01. Almost all of those TOAs had an SNR of 7.1–8.1, just above the grouping threshold: the template-shape threshold effect of R7.2. That tail set the outlier model of the navigation fit, which allows 2 % of the TOAs to scatter 4 times wider (navigation).
