Stage 7a · Phase prediction for a moving receiver

A pulsar's rotation is a clock at the centre of mass of the solar system, the barycentre. The receiver moves with the Earth and with its ship, so the pulses reach it early or late by up to ±500 s over a year and ±8 ms over a day. This stage predicts that motion, so that the fold (stage 7) stays aligned over hours and days. What the prediction does not explain becomes the position, in stage 10.

1. Why the phase must be predicted

The fold assigns every time bin a pulse phase. Without a predictor it uses a constant spin rate: φ(t) = f₀·(t − TRef). That is only right for a receiver at rest relative to the barycentre.

A real receiver moves. Its distance to the pulsar changes, so each pulse travels a little longer or shorter. The Earth's orbital speed changes the arrival rate by up to 10⁻⁴, a Doppler shift. Within one 120 s sub-integration that moves the pulse by 12 ms. That is more than B0329+54's 5 ms core width. For J0437 a 300 s sub-integration drifts by five whole turns. Without a prediction, every fold longer than a few minutes smears out.

This stage therefore computes the delay from the receiver to the barycentre. It turns that into a predicted phase, and the fold follows it. In the test, a pulsar with a Doppler factor of 10⁻³ folds as cleanly as one without Doppler: the profiles agree to 10⁻⁸ of the peak.

The terms of the delay, and how large they are:

Sections 2–4 build the prediction in three steps. The fold then follows it (stage 7). The fast simulator places the true pulses against it (trueResiduals). The difference between the two is what navigation measures (stage 10).

2 · makeTrajectory: where the receiver is validated

traj = makeTrajectory(type, ...)

Full interface and help text of makeTrajectory →

In short

makeTrajectory describes where the receiver is at any time. It returns one struct with a position function and a velocity function. The delay model and the predictor only ever see this struct, so they work the same for every kind of receiver.

How it works

TypeThe receiverFrame
'fixed'A site on the ground, given as latitude, longitude and height. An offset in east, north and up can move it, for example to put the true site 100 km from the assumed one.Earth-fixed
'track'A ship, given as waypoints in time. The positions between them are interpolated: linearly, smoothly (pchip) or as a cubic spline.Earth-fixed
'barycentric'A deep-space probe, given as positions relative to the barycentre.Barycentric
'custom'Anything that can return positions, such as a Kalman filter's propagated state.Either

Times are seconds since J2000.0 (1 January 2000, 12:00 TT). Positions are in metres. The Earth-fixed frame rotates with the Earth; the delay model adds that rotation.

One entry point for every vehicle

The end goal is sensor fusion: the pulsars combined with the vehicle's own motion sensors, on a ship or on a deep-space probe. A motion predictor such as a Kalman filter then supplies the trajectory. The 'custom' type is where it plugs in. Nothing else in block D needs to know what kind of vehicle it is.

3 · delayModel: from the receiver to the barycentre validated

d = delayModel(t, traj, psr, ...)

Full interface and help text of delayModel →

In short

delayModel gives the time to add to the receiver's arrival time to get the arrival time at the barycentre. It returns the total and each term separately.

How it works

  1. The Earth: by default, the centre of mass of the Earth and Moon follows a Keplerian orbit around the Sun, from JPL's approximate orbital elements. Jupiter and Saturn move the Sun around the barycentre, by about 0.005 AU. The Moon moves the Earth around the Earth–Moon centre by about 4700 km. With 'Ephemeris' the Earth and the Sun come from JPL's DE440 instead (loadEphemeris).
  2. The receiver: an Earth-fixed position is turned with the Earth's rotation angle and added to the Earth's position. A barycentric trajectory is used as it is.
  3. The terms: the Roemer delay projects the receiver's position onto the direction of the pulsar. The Einstein delay and the Shapiro delay are added from their standard formulas.
The formulas and their signs

With r the receiver relative to the barycentre and n̂ the unit vector to the pulsar:

\[ \Delta = t_\mathrm{SSB} - t = \frac{\mathbf r\cdot\hat n}{c} \;-\; \frac{|\mathbf r|^2 - (\mathbf r\cdot\hat n)^2}{2cD} \;+\; \Delta_E \;+\; 2T_\odot \ln\frac{|\mathbf r_\odot| + \mathbf r_\odot\cdot\hat n}{\mathrm{AU}} \]
  • The first term is the Roemer delay. A receiver displaced towards the pulsar gets each pulse earlier, so the barycentre's time is later.
  • The second term corrects for the curved wave front of a pulsar at distance D. It is about 1 µs for B0329+54.
  • ΔE = TDB − TT is the Einstein delay: 1.657 ms·sin g + 14 µs·sin 2g, with g the Earth's mean anomaly, plus a site term vE·rsite/c² of about 1 µs. With 'Ephemeris' the first two terms are replaced by TDB − TT integrated from DE440 (loadEphemeris).
  • The last term is minus the Shapiro delay. Here r⊙ is the receiver relative to the Sun, and T⊙ = GM⊙/c³ = 4.925 µs. It becomes strongly negative when the Sun is close to the line of sight.
An analytic Earth first, the exact one at the end of block D

The analytic orbit puts the Earth within about 1.5·10⁴ km of its true distance from the Sun. The Sun itself is off by up to 3·10⁵ km from the barycentre, because the analytic model leaves out Uranus and Neptune. B0329+54's Roemer delay is then off by up to 0.66 s, and 71 ms of that varies within two years. In the simulator this does not matter, because the true delays and the predicted ones come from the same model. Only their difference reaches the navigation fit, and that difference is the same with either orbit to 2·10⁻¹² s. Real data need JPL's planetary ephemeris DE440, which is accurate to centimetres, plus precession and nutation of the Earth's axis. loadEphemeris reads DE440 (below); delayModel takes it behind the same interface in the next step.

3b · loadEphemeris: JPL's DE440 passes in Claude's run

eph = loadEphemeris(file); [r, v] = eph.earth(t)

Full interface and help text of loadEphemeris →

In short

JPL's planetary ephemeris DE440 gives the positions of the Sun, the Earth and the Moon relative to the barycentre. For the Earth today it is accurate to well below a metre. loadEphemeris reads JPL's binary file once. It returns functions that evaluate it, in the same way as a trajectory from makeTrajectory.

How it works

  1. The file: de440s.bsp from JPL's NAIF server, 32.7 MB, covering 1850 to 2150. It lives in data/ephem/.
  2. The segments: each body has a sequence of equal time intervals. Each interval holds Chebyshev polynomial coefficients for x, y and z.
  3. Evaluation: the time selects the interval. The polynomials give the position, and their derivatives give the velocity.
  4. The chain: the file stores the Earth relative to the Earth–Moon barycentre, and that barycentre relative to the solar-system barycentre. The function adds the links up.
  5. TDB − TT: at loading, the function integrates the rate of a clock at the geocentre along the Earth's path, every 6 hours over the whole file. That rate follows from the Earth's speed and the potential of the Sun, the Moon and the planets. The integral takes 0.8 s and replaces the analytic model's two terms.

The time is TDB, the ephemeris' own time scale. The frame is ICRF, which is the J2000 equator and equinox to 0.02″.

Reading JPL's file directly

Alternative: MATLAB's planetEphemeris from the Aerospace Toolbox, or JPL's SPICE toolkit.

Why not: the toolbox is not installed, and SPICE is a large external dependency. The file format is simple: about 60 lines of MATLAB read it. JPL's own Horizons service then checks the result to centimetres.

4 · makePredictor: the predicted pulse phase validated

pred = makePredictor(psr, traj, [t1 t2], ...)

Full interface and help text of makePredictor →

In short

makePredictor computes the pulse phase the receiver should see, once per observation. It stores the phase as short polynomials, so the fold only has to evaluate them.

How it works

  1. The phase at the barycentre: φ = Φ₀ + f₀·(tSSB − TRef) + ½·F1·(tSSB − TRef)², the pulsar's timing model.
  2. The arrival time: a pulse received at time t reaches the barycentre at tSSB = t + Δ(t), with Δ from delayModel.
  3. Segments: the span is cut into one-hour segments. In each, the phase is fitted with a Chebyshev polynomial of degree 12.
  4. Checks: each segment is compared with the direct computation between its fitting points. A segment that misses 1 ns is split in two, until it passes. A ship's sharp turn is the typical case: around it the segments shrink to a few seconds.

The predictor returns the phase and the observed spin frequency. That frequency includes the Doppler shift.

The receiver's samples are timed by its own oscillator, so the data's time stamps are clock readings, not true time. With the option 'Clock' the predictor gets the observer's estimate of its clock error. It turns each clock reading into its best estimate of true time before it computes the delays. A commercial SDR's oscillator is specified to ±2 ppm. Without this correction that rate would move the pulse by 240 µs within a 120 s sub-integration, like an uncorrected Doppler shift.

Polynomials instead of delays inside the fold

Radio observatories do the same: they compute "polycos" before an observation. It keeps all the physics out of the streaming fold, which only evaluates a polynomial. That is also the interface an FPGA or GPU version needs. The trajectory is the predictor's only input about the receiver, so a motion predictor plugs in without touching the fold.

Precision

Absolute times (about 8·10⁸ s) and absolute phases (up to about 10¹¹ turns) lose precision in double arithmetic. So the polynomials hold the phase counted from the integer turn at the start of the span, as a function of the time since that start. Only one constant carries the rounding of the large numbers, about 10⁻⁶ turns, and it is the same for every segment. There are therefore no steps between segments.
