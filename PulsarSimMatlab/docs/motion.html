Motion and phase prediction (block D)

A pulsar's rotation is a clock at the centre of mass of the solar system, the barycentre. The receiver moves with the Earth and with its ship, so the pulses reach it early or late by up to ±500 s over a year and ±8 ms over a day. Block D predicts that motion, so that a fold stays aligned over hours and days. Later, what the prediction does not explain becomes the position.

1. Why the phase must be predicted

The fold assigns every time bin a pulse phase. Until block D it used a constant spin rate: φ(t) = f₀·(t − TRef). That is only right for a receiver at rest relative to the barycentre.

A real receiver moves. Its distance to the pulsar changes, so each pulse travels a little longer or shorter. The Earth's orbital speed changes the arrival rate by up to 10⁻⁴, a Doppler shift. Within one 120 s sub-integration that moves the pulse by 12 ms. That is more than B0329+54's 5 ms core width. For J0437 a 300 s sub-integration drifts by five whole turns. Without a prediction, every fold longer than a few minutes smears out.

Block D therefore computes the delay from the receiver to the barycentre. It turns that into a predicted phase, and the fold follows it. In the test, a pulsar with a Doppler factor of 10⁻³ folds as cleanly as one without Doppler: the profiles agree to 10⁻⁸ of the peak.

2 · makeTrajectory: where the receiver is validated

traj = makeTrajectory(type, ...)

Full interface and help text of makeTrajectory →

In short

makeTrajectory describes where the receiver is at any time. It returns one struct with a position function and a velocity function. The delay model and the predictor only ever see this struct, so they work the same for every kind of receiver.

How it works

TypeThe receiverFrame
'fixed'A site on the ground, given as latitude, longitude and height. An offset in east, north and up can move it, for example to put the true site 100 km from the assumed one.Earth-fixed
'track'A ship, given as waypoints in time. The positions between them are interpolated: linearly, smoothly (pchip) or as a cubic spline.Earth-fixed
'barycentric'A deep-space probe, given as positions relative to the barycentre.Barycentric
'custom'Anything that can return positions, such as a Kalman filter's propagated state.Either

Times are seconds since J2000.0 (1 January 2000, 12:00 TT). Positions are in metres. The Earth-fixed frame rotates with the Earth; the delay model adds that rotation.

One entry point for every vehicle

The end goal is sensor fusion: the pulsars combined with the vehicle's own motion sensors, on a ship or on a deep-space probe. A motion predictor such as a Kalman filter then supplies the trajectory. The 'custom' type is where it plugs in. Nothing else in block D needs to know what kind of vehicle it is.

3 · delayModel: from the receiver to the barycentre validated

d = delayModel(t, traj, psr, ...)

Full interface and help text of delayModel →

In short

delayModel gives the time to add to the receiver's arrival time to get the arrival time at the barycentre. It returns the total and each term separately.

How it works

  1. The Earth: the centre of mass of the Earth and Moon follows a Keplerian orbit around the Sun, from JPL's approximate orbital elements. Jupiter and Saturn move the Sun around the barycentre, by about 0.005 AU. The Moon moves the Earth around the Earth–Moon centre by about 4700 km.
  2. The receiver: an Earth-fixed position is turned with the Earth's rotation angle and added to the Earth's position. A barycentric trajectory is used as it is.
  3. The terms: the Roemer delay projects the receiver's position onto the direction of the pulsar. The Einstein delay and the Shapiro delay are added from their standard formulas.
The formulas and their signs

With r the receiver relative to the barycentre and n̂ the unit vector to the pulsar:

\[ \Delta = t_\mathrm{SSB} - t = \frac{\mathbf r\cdot\hat n}{c} \;-\; \frac{|\mathbf r|^2 - (\mathbf r\cdot\hat n)^2}{2cD} \;+\; \Delta_E \;+\; 2T_\odot \ln\frac{|\mathbf r_\odot| + \mathbf r_\odot\cdot\hat n}{\mathrm{AU}} \]
  • The first term is the Roemer delay. A receiver displaced towards the pulsar gets each pulse earlier, so the barycentre's time is later.
  • The second term corrects for the curved wave front of a pulsar at distance D. It is about 1 µs for B0329+54.
  • ΔE = TDB − TT is the Einstein delay: 1.657 ms·sin g + 14 µs·sin 2g, with g the Earth's mean anomaly, plus a site term vE·rsite/c² of about 1 µs.
  • The last term is minus the Shapiro delay. Here r⊙ is the receiver relative to the Sun, and T⊙ = GM⊙/c³ = 4.925 µs. It becomes strongly negative when the Sun is close to the line of sight.
An analytic Earth first, the exact one at the end of block D

The analytic orbit is off by about 10⁴ km in absolute terms. In the simulator that does not matter, because the true delays and the predicted ones come from the same model. Only their difference reaches the navigation fit, and that difference is exact. Real data will need JPL's planetary ephemeris DE440, which is accurate to centimetres, plus precession and nutation of the Earth's axis. They will go behind the same interface at the end of block D.

4 · makePredictor: the predicted pulse phase validated

pred = makePredictor(psr, traj, [t1 t2], ...)

Full interface and help text of makePredictor →

In short

makePredictor computes the pulse phase the receiver should see, once per observation. It stores the phase as short polynomials, so the fold only has to evaluate them.

How it works

  1. The phase at the barycentre: φ = Φ₀ + f₀·(tSSB − TRef) + ½·F1·(tSSB − TRef)², the pulsar's timing model.
  2. The arrival time: a pulse received at time t reaches the barycentre at tSSB = t + Δ(t), with Δ from delayModel.
  3. Segments: the span is cut into one-hour segments. In each, the phase is fitted with a Chebyshev polynomial of degree 12.
  4. Checks: each segment is compared with the direct computation between its fitting points. A segment that misses 1 ns is split in two, until it passes. A ship's sharp turn is the typical case: around it the segments shrink to a few seconds.

The predictor returns the phase and the observed spin frequency. That frequency includes the Doppler shift.

The receiver's samples are timed by its own oscillator, so the data's time stamps are clock readings, not true time. With the option 'Clock' the predictor gets the observer's estimate of its clock error. It turns each clock reading into its best estimate of true time before it computes the delays. A commercial SDR's oscillator is specified to ±2 ppm. Without this correction that rate would move the pulse by 240 µs within a 120 s sub-integration, like an uncorrected Doppler shift.

Polynomials instead of delays inside the fold

Radio observatories do the same: they compute "polycos" before an observation. It keeps all the physics out of the streaming fold, which only evaluates a polynomial. That is also the interface an FPGA or GPU version needs. The trajectory is the predictor's only input about the receiver, so a motion predictor plugs in without touching the fold.

Precision

Absolute times (about 8·10⁸ s) and absolute phases (up to about 10¹¹ turns) lose precision in double arithmetic. So the polynomials hold the phase counted from the integer turn at the start of the span, as a function of the time since that start. Only one constant carries the rounding of the large numbers, about 10⁻⁶ turns, and it is the same for every segment. There are therefore no steps between segments.

5 · Folding with a predictor validated

foldProfile takes the predictor with the option 'Predictor'. Each time bin then gets its phase from the predictor instead of from a constant spin rate. The predictor must be built with its start at the recording's time zero.

Each sub-integration still gets a reference time and frequency, so that a TOA is the reference time plus the fitted phase offset divided by the frequency. The reference time is where the predicted phase reaches the sub-integration's middle turn. For a partial last sub-integration that turn can fall just after the data. There the phase continues linearly with the last frequency.

Without a predictor nothing changes: the old constant-rate fold is bit for bit the same.

6 · trueResiduals: how late the pulse really is validated

res = trueResiduals(t, psr, trajTrue, trajAssumed, ...)

Full interface and help text of trueResiduals →

In short

The observer folds with a predictor made from the trajectory it assumes. The pulses arrive where the true trajectory puts them. trueResiduals computes how late each pulse is against the prediction, and where it therefore sits in the observer's fold. That lateness is the signal navigation measures. Only the simulator calls this function, because it needs the true trajectory.

How it works

  1. The true arrival: a pulse that passes the barycentre at time S reaches the receiver at the true time t, with t + ΔT(t) = S. ΔT is delayModel on the true trajectory.
  2. The predicted arrival: the observer expects the same pulse at tA, with tA + ΔA(tA) = S. ΔA is delayModel on the assumed trajectory.
  3. The clock: the observer's clock reads c = t + ε(t) when the pulse arrives. ε is the clock error. The observer corrects the reading with its own estimate ε̂(c), the same one its predictor uses.
  4. The residual: r = c − ε̂(c) − tA. Only the part of the clock error that the observer has not estimated shifts the pulse. A positive residual means the pulse is late.
  5. In the fold: the pulse sits at phase f·r turns. f is the observed spin frequency of the observer's predictor.

Without the clock, the residual is the difference of the two delays, divided by one plus the Doppler factor. A site 100 km east of the assumed one gives B0329+54 a daily sine of 193.33 µs. That is 2.7·10⁻⁴ of a turn. A true receiver closer to the pulsar than assumed gives a negative residual, because the pulse arrives early.

The exact solution

r = x + ε(t) − ε̂(c), with x = t − tA = (ΔA − ΔT)/(1 + dΔA/dt) to first order. On the Earth the Doppler factor dΔA/dt is below 10⁻⁴, so the division changes 193 µs by at most 19 ns. The function solves x exactly, with Newton steps on ΔA(t − x) − ΔT(t) − x = 0. It works with small differences only, because absolute times of about 8·10⁸ s carry 1.2·10⁻⁷ s of rounding. The phase uses f = fspin·(1 + dΔA/dt), the frequency of the observer's predictor.

Half a turn is the limit

The fold cannot tell a pulse that is f·r turns late from one that is a whole turn later. A TOA is therefore unambiguous only while |f·r| stays below half a turn. For B0329+54 that allows a position error of ±107,000 km. For J0437 it allows only ±863 km. Beyond half a turn trueResiduals warns.

7 · makeClock: the receiver's clock validated

clk = makeClock([t1 t2], ...)

Full interface and help text of makeClock →

In short

Every time stamp comes from the receiver's oscillator, so the oscillator's error goes straight into every TOA. makeClock draws one realization of that error over time, for the simulator's truth. The observer never sees it. Its navigation filter will estimate the clock's offset and rate instead.

How it works

  1. The fixed part: an offset at the start, a rate and an ageing drift. A commercial SDR's reference oscillator is specified to ±2 ppm. Its rate can therefore be up to 2·10⁻⁶, which adds 7.2 ms per hour.
  2. The noise: the oscillator's frequency also wanders at random. Its spectrum has three parts, each set by one coefficient: white frequency noise (h₀), flicker (h₋₁) and a random walk (h₋₂).
  3. The grid: the noise is drawn on a 60 s grid over the whole span and is linear in between.

An oscillator's stability is usually given as its Allan deviation σy(τ). It is the typical change of the average frequency from one interval of length τ to the next. White noise makes it fall with τ, flicker keeps it flat, and a random walk makes it grow.

Preseth₀h₋₁h₋₂What it is
"perfect"000No noise (the default).
"crystal"1.8·10⁻²¹6.5·10⁻²²1.37·10⁻²⁴The worked example of Van Dierendonck, McGraw and Brown (1984): a crystal oscillator stable to 3 parts in 10¹¹.

The noise of a commercial SDR's oscillator is not published. It is best measured on the device itself, against a better reference.

The formulas, and how each part is drawn

σy²(τ) = h₀/(2τ) + 2 ln2·h₋₁ + (2π)²/6·h₋₂·τ (the paper's eqs. 6–8).

  • White: the time error takes independent steps of variance h₀Δ/2 per grid step Δ. This is exact.
  • Random walk: the frequency and the time error form a two-state process with covariance q₂·[Δ³/3, Δ²/2; Δ²/2, Δ] per step, q₂ = 2π²h₋₂. This is exact, and it is the model the Kalman filter will use.
  • Flicker: no finite-state model is exact. The step-averaged frequency is synthesized by FFT with the spectrum h₋₁/f, over twice the span, so that the FFT never joins the two ends. It is then integrated.

Between grid nodes the left-out noise is a white-noise bridge of √(h₀Δ/8): 0.12 ns for the crystal at 60 s.

Flicker included, by FFT

Alternative: only white and random-walk noise, the two that a two-state Kalman model describes exactly.

Why not: for the paper's crystal, flicker is the largest of the three noises from 1 s to 100 s. After 200 s it is 42 % of the time error's variance. Leaving it out would make the clock look better than it is.

8 · navFit: the site from the TOAs done

fit = navFit(toa, psrs, site, ...)

Full interface and help text of navFit →

In short

navFit finds where a fixed site really is from the residuals of its TOAs. Each residual is the site's offset projected onto the pulsar's direction. The Earth's rotation turns that into a daily sine, and a fit of many TOAs recovers the offset. A few TOAs near the detection threshold are far off; the fit recognises them and lets them count much less.

How it works

  1. The model: each residual is the offset times a known direction vector, plus a constant per pulsar. The direction vector is the pulsar's direction turned back by the Earth rotation angle at the TOA's time.
  2. What one pulsar sees: the two components in the equatorial plane rotate with the Earth and give the daily sine. The component along the Earth's axis gives a constant, the same as the clock offset and the pulsar's own offset.
  3. The switch 'Start': with "self" the constant is unknown and fitted, so the fit gives the two equatorial components. With "calibrated" the observer started from a known position and time, so the constant is known and the fit gives the full 3-D position.
  4. Robustness: the noise model is a mixture. Most TOAs follow their error bar; a fraction ε = 2 % scatter k = 4 times wider. The fit repeats: each TOA gets its probability of belonging to the core, its weight follows from that, and the weighted fit is solved again until it no longer changes.
  5. The result is given in the site's own directions: east, radial (in the equatorial plane, away from the Earth's axis) and polar. With "calibrated" also east, north and up.

East lies entirely in the equatorial plane, so an offset to the east is fully visible to one pulsar. North and up each have a part along the Earth's axis. For B0329+54 from 52° N with 302 TOAs of 734 µs, each equatorial component is known to about ±30 km. A calibrated start gives the polar component to about ±16 km, because the constant uses every TOA at once.

The formulas

\[ r_i = \mathbf{a}_i\cdot\delta\mathbf{x} + c_k + \text{noise}, \qquad \mathbf{a}_i = -R_z(\theta_i)^\mathsf{T}\hat{\mathbf{n}}_k/c \]

with δx the offset in Earth-fixed coordinates and θ the Earth rotation angle. The noise is (1 − ε)·N(0, σ²) + ε·N(0, (kσ)²). A TOA with normalized residual u gets the core probability γ = (1 − ε)φ(u) / [(1 − ε)φ(u) + (ε/k)φ(u/k)] and the weight γ/σ² + (1 − γ)/(kσ)². Left out: the residual's factor 1/(1 + Doppler), about 10⁻⁴, and the Einstein site term, about 2·10⁻⁴ of the offset. With 'Predict' the function returns only the covariance a TOA schedule would give, to plan which pulsars to observe and for how long.

A mixture, not a hard cut

Alternative: drop every TOA beyond 4σ and fit the rest.

Why not: the 100-day runs show a continuum of far-off TOAs between 3σ and 5σ, not a clean gap. A hard cut either keeps some or throws away good ones. The mixture lets each TOA count according to how likely it is to be good.

9. The simulator's truth, and next: the navigation fit and the clock

D3 · the simulator's truth in the scenario done

The fast simulator shifts each sub-integration's pulse by the phase offset from trueResiduals: runScenario with motion.on (run scripts). For a 100 km offset the residual changes by 1.4·10⁻⁸ s per second. Over a 120 s sub-integration that is 1.7 ns, far below a phase bin. One offset per sub-integration is therefore enough for a position error.

A clock rate is different. At the 2 ppm of a commercial SDR the pulse moves by 240 µs within a 120 s sub-integration. For B0329+54 that is 5 % of its 5 ms core. For J0437 a 300 s sub-integration moves it by 600 µs, four times its pulse width. The observer must therefore fold with its own estimate of the clock rate, as it does with the Doppler shift. The predictor takes that estimate (section 4). What is left for the simulator is the clock error the observer has not estimated.

A first check over two days reproduces the 100 km daily sine to 1.6·10⁻⁸ s. It also shows how much a free-running crystal wanders: about 76 µs in a day, and 13 ms in a month. The clock must therefore be part of the navigation fit.

The first 30-day run of B0329+54 with the true site 100 km east and a perfect clock gives the navigation fit its first data:

D4 · the navigation fit

The first goal is a site 100 km from where the observer thinks it is, found from B0329+54 alone. A site offset changes each TOA by the offset projected onto the pulsar direction. That direction turns once per sidereal day in the Earth-fixed frame. The two equatorial components of the offset therefore show up as a daily sine: 193 µs for 100 km east. The component along the Earth's axis gives a constant that cannot be told apart from the clock offset, so it goes into the clock. After 30 days of TOAs, each equatorial component should be known to about ±30 km. A 100 km offset is then a 3σ detection.

Rare outliers are handled by redundancy, not by fewer TOAs

At SNR 7 about 0.5 % of B0329+54's TOAs land one core width off, 5–9 times their error bar. A higher SNR target would avoid them, but would halve the number of TOAs. The fit instead checks each TOA against the prediction from the other TOAs and from the ship's dead reckoning. For B0329+54 that prediction is good to about 70 km, while an outlier is 1100–2000 km off. A 4σ test rejects a good TOA only about once in four to five years. The fit's error model also gets a small outlier component, with its rate and size taken from the simulator. Without this, one outlier among a day's ten TOAs would shift that day's position by about 130 km.

Clock synchronisation with extra pulsars

The clock error is the same for every pulsar, while a position error projects differently onto each pulsar's direction. Differences between pulsars therefore cancel the clock, as differences between satellites do in GNSS. Once the position is known, the common part of all residuals is the clock error itself: the pulsars discipline the receiver's clock.

For a fixed site and B0329+54 alone the clock barely matters. The crystal's wander adds about 0.9 µs to the 30-day estimate of the daily sine, against 60 µs from the TOA noise. The reason is that the Earth's rotation puts the position signal at a period of one day, while the clock wanders slowly. The clock does matter in two places. The component along the Earth's axis and the absolute clock are both constants, so one pulsar cannot separate them. And on a moving ship the dead-reckoning errors drift slowly too, just like the clock. Extra pulsars in other directions solve both.

The array forms several digital beams at once, so extra pulsars cost no time on B0329+54. From 52° N they are weak, though: B0950+08, B1929+10, B1642−03 and B1749−28 give TOAs of 3–6 ms after about 4 hours. They therefore constrain the clock and the polar component over days to weeks, not hours. The crystal wanders less than 1 µs within an hour, so TOAs of different pulsars taken within hours share the same clock.

Each pulsar also has a constant offset between our profile's reference point and its timing model. A switch chooses how it is handled. Either the observer starts from a calibrated position and time, for example in port, or it estimates the offsets itself. Without calibration the polar component of a fixed site stays unobservable.

The clock as correlated noise, not as free segments

Alternative: a free clock offset and rate for every day.

Why not: a straight line per day absorbs part of a daily sine, and the daily sine is the position signal itself. The clock model from makeClock says how fast the clock can really wander. A fit that uses it as correlated noise lets the clock take only what it can physically do.

Later: the Kalman filter and real orbits

After the batch fit, a Kalman filter takes over. Its state is position, velocity and the clock, with a motion model per vehicle. Its predicted trajectory goes into the predictor through the 'custom' trajectory. When the filter improves its estimate, the stored sub-integration profiles are shifted by the phase correction instead of being folded again. Block D ends with J0437's binary orbit, JPL's DE440 ephemeris, and real ship and probe trajectories.

History

Block D's design was agreed on 21 Oct 2026: an analytic Earth orbit first and DE440 at the end; a phase predictor with a trajectory interface, for sensor fusion; B0329+54's 2-D equatorial offset as the first deliverable; the outlier handling by redundancy; the clock added after it had been left out of the plans. Later the same day: the receiver will be a commercial SDR; the observer folds with its own clock estimate; D4 was designed around Jasper's idea of clock synchronisation with extra pulsars, with simultaneous digital beams and a switch between a calibrated start and self-calibration.
