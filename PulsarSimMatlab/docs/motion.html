Motion and phase prediction (block D)

A pulsar's rotation is a clock at the centre of mass of the solar system, the barycentre. The receiver moves with the Earth and with its ship, so the pulses reach it early or late by up to ±500 s over a year and ±8 ms over a day. Block D predicts that motion, so that a fold stays aligned over hours and days. Later, what the prediction does not explain becomes the position.

1. Why the phase must be predicted

The fold assigns every time bin a pulse phase. Until block D it used a constant spin rate: φ(t) = f₀·(t − TRef). That is only right for a receiver at rest relative to the barycentre.

A real receiver moves. Its distance to the pulsar changes, so each pulse travels a little longer or shorter. The Earth's orbital speed changes the arrival rate by up to 10⁻⁴, a Doppler shift. Within one 120 s sub-integration that moves the pulse by 12 ms. That is more than B0329+54's 5 ms core width. For J0437 a 300 s sub-integration drifts by five whole turns. Without a prediction, every fold longer than a few minutes smears out.

Block D therefore computes the delay from the receiver to the barycentre. It turns that into a predicted phase, and the fold follows it. In the test, a pulsar with a Doppler factor of 10⁻³ folds as cleanly as one without Doppler: the profiles agree to 10⁻⁸ of the peak.

2 · makeTrajectory: where the receiver is done

traj = makeTrajectory(type, ...)

Full interface and help text of makeTrajectory →

In short

makeTrajectory describes where the receiver is at any time. It returns one struct with a position function and a velocity function. The delay model and the predictor only ever see this struct, so they work the same for every kind of receiver.

How it works

TypeThe receiverFrame
'fixed'A site on the ground, given as latitude, longitude and height. An offset in east, north and up can move it, for example to put the true site 100 km from the assumed one.Earth-fixed
'track'A ship, given as waypoints in time. The positions between them are interpolated: linearly, smoothly (pchip) or as a cubic spline.Earth-fixed
'barycentric'A deep-space probe, given as positions relative to the barycentre.Barycentric
'custom'Anything that can return positions, such as a Kalman filter's propagated state.Either

Times are seconds since J2000.0 (1 January 2000, 12:00 TT). Positions are in metres. The Earth-fixed frame rotates with the Earth; the delay model adds that rotation.

One entry point for every vehicle

The end goal is sensor fusion: the pulsars combined with the vehicle's own motion sensors, on a ship or on a deep-space probe. A motion predictor such as a Kalman filter then supplies the trajectory. The 'custom' type is where it plugs in. Nothing else in block D needs to know what kind of vehicle it is.

3 · delayModel: from the receiver to the barycentre done

d = delayModel(t, traj, psr, ...)

Full interface and help text of delayModel →

In short

delayModel gives the time to add to the receiver's arrival time to get the arrival time at the barycentre. It returns the total and each term separately.

How it works

  1. The Earth: the centre of mass of the Earth and Moon follows a Keplerian orbit around the Sun, from JPL's approximate orbital elements. Jupiter and Saturn move the Sun around the barycentre, by about 0.005 AU. The Moon moves the Earth around the Earth–Moon centre by about 4700 km.
  2. The receiver: an Earth-fixed position is turned with the Earth's rotation angle and added to the Earth's position. A barycentric trajectory is used as it is.
  3. The terms: the Roemer delay projects the receiver's position onto the direction of the pulsar. The Einstein delay and the Shapiro delay are added from their standard formulas.
The formulas and their signs

With r the receiver relative to the barycentre and n̂ the unit vector to the pulsar:

\[ \Delta = t_\mathrm{SSB} - t = \frac{\mathbf r\cdot\hat n}{c} \;-\; \frac{|\mathbf r|^2 - (\mathbf r\cdot\hat n)^2}{2cD} \;+\; \Delta_E \;+\; 2T_\odot \ln\frac{|\mathbf r_\odot| + \mathbf r_\odot\cdot\hat n}{\mathrm{AU}} \]
  • The first term is the Roemer delay. A receiver displaced towards the pulsar gets each pulse earlier, so the barycentre's time is later.
  • The second term corrects for the curved wave front of a pulsar at distance D. It is about 1 µs for B0329+54.
  • ΔE = TDB − TT is the Einstein delay: 1.657 ms·sin g + 14 µs·sin 2g, with g the Earth's mean anomaly, plus a site term vE·rsite/c² of about 1 µs.
  • The last term is minus the Shapiro delay. Here r⊙ is the receiver relative to the Sun, and T⊙ = GM⊙/c³ = 4.925 µs. It becomes strongly negative when the Sun is close to the line of sight.
An analytic Earth first, the exact one at the end of block D

The analytic orbit is off by about 10⁴ km in absolute terms. In the simulator that does not matter, because the true delays and the predicted ones come from the same model. Only their difference reaches the navigation fit, and that difference is exact. Real data will need JPL's planetary ephemeris DE440, which is accurate to centimetres, plus precession and nutation of the Earth's axis. They will go behind the same interface at the end of block D.

4 · makePredictor: the predicted pulse phase done

pred = makePredictor(psr, traj, [t1 t2], ...)

Full interface and help text of makePredictor →

In short

makePredictor computes the pulse phase the receiver should see, once per observation. It stores the phase as short polynomials, so the fold only has to evaluate them.

How it works

  1. The phase at the barycentre: φ = Φ₀ + f₀·(tSSB − TRef) + ½·F1·(tSSB − TRef)², the pulsar's timing model.
  2. The arrival time: a pulse received at time t reaches the barycentre at tSSB = t + Δ(t), with Δ from delayModel.
  3. Segments: the span is cut into one-hour segments. In each, the phase is fitted with a Chebyshev polynomial of degree 12.
  4. Checks: each segment is compared with the direct computation between its fitting points. A segment that misses 1 ns is split in two, until it passes. A ship's sharp turn is the typical case: around it the segments shrink to a few seconds.

The predictor returns the phase and the observed spin frequency. That frequency includes the Doppler shift.

Polynomials instead of delays inside the fold

Radio observatories do the same: they compute "polycos" before an observation. It keeps all the physics out of the streaming fold, which only evaluates a polynomial. That is also the interface an FPGA or GPU version needs. The trajectory is the predictor's only input about the receiver, so a motion predictor plugs in without touching the fold.

Precision

Absolute times (about 8·10⁸ s) and absolute phases (up to about 10¹¹ turns) lose precision in double arithmetic. So the polynomials hold the phase counted from the integer turn at the start of the span, as a function of the time since that start. Only one constant carries the rounding of the large numbers, about 10⁻⁶ turns, and it is the same for every segment. There are therefore no steps between segments.

5 · Folding with a predictor done

foldProfile takes the predictor with the option 'Predictor'. Each time bin then gets its phase from the predictor instead of from a constant spin rate. The predictor must be built with its start at the recording's time zero.

Each sub-integration still gets a reference time and frequency, so that a TOA is the reference time plus the fitted phase offset divided by the frequency. The reference time is where the predicted phase reaches the sub-integration's middle turn. For a partial last sub-integration that turn can fall just after the data. There the phase continues linearly with the last frequency.

Without a predictor nothing changes: the old constant-rate fold is bit for bit the same.

6. Next: the simulator's truth, the navigation fit and the clock

D3 · the simulator's truth

The fast simulator will place the pulses where a true trajectory puts them, while the observer folds with a predictor from the assumed trajectory. Their difference is the navigation signal. The simulator will also add a clock process to the true time stamps (see below).

D4 · the navigation fit

The first goal is a site 100 km from where the observer thinks it is, found from B0329+54 alone. A site offset changes each TOA by the offset projected onto the pulsar direction. That direction turns once per sidereal day in the Earth-fixed frame. The two equatorial components of the offset therefore show up as a daily sine: 193 µs for 100 km east. The component along the Earth's axis gives a constant that cannot be told apart from the clock offset, so it goes into the clock. After 30 days of TOAs, each equatorial component should be known to about ±30 km. A 100 km offset is then a 3σ detection.

Rare outliers are handled by redundancy, not by fewer TOAs

At SNR 7 about 0.5 % of B0329+54's TOAs land one core width off, 5–9 times their error bar. A higher SNR target would avoid them, but would halve the number of TOAs. The fit instead checks each TOA against the prediction from the other TOAs and from the ship's dead reckoning. For B0329+54 that prediction is good to about 70 km, while an outlier is 1100–2000 km off. A 4σ test rejects a good TOA only about once in four to five years. The fit's error model also gets a small outlier component, with its rate and size taken from the simulator. Without this, one outlier among a day's ten TOAs would shift that day's position by about 130 km.

The clock

Every time stamp comes from the receiver's clock, so its errors go straight into the TOAs. Three effects matter:

EffectWhat it doesSize, and what follows
Offset, rate and wander of the clockShift every TOA.A temperature-compensated crystal (TCXO) drifts about 3.6 µs per hour, an oven-controlled one (OCXO) 4–36 ns. B0329+54's 734 µs TOAs tolerate a TCXO; J0437's 13 µs TOAs need an OCXO or better. The clock offset and rate become part of the navigation state.
Frequency error within a foldSmears the fold like an uncorrected Doppler shift.10⁻⁹ over an hour is 3.6 µs: below B0329+54's bins.
Sampling jitter of the ADCAdds phase noise to the voltage samples.1 ps adds 10⁻⁴ of the input power as noise: negligible for the pulsar, a noise floor under strong RFI.

Later: the Kalman filter and real orbits

After the batch fit, a Kalman filter takes over. Its state is position, velocity and the clock, with a motion model per vehicle. Its predicted trajectory goes into the predictor through the 'custom' trajectory. When the filter improves its estimate, the stored sub-integration profiles are shifted by the phase correction instead of being folded again. Block D ends with J0437's binary orbit, JPL's DE440 ephemeris, and real ship and probe trajectories.
