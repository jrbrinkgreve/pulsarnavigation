Stages 1–2 · Sky: pulsar signal and dispersion

These two stages make the signal that reaches the antenna. Stage 1 creates the pulsar's radio noise. Stage 2 sends it through the interstellar medium, which delays the low frequencies. Both stages know the full ground truth; nothing after them may use it.

Run by runStage.sky in main.m · the slowest part of the pipeline · does not depend on receiver noise, so it runs once and is reused.

1 · generatePulsarSignal validated

info = generatePulsarSignal(outFile, T, f_in, A, L, dutycycle, ...)

Full interface and help text of generatePulsarSignal →

In short

It writes a file of random voltage samples, 4 billion per second, that get louder once per pulsar turn. It also records the exact time of every pulse centre. Those times are the ground truth that every TOA is checked against.

1.1 The signal: noise whose power pulses

A pulsar's radio emission comes from a huge number of independent emitters. Their sum is Gaussian noise, so there is no pulse waveform in the voltage. What changes with the rotation is the variance of the noise: its power (see physics). The generator therefore multiplies white noise by a slowly varying envelope:

(1.1)\[ x(t) = A\,\sqrt{G(t)}\;n(t) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathrm{E}\!\left[x^2(t)\right] = A^2\,G(t) \]
\(x(t)\)
the generated pulsar voltage at time \(t\) [V], written to the file at 4 GHz (f_in)
\(n(t)\)
white Gaussian noise with unit variance, a fresh random number for every sample
\(G(t)\)
the power profile: a train of Gaussian bumps, one per turn, with peak 1 and FWHM 5 % of the period (dutycycle)
\(A\)
the noise standard deviation at the pulse peak [V] (A = 1). It sets the signal scale
\(\mathrm{E}[\cdot]\)
expected value: the average over many noise realizations

The pulse appears only after squaring and averaging (Figure 1.1), which is why the processing detects power and folds. A deterministic pulse in the voltage would hide two things the pipeline must handle: the choice of detector, and the noise the pulsar adds itself (self-noise), which matters at high SNR. The noise is white from 0 to fin/2 = 2 GHz. That is a property of the simulation grid, not of real pulsars, whose spectra fall as a power law (see limitations).

1.2 The power profile

Eq. (1.1) needs G(t) at every sample. The code builds it in four steps.

  1. Pulse width. It is given as a duty cycle: FWHM = 5 % of T = 0.5 ms, so σ = 212.3 µs.
  2. Pulse times. Pulse k peaks at (k + ½)·T: at 5, 15, 25 ms, … These times are stored in info.pulseCenters.
  3. Profile, sample by sample. For each sample, the code evaluates the Gaussian of the nearest pulse. Neighbouring pulses are added only where they still reach, above ~20 % duty (Figure 1.2). The sum is scaled so that its peak is exactly 1.
  4. Samples. √G is multiplied by fresh Gaussian random numbers and by A, and written as float32 in blocks of 4 million samples.
Exact formulas and numbers
  • σ = FWHM / (2√(2 ln 2)).
  • Nearest pulse: k0 = round(t/T − ½).
  • Neighbours on each side: nNeighbors = ⌊11.75σ/T + ½⌋. This includes every Gaussian that reaches 10⁻³⁰ of the peak. At 5 % duty it is 0.
  • The sum is divided by its exact peak value (peakNorm), so overlapping pulses keep their true shape.
  • Above 20 % duty the function warns (generatePulsarSignal:overlap), because real pulsars stay below about 20 %.
  • N = round(L·fin) + 1 samples, including t = L. That is why file lengths end in …001.
  • Times are computed in double precision; only the written samples are single.
History

13 Oct 2026: the neighbour rule became ⌊11.75σ/T + ½⌋, bit-identical to the earlier max(1, ⌈6σ/T + ½⌉). The overlap warning was lowered the same day, from 50 % to 20 % duty.

The first pulse sits at T/2 on purpose. The observer's phase model starts at ephem.TRef = T/2 with phase 0, so every true pulse peak sits at a whole number of turns. The fold cuts turns halfway between pulses, so no pulse is split, and the true TOA offset is always 0. A hidden, nonzero offset is noted on the roadmap (item R3).

1.3 A pulse with several components

Real profiles are rarely one Gaussian. B0329+54 has five components, and J0437 has a sharp core with broad wings. A single Gaussian with the same width at half maximum puts too much power in the peak, which overstates the SNR. The option 'Components' therefore takes a list of Gaussians, one row each: amplitude, centre and FWHM, the last two in turns. Their sum is scaled to a peak of 1, so A keeps its meaning. pulseCenters are the times of the components' phase 0, which need not be the peak. The fast simulator and the TOA template use the same convention, and one centred component gives the single Gaussian bit for bit.

Details and history

Components need EnvelopeMode 'power'; dutycycle is then unused. Each component is summed around its own nearest pulse, like the single Gaussian. The peak is the maximum of the sum on a phase grid of 216 points and at every component's centre. In main.m the setting is profileComponents in pipelineParams.m. The voltage chain with B0329+54's five components agrees with the fast simulator within about 1 % (expComponentsChain). Added 21 Oct 2026.

1.4 Exact times, private random numbers

Each sample's time is computed in double precision from its index, never by adding up steps. The random numbers come from a private stream (mt19937ar, seed 42) that nothing else touches. The pulse times in info.pulseCenters are therefore exact, and the block size cannot change the data (test 5 of testGeneratePulsarSignal checks this bit for bit). The receiver noise has its own seed, so it can change without changing the pulsar. See conventions §5–6.

Design choices

A voltage at RF, not detected power

Every processing stage must later run unchanged on real data (the ground-truth rule), and real data starts as a voltage at the antenna. Simulating detected power, or the receiver's baseband, would skip dispersion, the I/Q receiver, coherent dedispersion and RFI blanking: exactly the stages that need testing. Starting at RF also makes the off-centre LO check on the receiver page possible. The price is that this is the slowest stage, and its memory limits the DM (limits). For long observations, the fast simulator draws folded profiles directly instead.

Sampling at 4 GHz

A real signal sampled at fin holds frequencies only up to fin/2, and the band reaches 1.6 GHz. 4 GHz leaves 0.4 GHz of margin above 3.2 GHz (Figure 1.3). It also divides by whole numbers to the receiver's sample rates: 800 MHz (÷ 5) and, originally, 500 MHz (÷ 8).

One Gaussian by default, with the width of the power profile

With one Gaussian, the TOA template is known exactly, so any TOA error comes from the pipeline. The TOA fit can take the same components as the generator when needed (TOA page, section 7). The width refers to the power profile, because the processing measures power. In 'power' mode (default) the power follows G with exactly the stated FWHM. The older 'amplitude' mode makes the power profile √2 narrower; its removal is noted on the roadmap (item R2).

info = applyDispersionStream(inFile, outFile, DM, fs, fLow, fHigh, ...)

In short

It delays every frequency in the band by the amount the interstellar medium would: the lowest frequencies arrive 31.5 ms after the highest (DM 25). It does this exactly, by filtering the voltage with a long chirp filter (126 million taps at DM 25), streamed through the file in blocks.

2.1 Dispersion

Between the pulsar and us lies ionized gas. Radio waves travel through it slightly slower than light, and lower frequencies are slowed more. The delay at frequency f, relative to an infinitely high frequency, is

(2.1)\[ \tau(f) = \frac{K\,\mathrm{DM}}{f^2} \]
\(\tau(f)\)
delay at radio frequency \(f\), relative to an infinitely high frequency [s]
\(f\)
radio frequency [MHz in this formula]
\(K\)
dispersion constant, 4.148808·10³ s MHz² pc⁻¹ cm³
\(\mathrm{DM}\)
dispersion measure: the column density of free electrons along the line of sight [pc cm⁻³] (DM = 5 in the worked numbers, 25 by default)

See physics. The delay also changes inside each sub-band, so the pulse is smeared there too (Figure 2.1). Shifting whole sub-bands back, or a Taylor expansion of the phase, cannot undo that smear; filtering the voltage with the inverse chirp can (stages 5 and 5b). Testing that only makes sense if the simulated dispersion is exact too, so stage 2 applies the exact phase.

Only the delay relative to the top of the band is applied (Figure 2.2). The top, 1.6 GHz, keeps its time, and 1.2 GHz arrives 6.302 ms later, as in a real observation. The delay that the whole band shares, τ(1.6 GHz) = 8.103 ms, would only shift everything. It is recorded in info.bulkDelayRef, so TOAs can later be converted to infinite frequency.

2.2 The filter

Dispersion is a filter: it changes the phase of each frequency, not its strength. The stage builds that filter once and runs the whole file through it. In frequency, the filter is

(2.2)\[ H(f) = W(f)\, \exp\!\left(+i 2\pi K\,\mathrm{DM}\, \frac{(f - f_\mathrm{ref})^2}{f\, f_\mathrm{ref}^2}\right) \exp\!\left(-i 2\pi f\, \frac{s_0}{f_s}\right) \]
\(H(f)\)
the dispersion filter at radio frequency \(f\) (the kernel's spectrum)
\(f\)
radio frequency [Hz]
\(f_\mathrm{ref}\)
radio reference frequency: content there keeps its time [Hz] (RefFreq, default fHigh = 1.6 GHz)
\(K\), \(\mathrm{DM}\)
dispersion constant and dispersion measure, as above; with \(f\) in Hz, \(K\) = 4.148808·10¹⁵ s Hz² pc⁻¹ cm³
\(W(f)\)
band window: 1 inside, 0 outside, sin² edges 8 MHz wide just inside 1.2 and 1.6 GHz
\(s_0\)
shift that keeps the start of the response inside the kernel [samples] (10,000 = 2.5 µs; removed again on output)
\(f_s\)
sample rate of the RF file [Hz] (f_in = 4 GHz)
\(i\)
the imaginary unit

The middle factor makes frequency f arrive τ(f) − τ(fref) late. Its magnitude is 1, so no frequency gets stronger or weaker.

Check: the group delay
\[ \tau_g(f) = -\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\frac{d\,\arg H(f)}{df} = \frac{K\,\mathrm{DM}}{f^2} - \frac{K\,\mathrm{DM}}{f_\mathrm{ref}^2} + \frac{s_0}{f_s} \]
\(\tau_g(f)\)
group delay of the filter at frequency \(f\) [s]: how late content at \(f\) comes out
\(\arg H(f)\)
the phase of the filter [rad]

That is the dispersion delay relative to \(f_\mathrm{ref}\), plus \(s_0\) samples.

A filter that disperses all frequencies down to 0 Hz cannot exist: τ(f) grows without limit as f → 0. Limiting it to the band changes nothing inside the band. Dispersion only changes phases, so energy never moves between frequencies. And dispersion and the receiver's band-pass are both linear and time-invariant, so their order does not matter. W(f) is therefore really the receiver's band-pass, applied a little early. (Strong out-of-band RFI in a real, slightly nonlinear receiver could create in-band products; that is not modelled.)

2.3 The kernel (makeDispersionKernel)

The kernel is Eq. (2.2) in time: 25,229,772 taps at 4 GHz. It is a chirp, with high frequencies first (Figure 2.3a). It is built in four steps.

  1. Sample H(f) on a grid of Nf = 2²⁶ points, at least twice the kernel length. Only the bins inside the band are computed, with the phase in double precision, because it spans about a million turns.
  2. Inverse FFT. The spectrum is made conjugate-symmetric, so the response is real.
  3. Keep the first Nk taps: a 2.5 µs guard, the 6.302 ms sweep, another 2.5 µs guard.
  4. Measure what was cut off: info.kernelLeakage = 3.5·10⁻¹³ of the energy. A warning appears above 10⁻⁸.

Sampling H(f) makes the time response periodic, so anything outside the kernel wraps around onto it. A grid of at least twice the kernel leaves an empty zone for that (Figure 2.3b), and the leakage measures what was cut, every run.

Exact formulas
  • Guard g = ⌈GuardTime·fs⌉ = 10,000 samples, with GuardTime = 20 / edge width = 2.5 µs.
  • Zero lag of fref: s0 = g + ⌈(τ(fref) − τ(fHigh))·fs⌉ (= g for fref = fHigh).
  • Kernel length Nk = s0 + ⌈(τ(fLow) − τ(fref))·fs⌉ + g + 1.
  • Design grid Nf = 2nextpow2(2Nk). The shift term uses mod(k·s0, Nf), exact even for large bin indices.
  • Kernel energy Σh² ≈ 0.195: the fraction of white-noise power passed, ≈ 2(B − 1.25·edge)/fs (the sin⁴ edges average 3/8).

2.4 Streaming by overlap-add

The file is far too long to filter in one piece. One FFT of 1 s of data would need 2³² points, about 189 GB, and direct convolution 25 million multiply-adds per sample. The file is therefore cut into blocks, each filtered with one FFT. The result is longer than the block by the kernel length minus one; that tail, the carry, is added to the start of the next block's result (Figure 2.4).

The block size is chosen automatically: the power-of-two FFT with the least total work that fits in MaxMemoryGB (16 GB). For the 1 s reference file:

FFT sizeuseful part of each FFTblocksmemorytotal work (2²⁸ = 1)
2²⁶62 %963.0 GB1.31

2²⁸91 %1711.8 GB1.00chosen

2³² (whole file)93 %1189 GB1.08over the budget

The work curve is flat near its minimum: small FFTs waste time on the overlap, huge ones on the extra log₂N per sample. The reference run took 81.5 s. Every output index is shifted back by s0, so 1.6 GHz keeps its original time.

The output is exactly as long as the input. In the last 6.3 ms, the low frequencies of the last pulses would arrive after the file ends; that part is dropped, as a real observation stops while a sweep is still arriving. The processing marks which output samples saw their complete sweep (fullySupported), and the fold uses only those.

Memory estimate and default

44 bytes per FFT sample, all single precision: the kernel spectrum (8), the block spectrum (8), the product (8), the inverse-FFT workspace (8), the real output (4), the input block (4) and slices (4). The MaxMemoryGB default is 16 GB (raised from 8 so that the 11.8 GB reference run fits on the 24 GB machine).

Design choices

Tapered and designed in frequency

An earlier version put a Hann window on the chirp in time. But in a chirp, time is frequency, so the window weakened the low end of the band (bugs and lessons #3). Tapering in frequency leaves the band flat, and the smooth edges keep the ringing short enough for a 2.5 µs guard. H(f) is also known exactly in frequency, taper included; the usual time-domain chirp formula is a stationary-phase approximation. The guard rule 20 / edge width is empirical: it gave leakage ~10⁻¹² in tests.

Overlap-add, not overlap-save

Both are exact. Overlap-add fits a filter that only reaches forward in time, as this one does with fref at the top of the band: each block's result spills into the next, and every input sample is read once. Direct convolution would take about 10¹⁷ multiply-adds per second of data, months of computing. The inverse filter in stage 5 reaches 6.3 ms into the future instead, and uses overlap-save (full-band page).

One reference frequency for both sides

The same refFreq sets the frequency that keeps its time here and the frequency that the observer dedisperses to. info.pulseCenters is the truth only because the two are equal. This is noted on the roadmap (review item R-D2).

Validation

  • testApplyDispersionStream checks the stage against its formula (unit tests). The impulse response matches with a relative error energy of 1.2·10⁻¹². At DM 5, tone bursts arrive within 0.001 sample of the predicted delay. Streaming in blocks matches one long convolution to 1.1·10⁻⁶.
  • Forward dispersion, IQ and dedispersion together give back the band-limited input to 1.9·10⁻⁶ (testApplyInverseDispersion).
  • plotDispersionCheck: the raw and dispersed dynamic spectra follow the expected sweep; the band-integrated power is as expected.
History: why the current form

The first dispersion kernel had the wrong sign: low frequencies arrived earlier. It also wrapped around inside the kernel. Nobody noticed, because the old inverse conjugated the same kernel, so the round trip still worked. The current chirp was written from the physics, with the sign checked by its group delay. Two more faults were fixed at the same time: an fftshift offset (a bulk delay of 8.4 ms that depended on the kernel length) and out-of-band content that wrapped around in time. See bugs and lessons #1–4.

Limits

Memory at high DM

The kernel grows with the sweep, and so with DM. At DM 30 this stage needs about 24 GB. From DM ~54 up to DM ~106, which includes DM 60 and DM 100, it needs about 47 GB: more than the 24 GB machine. The memory rises in steps, because the FFT sizes are powers of two (figure). On the processing side, the channelized front end solves this. On the generator side it is still open: a memory-efficient FFT, dispersion at complex baseband, or generating per channel are the options (roadmap).

Summary
  • A pulsar sends Gaussian noise whose power pulses once per turn (Eq. 1.1). The pulse appears only after squaring and averaging.
  • Every sample is computed from its absolute time with a private random stream, so the pulse times are exact ground truth.
  • Dispersion delays frequency f by K·DM/f² (Eq. 2.1). Stage 2 applies it exactly, as a band-limited chirp filter on the voltage (Eq. 2.2), relative to the top of the band.

The signal now reaches the antenna. The next page, the receiver, adds noise and RFI and converts the RF stream to complex baseband.
