Limitations and modelling gaps

What the simulation leaves out, what the processing assumes without checking, and how big each effect is. Each item says whether it matters for the weak-signal target (−54 dB, J0437 on a 0.61 m² array).

The voltage chain simulates 0.1–1 s, and it is exact for what it models. The fast simulator covers days. Four effects are larger than a J0437 TOA and must be handled before folding for hours. Three of them are modelled since block D: Earth's motion, the receiver's clock and the station position. The fourth, the pulsar's binary orbit, is left out and parked: J0437 never rises at 52° N. Other effects only matter for nanosecond navigation: the ionosphere, DM changes, scattering at high DM. A few matter only for real data, such as the Doppler-shifted dispersion delay. The receiver model is ideal, and the periodic-mask fix is parked with a design.

How big each effect is

Processing assumptions not checked

The processing takes four things on trust. The DM must be right: a wrong DM leaves a residual sweep, and a DM check from sub-band TOAs is planned (roadmap, longer term). The reference-frequency convention must match the TOA definition, or a constant offset appears when the forward and inverse references differ. The receiver frequency mapping is assumed to have no spectral inversion (fRF = fLO + fbb); the off-centre LO makes a mirror error visible in simulation, but real hardware must be checked. And the true sample rate is trusted via the info chain.

How scintillation would be modelled

Within a ≤ 1 s voltage simulation, diffractive scintillation is static: a random frequency gain g(f) with correlation bandwidth Δνd multiplied into the forward filter, plus optionally a multipath tail. Long-term fading, Doppler and DM(t) belong at the TOA / SNR level of a later mission simulation. With a scintillated spectrum, optimal detection weights channels by pulsar S/N (ChannelGain in the optimal fit).

Signal and receiver

The simulated signal and receiver are simpler than real ones in five ways. The first is the SNR regime itself: −5 dB is a bright pulsar on a large dish. A small antenna (SEFD ~10⁵ Jy) gives ρ ≈ −27 dB (Vela) to ≲ −50 dB (typical millisecond pulsars): per-pulse SNR ≪ 1, TOAs need minutes to hours of folding, where motion, DM(t) and scintillation matter.

The second is the spectrum: the generator is white, while real pulsars have S ∝ fα, α ≈ −1.6 (factor 1.6 across 1.2–1.6 GHz). Equal-weight loss ~1 %; with profile evolution and a DM error it biases wideband TOAs.

The third is the receiver, which is ideal: linear filters and infinite dynamic range. There is no amplifier compression, ADC quantization or clipping (2-bit costs ≈ 12 % SNR), intermodulation, bandpass ripple, gain drift. The voltage chain has a single polarization (dual pol gives √2 in SNR; the hardware is dual pol). The fast simulator covers the second polarization statistically (simulateFold 'NPol'), and the scenario runs use it. The second polarization is part of block E. The I/Q conversion is ideal too (a digital downconverter): no I/Q imbalance (a mirror image, −25…−40 dB before correction), no DC offset or LO leakage, no LO phase noise. Our LO (1.3 GHz) lies inside the band, so a DC spike and imbalance ghosts (RFI at 1.35 GHz → ghost at 1.25 GHz) would land in it; real hardware needs a low-IF LO or imbalance calibration.

The fourth is that receiver noise does not pass the dispersion taper in the simulation: only the pulsar is tapered at the band edges. On the full band this costs +0.4 % in σTOA, but +2.5 % on the default channel path. There the pulsar sees the band-edge taper and every channel's edges, while the noise sees only the channel edges. So the six edge channels hold 2–96 % of the pulse, although the optimal fit assumes equal pulse strength (ChannelGain 1). This is the whole 2.2 % gap between the channel path's and the full band's error bars: 2.943 against 2.880 µs (predicted +2.13 %, measured +2.19 %). With a realistic receiver, which applies the bandpass to pulsar and noise alike, the channel path would cost 0.37 % (logs/2026-10-13_deep-analysis.md, section 2).

The fifth is the real bandpass, which is neither ideal nor known exactly. It must be measured as W² from the off-pulse spectrum and used in (∫W²)²/∫W⁴; the difference can be 10–30 % on real data.

Timing and pulse

Timing has more gaps than any other area, and the first is the clock: the simulation has a model clock, not the real one. A clock rate off by 10⁻⁹ accumulates 72 µs over 20 h, about 6× the J0437 TOA precision. The simulator's truth therefore has a clock process with offset, rate and noise (the receiver's clock). The observer folds with its own clock estimate, and the navigation state holds the clock's offset and rate (navigation). The noise is that of the paper's crystal. Tracked day by day, its prediction one day ahead is off by a few hundred µs: harmless for B0329+54, too much for J0437's 0.14 ms pulse. The real SDR's oscillator is not characterised yet; its noise is best measured on the device. The ADC's sampling jitter is left out: 1 ps adds only 10⁻⁴ of the input power as noise.

Relative motion uses an analytic Earth orbit in the simulations and DE440 for real data. Earth's orbital speed changes the pulse rate by up to 10⁻⁴: at 100 Hz, about 100 µs of phase drift per second. The phase predictor follows that drift, and the fold uses it (phase prediction). The fast simulator puts every pulse where a true trajectory puts it (the simulator's truth). By default both use the same analytic Earth orbit. That orbit is up to 3.2·10⁵ km off the barycentre, because it leaves out Uranus and Neptune. In a simulation the error cancels: a 100 km offset is measured the same to 2·10⁻¹² s. For real data delayModel takes JPL's DE440 ('Ephemeris', phase prediction), which matches JPL Horizons to 28–30 m for a site.

The pulsar's own binary motion is not modelled at all. The reference pulsar J0437−4715 is in a 5.74-day orbit, with a projected size of 3.37 light-seconds. Its orbit moves the arrival time by about 3 s within one 20 h fold (0.15 s per hour). That is about 2·10⁵× the fold's phase budget (W50/10 ≈ 14 µs). J2145−0750 and J1713+0747 are binaries too. So the phase predictor needs a binary timing model before J0437 is used; the circular-orbit model ELL1 suffices for J0437 at the µs level. The simulator's truth then needs the same orbit. Both were parked at the close of block D: J0437 never rises at 52° N, so it matters only for a southern route or a probe (navigation).

A long fold also needs a prior position. A station position error δr gives a daily-varying delay error up to δr/c, which smears a long fold: J0437 needs the position to ≲ 4 km, wide-pulse normal pulsars tolerate ~100 km. A fix from scratch starts from the normal pulsars and refines with J0437.

How far navigation gets with all this is known so far only from simulations without timing noise (validation record). From 52° N, B0329+54 alone finds a fixed site to ±30 km per equatorial component in 30 days at nominal flux. In the worst case that is ±110 km. A ship with B0329+54, B0950+08 and a drifting SDR clock stays within 73 km rms of its track over 14 days, with a calibrated start, at nominal flux. At worst-case flux the same ship is at 298 km rms, against 372 km for dead reckoning: from 52° N these two pulsars do not navigate a ship at the design point (navigation). Its clock is the paper's crystal, steadier than a stock SDR oscillator (clock). From 30° S there is only a rough estimate from the scenario numbers. A first fix with four pulsars (J0437, Vela, B0950+08, B1749−28) would be good to about 300 km in 3-D. It is limited by the normal pulsars' TOAs of 0.3–0.7 ms. It needs 184 h on source in the worst case (34 h nominal): with visibility, about 2–3 weeks of calendar time (scenario).

Finally, the profile is stable: there is no pulse-to-pulse jitter, no profile evolution with frequency, no polarization, and no mode changes (B0329+54 is in an abnormal mode about 15 % of the time). The fast simulator and the TOA code do take multi-component profiles. The voltage-chain generator and expectedPowerModel are still single-Gaussian.

Estimation and detection

Two limits of the estimator are known and harmless here. FFTFIT is not optimal under self-noise (1.5× at +10 dB, 2.4× at +20 dB), which is irrelevant at weak signal. Below SNR ≈ 6 per sub-int TOAs have outliers, and the good-TOA definition removes them.

The error bars at SNR ~7 needed a closer look. The formal σ uses the fitted amplitude. So at low SNR it is too small when noise inflates the amplitude, and too large when noise deflates it. In the scenario runs the pull width was 0.7 below the median fitted SNR, and 1.1–1.4 above it. Overall the error bars are honest for single Gaussians (B0329+54 1.04, J0437 0.87–0.94). With a five-component template there was a fat tail: 1.18, robust 1.01, and one TOA at 6.9σ.

Two separate effects showed up with fixed sub-ints at SNR 5–40. (1) The five-component template makes the error bars too small at low SNR, whatever the grouping: pull width 1.82 at SNR 5, 1.19 at 7, 1.09 at 10, 1.03 at 20. Single Gaussians are honest from SNR 7 (0.97–1.10). (2) J0437's narrow pulls were a statistical fluctuation, not an effect. Its two scenario runs gave 0.94 and 0.87, and the robust width of ~110 TOAs scatters by ±0.16. Adaptive and fixed groups on the same simulated data both give ~1.1 ± 0.07.

The cause of (1) is mostly bin resolution. B0329+54's narrowest component (FWHM 0.0055 turns) is only 5.6 bins wide at NBin 1024. That was within estimateTOA's rule then (bins ≤ 1/5 of the width at half maximum), but at low SNR it costs more than the rule assumed. With NBin 2048 (11 bins), the pull width at SNR 7 drops from 1.18 to 1.08 ± 0.03: the ~5–10 % that single Gaussians show at SNR 7 too. The fix is NBin 2048 for B0329+54, so that the narrowest component is ≥ ~10 bins wide, and a warning now catches such cases: bins must be ≤ 1/10 of the spectral template width. That width is 0.0077 turns for B0329+54, so NBin 1024 warns, at 1/7.9 (see TOA).

What is left are rare outliers at SNR ~7. With adaptive groups, about 0.5 % of B0329+54's TOAs land one core width off, 5–9 times their error bar. χ² cannot flag them. The cause is the estimator's threshold effect: the narrow core sits on a broad component, and noise now and then moves the best match onto that shoulder (scenario). At SNR 10 there were none in 600 TOAs. Over 100 days the tail is larger: 1.9 % of B0329+54's TOAs lie beyond 3σ, against 0.27 % for a Gaussian, while the core stays honest (scenario).

These outliers are handled in navigation, not in the grouping. The groups stay at SNR 7, for the larger number of TOAs. The navigation fit uses redundancy: its error model is a mixture of the stated Gaussian and a 4× wider one that takes 2 % of the TOAs (navFit and navFilter, 'OutlierFraction' 0.02, 'OutlierScale' 4). An outlier then counts about 16× less, instead of pulling the fit. navFit iterates the weights; navFilter weighs each TOA once, against the prediction from the TOAs before it. For B0329+54 an outlier stands 4.8–8.6σ from the prediction. Fixed groups are not used for multi-component pulsars. See navigation.

Three smaller limits remain. PFA is validated down to 10⁻²…10⁻³; smaller values (10⁻⁶ for navigation) rely on extrapolating the Gaussian and Rice tails. The covariance between consecutive sub-ints is not stored (~1–4 % of the variance of 2–3 off-pulse bins at the boundary; negligible). And with blanking, V and X use white input (error bars only, within 1–2 % in heavily blanked bins). Only the first 7 lags of X are stored; near a blank 0.4–0.7 % of a bin's variance (up to ~1.6 %) lies at longer lags (error bars only).

RFI excision

The full list is on RFI excision: limits. In brief: blanking truncates the data slightly (−0.4 % pulse amplitude at −5 dB); bright pulsars (above −5 dB, or noise-free) blank themselves, and main.m warns; an exactly locked radar leaves a bias of 0.41 µs at −54 dB, with the frequency guard and the periodic mask; the periodic mask handles fixed PRFs only, not staggered ones yet; carrier and GNSS channels have high baselines; windows are at most 16 samples; noise-like RFI cannot be detected; and the 0.1 µs radar rise time is an assumption. Two of these limits need more space here.

Flat spectrum within a channel

detectRFI's thresholds and the fold's noise model (V, X) assume that each channel's noise spectrum is flat: the channelizer's. A channel only partly covered by strong wideband RFI, such as the band edge of an LTE carrier, has a lopsided spectrum. Its samples stay correlated over microseconds, so a window holds fewer independent samples than assumed. At LTE 0 dB this gave false flags in the two edge channels (540 and 367 windows, instead of about 3). And the detected-power noise was above the model: reduced χ² 1.04, so the TOA error bars were slightly too small.

How much this matters was measured in the realistic scenario (results). The TOA fit weights a channel by 1/level², and these channels contain the RFI. So χ² overstates the effect: the error bars are 0.08 % too small at LTE 0 dB, and 0.39 % in the realistic scenario. The worst case is a narrow noise-like carrier about as strong as its channel's noise. With ten such Inmarsat-like carriers, χ² is 1.22 and the error bars are 3.4 % too small.

Two remedies exist. Per-channel noise calibration is a switch (noiseCal, off by default): the observation's own off-pulse noise per channel replaces the flat-channel model. Synthetic checks pass. On the voltage chain χ² goes from 1.24 to 0.99 for the narrow carriers, and from 1.07 to 1.00 for the realistic scenario. Per-channel detectRFI thresholds from each channel's own correlation remove the false flags (excision). With them, testRFIChain's realistic and narrow-carrier cases pass on the voltage chain (Jasper's run). Only runRFITest's cases 14–15 have not been rerun with the new thresholds. Flagging such edge channels whole remains an option.

Periodic mask: false emitters when detections are dense

periodicRFI looks for a fixed rhythm in the detections. It assumes that the other detections are few and randomly timed. In the realistic scenario, the lopsided channels give hundreds of false flags each. In that dense cloud it accepted rhythms that are not there: 14.1 kHz, every second pulse of a real 973 Hz radar plus random flags; and 14.5 kHz, because detectRFI's window steps put all detection times on a 4-sample grid, which the chance calculation ignores.

It then blanked every predicted "pulse": every ~70 µs, across ~100 channels. That is 29–32 % of the data, and costs 15 % SNR. Nothing checks physical plausibility: only about 5 % of these "radars'" predicted pulses were detected, against ≥ 40 % for real ones.

The periodic mask is therefore off by default; it is only needed for radars locked to the pulsar. A fix is designed but not implemented: a chance calculation that knows the grid, a minimum fraction of predicted pulses that must be detected, and trial periods from strong pulses only (block-B handover 2026-10-09_handover-B-closed.md).

Edges and scale

The last limits concern the edges of a run and its scale. The last sweep of each file (31.5 ms at DM 25; 6.3 ms at DM 5) is not fully supported, and partial first and last turns are skipped (MinCoverage = 1). High DM is out of reach in the generator: forward dispersion needs about 47 GB from DM ~54 up to DM ~106, so at both DM 60 and DM 100. The machine has 24 GB. The channelized front end solves the processing side; the generator side is open (roadmap). The disk use per second of simulated data is ~61 GB on the full band, ~63.5 GB on the channel path, and ~72.5 GB with excision (blanked copies and the weight file), so hours of data use the fast simulator.

Excision does not yet stream on long files. The front end streams, but three excision functions do not: detectRFI reads each channel whole, periodicRFI takes all events at once, and blankingWeights processes each blanked channel whole (its weight array is 4.8 GB at L = 1 s, above its 4 GB default). That is fine for 0.1 s simulations and for the fast simulator, but real data will need it. A design exists (time blocks, with the filter reach as overlap); it is not built.
