Limitations and modelling gaps

What the simulation leaves out, what the processing assumes without checking, and how big each effect is. Each item says whether it matters for the weak-signal target (−54 dB, J0437 on a 0.61 m² array).

In short

Over the 0.1–1 s it simulates, the pipeline is exact for what it models. What it leaves out matters on longer timescales. Four effects are larger than a J0437 TOA and must be handled before folding for hours: Earth's motion, the pulsar's binary orbit, the clock and the station position (block D). Others only matter for nanosecond navigation: the ionosphere, DM changes, scattering at high DM. The receiver model is ideal, and two excision items are parked with designs.

How big each effect is

Processing assumptions not checked

  • DM correctness: a wrong DM leaves a residual sweep. A DM check from sub-band TOAs is planned (block 2).
  • Reference-frequency convention vs the TOA definition: a constant offset if the forward and inverse references differ.
  • Receiver frequency mapping: no spectral inversion assumed (fRF = fLO + fbb); the off-centre LO makes a mirror error visible in simulation, but real hardware must be checked.
  • True sample rate: trusted via the info chain.

How scintillation would be modelled

Within a ≤ 1 s voltage simulation, diffractive scintillation is static: a random frequency gain g(f) with correlation bandwidth Δνd multiplied into the forward filter, plus optionally a multipath tail. Long-term fading, Doppler and DM(t) belong at the TOA / SNR level of a later mission simulation. With a scintillated spectrum, optimal detection weights channels by pulsar S/N (ChannelGain in the optimal fit).

Signal and receiver

  • Realistic SNR regime. −5 dB is a bright pulsar on a large dish. A small antenna (SEFD ~10⁵ Jy) gives ρ ≈ −27 dB (Vela) to ≲ −50 dB (typical millisecond pulsars): per-pulse SNR ≪ 1, TOAs need minutes to hours of folding, where motion, DM(t) and scintillation matter.
  • Spectrum: the generator is white; real pulsars have S ∝ fα, α ≈ −1.6 (factor 1.6 across 1.2–1.6 GHz). Equal-weight loss ~1 %; with profile evolution and a DM error it biases wideband TOAs.
  • Receiver: ideal linear filters, infinite dynamic range. No amplifier compression, ADC quantization or clipping (2-bit costs ≈ 12 % SNR), intermodulation, bandpass ripple, gain drift. Single polarization (dual pol gives √2 in SNR; the hardware is dual pol). Ideal I/Q (a digital downconverter): no I/Q imbalance (a mirror image, −25…−40 dB before correction), no DC offset or LO leakage, no LO phase noise. Our LO (1.3 GHz) lies inside the band, so a DC spike and imbalance ghosts (RFI at 1.35 GHz → ghost at 1.25 GHz) would land in it; real hardware needs a low-IF LO or imbalance calibration.
  • Receiver noise does not pass the dispersion taper in the simulation: only the pulsar is tapered at the band edges. On the full band this costs +0.4 % in σTOA, but +2.5 % on the default channel path. There the pulsar sees the band-edge taper and every channel's edges, while the noise sees only the channel edges. So the six edge channels hold 2–96 % of the pulse, although the optimal fit assumes equal pulse strength (ChannelGain 1).
    This is the whole 2.2 % gap between the channel path's and the full band's error bars: 2.943 against 2.880 µs (predicted +2.13 %, measured +2.19 %). With a realistic receiver, which applies the bandpass to pulsar and noise alike, the channel path would cost 0.37 % (logs/2026-10-13_deep-analysis.md, section 2).
  • Real bandpass is not ideal and not known exactly: measure W² from the off-pulse spectrum and use it in (∫W²)²/∫W⁴; can be 10–30 % on real data.

Timing and pulse

  • Clock errors: a model clock, not the real one. A clock rate off by 10⁻⁹ accumulates 72 µs over 20 h, about 6× the J0437 TOA precision. The simulator's truth therefore has a clock process with offset, rate and noise (the receiver's clock). The observer folds with its own clock estimate, and the navigation state holds the clock's offset and rate (navigation). The noise is that of the paper's crystal. The real SDR's oscillator is not characterised yet; its noise is best measured on the device. The ADC's sampling jitter is left out: 1 ps adds only 10⁻⁴ of the input power as noise.
  • Relative motion: an analytic Earth orbit. Earth's orbital speed changes the pulse rate by up to 10⁻⁴: at 100 Hz, about 100 µs of phase drift per second. The phase predictor follows that drift, and the fold uses it (phase prediction). The fast simulator puts every pulse where a true trajectory puts it (the simulator's truth). Both use the same analytic Earth orbit, so its error cancels in the simulations. Real data need JPL's DE440 ephemeris.
  • No binary motion of the pulsar (found 13 Oct, deep analysis section 14). The reference pulsar J0437−4715 is in a 5.74-day orbit, with a projected size of 3.37 light-seconds. Its orbit moves the arrival time by about 3 s within one 20 h fold (0.15 s per hour). That is about 2·10⁵× the fold's phase budget (W50/10 ≈ 14 µs). J2145−0750 and J1713+0747 are binaries too. So the phase predictor (D) needs a binary timing model; the circular-orbit model ELL1 suffices for J0437 at the µs level. End-to-end tests of D also need binary motion in the simulator.
  • Position prior for folding. A station position error δr gives a daily-varying delay error up to δr/c, which smears a long fold: J0437 needs the position to ≲ 4 km, wide-pulse normal pulsars tolerate ~100 km. A fix from scratch starts from the normal pulsars and refines with J0437.
  • Navigation expectations (rough, from the scenario numbers, without timing noise). From 30° S, a first fix with four pulsars (J0437, Vela, B0950+08, B1749−28) is good to about 300 km in 3-D. It is limited by the normal pulsars' TOAs of 0.3–0.7 ms. It needs 184 h on source in the worst case (34 h nominal): with visibility, about 2–3 weeks of calendar time. From 52° N, with no millisecond pulsar, a fix is about 1500 km, after weeks to months. See scenario.
  • Single pulsar.
  • Stable profile: no pulse-to-pulse jitter, no profile evolution with frequency, no polarization, and no mode changes (B0329+54 is in an abnormal mode about 15 % of the time).
    Since 20 Oct the fast simulator and the TOA code take multi-component profiles. For B0329+54 these are five Gaussians at 1410 MHz; a single Gaussian with the catalogue W50 overstated its peak by 3 dB, and its TOA budget by 3×. J0437's wings (W10 1.02 ms against W50 0.14 ms) are modelled in the fast simulator since 21 Oct, with an eight-component fit to the measured profile (scenario). The voltage-chain generator and expectedPowerModel are still single-Gaussian.

Estimation and detection

  • FFTFIT is not optimal under self-noise (1.5× at +10 dB, 2.4× at +20 dB); irrelevant at weak signal.
  • Below SNR ≈ 6 per sub-int TOAs have outliers; the good-TOA definition removes them.
  • Error bars at SNR ~7 (found 20 Oct).
    • The effect. The formal σ uses the fitted amplitude. So at low SNR it is too small when noise inflates the amplitude, and too large when noise deflates it. In the scenario runs the pull width was 0.7 below the median fitted SNR, and 1.1–1.4 above it.
    • Overall the error bars are honest for single Gaussians (B0329+54 1.04, J0437 0.87–0.94). With a five-component template there was a fat tail: 1.18, robust 1.01, and one TOA at 6.9σ.
    • Two separate effects showed up with fixed sub-ints at SNR 5–40. (1) The five-component template makes the error bars too small at low SNR, whatever the grouping: pull width 1.82 at SNR 5, 1.19 at 7, 1.09 at 10, 1.03 at 20. Single Gaussians are honest from SNR 7 (0.97–1.10). (2) J0437's narrow pulls were a statistical fluctuation, not an effect. Its two scenario runs gave 0.94 and 0.87, and the robust width of ~110 TOAs scatters by ±0.16. Adaptive and fixed groups on the same simulated data both give ~1.1 ± 0.07.
    • The cause of (1) is mostly bin resolution. B0329+54's narrowest component (FWHM 0.0055 turns) is only 5.6 bins wide at NBin 1024. That was within estimateTOA's rule then (bins ≤ 1/5 of the width at half maximum), but at low SNR it costs more than the rule assumed. With NBin 2048 (11 bins), the pull width at SNR 7 drops from 1.18 to 1.08 ± 0.03: the ~5–10 % that single Gaussians show at SNR 7 too.
    • The fix: NBin 2048 for B0329+54, so that the narrowest component is ≥ ~10 bins wide. Since 20 Oct the warning catches such cases: bins must be ≤ 1/10 of the spectral template width. That width is 0.0077 turns for B0329+54, so NBin 1024 warns, at 1/7.9 (see TOA).
    • What is left: rare outliers at SNR ~7. With adaptive groups, about 0.5 % of B0329+54's TOAs land one core width off, 5–9 times their error bar. χ² cannot flag them. The cause is the estimator's threshold effect: the narrow core sits on a broad component, and noise now and then moves the best match onto that shoulder (scenario). At SNR 10 there were none in 600 TOAs.
    • How they will be handled (agreed 21 Oct). The groups stay at SNR 7, for the larger number of TOAs. The navigation fit uses redundancy: it checks each TOA against the prediction from the others and from the ship's dead reckoning, and its error model has a small outlier part. For B0329+54 an outlier then stands 4.8–8.6σ from the prediction. Fixed groups are not used for multi-component pulsars. See navigation.
  • PFA validated down to 10⁻²…10⁻³; smaller values (10⁻⁶ for navigation) by extrapolating Gaussian / Rice tails.
  • Covariance between consecutive sub-ints not stored (~1–4 % of the variance of 2–3 off-pulse bins at the boundary; negligible).
  • Blanking: V and X use white input (error bars only, within 1–2 % in heavily blanked bins). Only the first 7 lags of X are stored; near a blank 0.4–0.7 % of a bin's variance (up to ~1.6 %) lies at longer lags (error bars only).

RFI excision

The full list is on RFI excision: limits. In brief:

  • data-dependent truncation: −0.4 % pulse amplitude at −5 dB;
  • bright pulsars (above −5 dB, or noise-free) blank themselves; main.m warns;
  • an exactly locked radar leaves a bias of 0.41 µs at −54 dB, with the frequency guard and the periodic mask;
  • the periodic mask handles fixed PRFs only, not staggered ones yet;
  • carrier and GNSS channels have high baselines;
  • windows are at most 16 samples;
  • noise-like RFI cannot be detected;
  • the 0.1 µs radar rise time is an assumption.
Flat spectrum within a channel (found 8 Oct)

The problem. detectRFI's thresholds and the fold's noise model (V, X) assume that each channel's noise spectrum is flat: the channelizer's. A channel only partly covered by strong wideband RFI, such as the band edge of an LTE carrier, has a lopsided spectrum. Its samples stay correlated over microseconds, so a window holds fewer independent samples than assumed. At LTE 0 dB this gave false flags in the two edge channels (540 and 367 windows, instead of about 3). And the detected-power noise was above the model: reduced χ² 1.04, so the TOA error bars were slightly too small.

How much it matters (measured 9 Oct in the realistic scenario, results). The TOA fit weights a channel by 1/level², and these channels contain the RFI. So χ² overstates the effect: the error bars are 0.08 % too small at LTE 0 dB, and 0.39 % in the realistic scenario. The worst case is a narrow noise-like carrier about as strong as its channel's noise. With ten such Inmarsat-like carriers, χ² is 1.22 and the error bars are 3.4 % too small.

What is done. Per-channel noise calibration exists since 20 Oct, as a switch (noiseCal, off by default): the observation's own off-pulse noise per channel replaces the flat-channel model. Synthetic checks pass. On the voltage chain χ² goes from 1.24 to 0.99 for the narrow carriers, and from 1.07 to 1.00 for the realistic scenario. Per-channel detectRFI thresholds from each channel's own correlation exist since 21 Oct, against the false flags (excision); the realistic scenario has not been rerun with them yet. Flagging such edge channels whole remains an option.

Periodic mask: false emitters when detections are dense (found 9 Oct, realistic scenario)

The problem. periodicRFI looks for a fixed rhythm in the detections. It assumes that the other detections are few and randomly timed. In the realistic scenario, the lopsided channels give hundreds of false flags each. In that dense cloud it accepted rhythms that are not there:

  • 14.1 kHz: every second pulse of a real 973 Hz radar, plus random flags;
  • 14.5 kHz: detectRFI's window steps put all detection times on a 4-sample grid, which the chance calculation ignores.

It then blanked every predicted "pulse": every ~70 µs, across ~100 channels. That is 29–32 % of the data, and costs 15 % SNR. Nothing checks physical plausibility: only about 5 % of these "radars'" predicted pulses were detected, against ≥ 40 % for real ones.

What is done. The periodic mask is off by default since 9 Oct; it is only needed for radars locked to the pulsar. A fix is designed but not implemented: a chance calculation that knows the grid, a minimum fraction of predicted pulses that must be detected, and trial periods from strong pulses only (block-B handover 2026-10-09_handover-B-closed.md).

Edges and scale

  • The last sweep of each file (31.5 ms at DM 25; 6.3 ms at DM 5) is not fully supported; partial first and last turns are skipped (MinCoverage = 1).
  • High DM in the generator: forward dispersion needs about 47 GB from DM ~54 up to DM ~106, so at both DM 60 and DM 100. The machine has 24 GB. The channelized front end solves the processing side; the generator side is open (roadmap).
  • Disk per second of simulated data: ~61 GB on the full band, ~63 GB on the channel path, ~72 GB with excision (blanked copies and the weight file). Hours of data need the fast simulator (block C).
  • Excision on long files (12 Oct). The front end streams, but three excision functions do not: detectRFI reads each channel whole, periodicRFI takes all events at once, and blankingWeights processes each blanked channel whole (its weight array is 4.8 GB at L = 1 s, above its 4 GB default). That is fine for 0.1 s simulations and for the fast simulator, but real data will need it. A design exists (time blocks, with the filter reach as overlap); it is not built.
