Localisation: frames, time and relativity

A pulse's arrival time becomes a position only after every other reason for it to be early or late has been taken out. This page explains how a position follows from arrival times, which coordinate frames and time scales the code uses, and which geometric and relativistic delays sit between the pulsar and the receiver. It ends with what the model still leaves out, and what each omission would cost against real data.

Code: makeTrajectory (frames), delayModel (delays and time scales), makePredictor (phase), trueResiduals and makeClock (the simulator's truth) · details per function on the pages phase prediction, fast simulator and receiver clock

In short

A pulsar's phase is defined at the barycentre of the solar system, in its own time scale. The receiver sits on a ship on a rotating Earth that orbits the Sun, and it time-stamps the data with its own oscillator. The code therefore carries every arrival time through a chain: from the clock reading to true time, from the Earth's clock to the barycentre's clock, and from the receiver's position to the barycentre. What is left after that chain is the position error, seen along the pulsar's direction.

One microsecond of residual is 300 m along the line of sight. The delays that are removed are up to ±412 s over a year for B0329+54, several hundred million times larger.

1. From arrival times to a position

A pulse front is a plane, because the pulsar is so far away. A receiver that is closer to the pulsar along its direction meets the front earlier. A sideways offset changes nothing. Each arrival time therefore measures one component of the position: the one along the pulsar's direction n̂.

The residual of a TOA is the measured arrival time minus the predicted one. For a receiver that is δr away from where the observer thinks it is, and a clock that is off by δb, the residual is

\[ r = -\frac{\delta\mathbf{r}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}}{c} + \delta b + \text{noise}. \]

A true receiver closer to the pulsar gives a negative residual: the pulse arrives early. One microsecond is 300 m.

One pulsar on a rotating Earth

On the Earth, the pulsar's direction turns once per sidereal day. A fixed position error therefore shows up as a daily sine. Its amplitude is the offset's equatorial part times cos δ, where δ is the pulsar's declination. For B0329+54, with cos δ = 0.58, a site 100 km east of the assumed one gives a daily sine of 193.33 µs.

The offset's component along the Earth's axis does not rotate. It gives a constant, which cannot be told apart from the clock offset. So one pulsar finds the two equatorial components of a fixed site, and the third merges with the clock.

Several pulsars, and a moving ship

Each pulsar measures the position along its own direction. With pulsars in well-separated directions the three components and the clock offset are four unknowns, so four TOAs at one moment fix them. A moving ship adds its velocity, and the clock adds its rate. These unknowns change in time, so a Kalman filter estimates them together, with a motion model per vehicle.

How precise, and the half-turn limit

One TOA with an uncertainty σ locates the receiver to c·σ along the pulsar's direction. B0329+54's TOAs have about 734 µs, which is 220 km per TOA. After 30 days of TOAs, each equatorial component of a fixed site should be known to about ±30 km. J0437's TOAs have about 13 µs, which is 3.9 km per TOA. J0437 is too far south to be seen from the Netherlands, though.

The fold cannot tell a pulse that is f·r turns late from one that is a whole turn later. A TOA is therefore unambiguous only while the position error stays below half a period times c.

PulsarPeriodPosition error that stays unambiguous
B0329+54714 ms±107,000 km
J0437−47155.76 ms±863 km

A millisecond pulsar therefore needs a prior position, for example from dead reckoning, that is good to a few hundred km.

2. Coordinate frames

Positions are needed in several frames: the pulsar's direction is fixed on the sky, the Earth's orbit is given in the ecliptic, and a ship's position is given in latitude and longitude on a rotating Earth. The code converts all of them into one frame before any delay is computed.

FrameOrigin and axesWhat uses it
Equatorial, J2000 (ICRS)The barycentre; the axes are fixed on the sky, with z along the Earth's axis of 2000.The pulsar's direction (right ascension, declination); all delays; probes ('SSB' trajectories).
Ecliptic, J2000As above, tilted by the obliquity 23.439281° about the x axis.The orbital elements of the Earth–Moon barycentre, Jupiter and Saturn; turned into equatorial at once.
Earth-fixed (ECEF)The geocentre; the axes turn with the Earth.Sites and ships ('ECEF' trajectories).
Geodetic (WGS84)Latitude, longitude and height on the WGS84 ellipsoid.The inputs of 'fixed' and 'track' trajectories; converted to Earth-fixed.
Local east-north-upA site; the axes point east, north and up.Offsets of a site ('OffsetENU'), such as the true site 100 km east of the assumed one.

An Earth-fixed position becomes equatorial through the Earth's rotation: a turn about the z axis by the Earth rotation angle. The site's velocity gets the rotation's 465 m/s at the equator added. The geocentre's own position comes from the position chain in the figure above.

Every trajectory in one of two frames

A trajectory is either Earth-fixed or barycentric. A ship and a ground site are naturally Earth-fixed: their tracks come as latitude and longitude. A deep-space probe is naturally barycentric. The delay model turns both into the barycentric frame itself, so the predictor and a later Kalman filter never need to know which kind of vehicle they handle (phase prediction).

3. Time scales and the receiver's clock

Several clocks are involved, and they disagree by up to minutes. The code counts every time in seconds since J2000.0, which is 2000-01-01 12:00 in terrestrial time.

Time scaleWhat it isRelation
The receiver's clockThe reading of the SDR's own oscillator. It also times the samples.Clock reading − true time = ε(t): an offset, a rate (up to 2·10⁻⁶ for a ±2 ppm oscillator) and noise.
TAIInternational atomic time.The reference for the atomic clocks.
TTTerrestrial time: an ideal clock on the rotating geoid.TT = TAI + 32.184 s, exactly.
UTCCivil time, kept within 0.9 s of the Earth's rotation by leap seconds.TAI − UTC = 37 s since 2017.
UT1The Earth's rotation itself, as an angle.The code takes UT1 = TT − 69.184 s, that is UT1 = UTC.
TDBBarycentric dynamical time: the time of the solar-system ephemerides and of pulsar timing models.TDB − TT = the Einstein term, ±1.66 ms over a year.
TCBBarycentric coordinate time, the relativistically "pure" version.Runs faster than TDB by 1.55·10⁻⁸: 0.49 s per year.

The observer never knows true time directly. It corrects its clock readings with its own estimate of the clock error, and the predictor uses the corrected time (phase prediction). In the simulator the true clock comes from makeClock, and only the part of it that the observer has not estimated moves the pulse.

The ephemeris must match the time scale

Published timing models come in TDB or in TCB units. Their spin frequencies differ by 1.55·10⁻⁸ of the value. Using a TCB model as if it were TDB makes the phase drift by 1.3 ms per day for any pulsar. The code's delays end in TDB, so a timing model must be in TDB units, or be converted first.

4. The delays, term by term

The delay Δ is the time to add to a pulse's arrival at the receiver to get its arrival at the barycentre. delayModel computes it as a sum of terms. The table gives each term's size for B0329+54 seen from 52° N.

The formulas

With r the receiver's position relative to the barycentre and D the pulsar's distance:

\[ \Delta_\mathrm{R} = \frac{\mathbf{r}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}}{c}, \qquad \Delta_\mathrm{curv} = -\frac{|\mathbf{r}|^2 - (\mathbf{r}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})^2}{2cD}, \]

\[ \Delta_\mathrm{E} = 1.657\,\mathrm{ms}\,\sin g + 14\,\mu\mathrm{s}\,\sin 2g + \frac{\mathbf{v}_\oplus\cdot\mathbf{r}_\mathrm{site}}{c^2}, \qquad \Delta_\mathrm{S} = 2T_\odot \ln\frac{|\mathbf{r}_\odot| + \mathbf{r}_\odot\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}}{1\,\mathrm{AU}}, \]

with g the Earth's mean anomaly, r⊙ the receiver relative to the Sun, and T⊙ = GM⊙/c³ = 4.925 µs.

With DE440 the geocentric part of ΔE is integrated instead of taken from the two-term series:

\[ \frac{d(\mathrm{TDB}-\mathrm{TT})}{dt} = \frac{v_\oplus^2/2 + U}{c^2} - L_C, \qquad U = \sum_i \frac{GM_i}{|\mathbf r_\oplus - \mathbf r_i|}, \]

with the sum over the Sun, the Moon and the planets, and LC = 1.48082686741·10⁻⁸ the IAU's mean rate, which keeps TDB in pace with TT on average.

5. Relativistic corrections

Two effects of general relativity are large enough to matter, and a third concerns which clock is meant.

The Einstein delay: the receiver's clock runs at a varying rate

A clock on the Earth moves at 30 km/s through the Sun's gravitational field. Both the speed and the field strength change along the slightly eccentric orbit. The clock therefore runs a little slow near perihelion, where it is faster and deeper in the field, and a little fast near aphelion, compared with the uniform barycentric time TDB. Accumulated over a year this is ±1.66 ms. The site adds a smaller daily term, because it moves with the Earth's rotation: up to about 2 µs. Converting the Earth's time TT into TDB removes these variations, and that conversion is the Einstein term.

The analytic model keeps the two largest terms of the conversion: 1.657 ms over a year and 14 µs over half a year. The Moon and the planets add many smaller terms, up to about 60 µs together. With JPL's DE440 the code instead integrates the clock's rate along the Earth's actual path through the solar system. That matches the published series to about 2 µs.

The Shapiro delay: gravity slows the pulse

A pulse that passes the Sun travels through curved space-time and arrives later. The delay grows as the line of sight passes closer to the Sun. For B0329+54 it varies between −17 and +6 µs over a year. For a pulsar near the ecliptic, the line of sight can graze the Sun, and the delay reaches about −110 µs. Jupiter adds up to about 180 ns, but only when the line of sight passes close to it.

Whose clock: TT, TDB and a probe's own time

The Einstein term converts terrestrial time into barycentric time. That is right for a receiver on the Earth. A deep-space probe's clock follows neither: its proper time runs at a rate set by its own speed and its distance from the Sun. TDB's rate already contains the Earth's orbital speed and the Sun's pull at 1 AU, so a probe near the Earth's orbit runs within about 10⁻⁹ of TDB. A probe at Jupiter's distance feels less of both, and runs 1.3·10⁻⁸ fast: 1.1 ms per day. Most of that is a constant rate, which the clock estimate absorbs. The code does not yet model a probe's own clock; it treats a probe's time stamps as TDB.

Height

A clock higher up runs faster: 1.1·10⁻¹³ per km of height. At sea level this is part of TT's definition. On an aircraft at 10 km it is 95 ns per day, again a rate that the clock estimate absorbs.

The simulator only needs the model to be consistent

In the simulator, the truth and the observer's prediction use the same delay model. Its own errors therefore cancel in the residual, and only the difference between the true and the assumed trajectory and clock remains. That is why an analytic Earth orbit was enough to start with. Real data are different: there every omission below shows up in the residual (phase prediction).
