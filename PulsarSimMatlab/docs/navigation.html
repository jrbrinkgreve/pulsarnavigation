Stage 10 · Navigation: position and clock from the TOAs

Each TOA arrives a little early or late against the phase that stage 7a predicted. The receiver is not quite where it thinks it is, and its clock is not quite right. This stage turns those residuals into the receiver's position and clock.

10.1 The navigation signal

The first goal is a site 100 km from where the observer thinks it is, found from B0329+54 alone. A site offset changes each TOA by the offset projected onto the pulsar direction, and that direction turns once per sidereal day in the Earth-fixed frame. The two equatorial components of the offset therefore show up as a daily sine: 193 µs for 100 km east (Figure 10.1). The component along the Earth's axis gives a constant that cannot be told apart from the clock offset, so it goes into the clock.

A sine can only be fitted if it is sampled at several hour angles, and the array's panel is fixed (scenario, Section R1.1). B0329+54 drifts through a beam once a day and gives one TOA per pass: an SNR of 11.5 and a TOA error of 459 µs, with every one of 120 simulated passes detected. With one beam on the pulsar that is one hour angle a day and no position. With both beams on it, 30 days give ±108 km along one axis and nothing along the other (Section 10.2). So with the fixed panel as it stands, the array detects and times B0329+54 every day but does not yet locate itself. What a tracking mount would give is the what-if that the rest of this page works out: with B0329+54 followed through its pass, 30 days of TOAs give the east component to ±9.4 km and the radial one to ±11.9 km, and the 100 km offset is detected at 7.9σ (Claude's runs, 23 Nov 2026).

What if the panel were on a tracking mount

Except where the fixed panel is named, the fixed-site results of Sections 10.1 to 10.3 are this what-if, with their figures, tables and the planning study: the pulsar is followed through its pass, at the array's design point (an SEFD of 6.44·10⁴ Jy and 150 MHz; scenario, Section R1). They rest on the integration time per TOA, not on the SNR of a single pass (the two figures of merit), and come from the repository's scripts. The fixed panel's numbers are scratch runs. All are Claude's runs, of 23 November 2026. The ship results of Section 10.4 are a dated record at the design points before that date.

One complication is known before any fit: a few TOAs are far off, and they are handled by redundancy, not by fewer TOAs. At SNR 7 a few of B0329+54's TOAs land one core width off: in the followed runs at the design point 1.34 % lie beyond 3σ and 0.14 % beyond 5σ. A higher SNR target would avoid them, but would halve the number of TOAs. The fit instead judges each TOA against the others. For B0329+54 the prediction from a day's other TOAs is good to about 23 km, while an outlier is 1100–1900 km off. The fit's error model therefore has an outlier component, with its rate and size taken from the simulator's tail (Section 10.2). Without it, one outlier among the 87 TOAs of a followed day would shift that day's position by about 15 km.

History

The design of 21 Oct 2026 handled outliers by redundancy. It estimated that a hard 4σ test would reject a good TOA only about once in four to five years. The fit was built with a mixture instead (section 2). The runs of 20–22 Oct 2026, at the design points before 23 November 2026, had about 0.5 % of B0329+54's TOAs 5–9σ off in 30 days and 1.9 % beyond 3σ in 100 days; the mixture's 2 % comes from them.

10.2 navFit: the site from the TOAs validated

fit = navFit(toa, psrs, site, ...)

Full interface and help text of navFit →

navFit finds where a fixed site really is from the residuals of its TOAs. Each residual is the site's offset projected onto the pulsar's direction; the Earth's rotation turns that into a daily sine, and a fit of many TOAs recovers the offset. A few TOAs near the detection threshold are far off; the fit recognises them and lets them count much less.

The model is linear: each residual is the offset times a known direction vector, plus a constant per pulsar. The direction vector is the pulsar's direction turned back by the Earth rotation angle at the TOA's time. One pulsar sees two kinds of component in it. The two components in the equatorial plane rotate with the Earth and give the daily sine; the component along the Earth's axis gives a constant, the same as the clock offset and the pulsar's own offset.

Whether that constant is known decides what the fit can find, and the switch 'Start' sets it. With "self" the constant is unknown and fitted, so the fit gives the two equatorial components. With "calibrated" the observer started from a known position and time, so the constant is known and the fit gives the full 3-D position. The result is given in the site's own directions: east, radial (in the equatorial plane, away from the Earth's axis) and polar; with "calibrated" also east, north and up.

The fit must also survive the far-off TOAs, so its noise model is a mixture. Most TOAs follow their error bar; a fraction ε = 2 % scatter k = 4 times wider. The fit repeats: each TOA gets its probability of belonging to the core, its weight follows from that, and the weighted fit is solved again until it no longer changes. A hard cut, such as dropping every TOA beyond 4σ, does not work: the 100-day runs show a continuum of far-off TOAs between 3σ and 5σ, so a cut either keeps some or throws away good ones.

East lies entirely in the equatorial plane, so an offset to the east is fully visible to one pulsar; north and up each have a part along the Earth's axis. For B0329+54 from 52° N, followed for 30 days at the design point (the what-if; 2634 TOAs of 711 µs), east is known to ±9.4 km and radial to ±11.9 km. A calibrated start gives the polar component to ±5.4 km, because the constant uses every TOA at once.

So far the clock was perfect. A real clock adds its unestimated error to every residual. The crystal's error wanders by about 76 µs a day and 13 ms a month, more than one TOA's σ. TOAs close in time share almost the same clock error, so it is correlated noise, not extra noise per TOA. With the option 'Clock' the fit uses the clock model's covariance between every pair of TOAs, and solves a generalized least-squares problem. A free clock offset and rate for every day would not do: a straight line per day absorbs part of a daily sine, and the daily sine is the position signal itself. The clock model from makeClock says how fast the clock can really wander, so the clock takes only what it can physically do. With "self" the clock's offset goes into the constants and its rate is fitted too; with "calibrated" both were known at the start, and only the wander since then is unknown. Either way the fit also estimates the clock at every TOA and predicts it ahead, with an error bar, which is what the observer feeds back into its predictor. For a fixed site the position hardly suffers, because the clock wanders slowly while the position signal is a daily sine: the planning study gives the same east and radial errors with a crystal clock as with a perfect one, to 0.1–0.3 km (Section 10.3). Without the clock model the same fit flags a third of the TOAs as outliers (test 9).

The formulas

\[ r_i = \mathbf{a}_i\cdot\delta\mathbf{x} + c_k + \text{noise}, \qquad \mathbf{a}_i = -R_z(\theta_i)^\mathsf{T}\hat{\mathbf{n}}_k/c \]

with δx the offset in Earth-fixed coordinates and θ the Earth rotation angle. The noise is (1 − ε)·N(0, σ²) + ε·N(0, (kσ)²). A TOA with normalized residual u gets the core probability γ = (1 − ε)φ(u) / [(1 − ε)φ(u) + (ε/k)φ(u/k)] and the weight γ/σ² + (1 − γ)/(kσ)². Left out: the residual's factor 1/(1 + Doppler), about 10⁻⁴, and the Einstein site term, about 2·10⁻⁴ of the offset. With 'Predict' the function returns only the covariance a TOA schedule would give, to plan which pulsars to observe and for how long.

The clock's covariance from its start, with τ the time since then: Cov = q₁·min + q₂·(m²M/2 − m³/6), where m and M are the smaller and larger of the two τ, q₁ = h₀/2 and q₂ = 2π²h₋₂. Flicker is left out of this model; at daily scales it adds about 3 µs against 76 µs. The clock estimate is the posterior mean K S⁻¹(r − Ap), and its error includes the uncertainty of the fitted parameters (universal kriging).

A TOA is a time-average

The fit's rows also assume that each TOA belongs to one moment, and it does not. A TOA merges sub-integrations until its SNR reaches about 7. At the design point that is about 0.23 hours of data for B0329+54 and 1.3 hours for B0950+08; a weaker pulsar such as B1929+10 needs 11.5 hours, spread over two passes a day apart. The TOA's residual is therefore the average over its sub-integrations, not the value at one time. For short TOAs the difference is small; for long ones it is not. The daily position signal averaged over 10 hours is only 0.74 of its value at the middle of that time. Over two passes a day apart at the same hour angle it keeps its full size, but the middle of that time span lies near the opposite hour angle, where the signal has the opposite sign (Figure 10.2, drawn for TOAs of 10 hours and of 5 + 5 hours).

Each TOA can therefore carry the times of its sub-integrations (toa.tSub), and the fit averages the TOA's row over them, just as the data are averaged. Test 11 shows the difference: with the rows averaged, a 1000 km offset comes out unbiased; with one time per TOA it comes out at 0.84 of the truth. No single tag time can replace this: even the time whose hour angle best represents the data damps the daily signal, by 0.74 for 10 hours of data, while averaging the row is exact and costs nothing. The run scripts still tag each TOA at its data's mean hour angle, for the filter's time order and for plots.

The fixed panel: one hour angle a day Claude's run

The array's panel is fixed, so B0329+54 gives one TOA per pass, at one hour angle per beam. With one beam on the pulsar the fit is singular: 30 days of passes give no position, because the site's delay at that one hour angle is a constant, and it merges with the pulsar's own phase constant. With both beams on the pulsar, the second 2.23 h after culmination, 30 days give ±108 km along one axis (0.958 east, 0.287 radial); the other axis stays undetermined. These are scratch runs with the true site 100 km east, and they rest on the SNR of a single pass, 11.5 (scenario, Section R7.4). A position therefore needs the pulsar at several hour angles. The rest of Section 10.2 is the what-if in which a tracking mount provides them.

10.3 Extra pulsars: synchronising the clock

The clock error is the same for every pulsar, while a position error projects differently onto each pulsar's direction. Differences between pulsars therefore cancel the clock, as differences between satellites do in GNSS. Once the position is known, the common part of all residuals is the clock error itself: the pulsars discipline the receiver's clock.

For a fixed site and B0329+54 alone, followed through its pass, the clock barely matters. The crystal's wander adds about 0.9 µs to the 30-day estimate of the daily sine, against 18 µs from the TOA noise, because the Earth's rotation puts the position signal at a period of one day while the clock wanders slowly. The clock does matter in two places. The component along the Earth's axis and the absolute clock are both constants, so one pulsar cannot separate them. And on a moving ship the dead-reckoning errors drift slowly too, just like the clock. Extra pulsars in other directions solve both.

The planning study below is part of the what-if. It assumes that every pulsar is followed whenever it is up, each in its own beam, so that extra pulsars cost no time on B0329+54. The hardware does not do that: it has two fixed analogue beams, and a pulsar is seen only while it is inside one of them (scenario, Section R1.1). The study therefore shows what extra pulsars could add, not what this array gives. From 52° N the extra pulsars are weak. The scenario table gives their TOAs at SNR 7 at the array's design point (B1749−28 never rises above 20° there):

The crystal wanders less than 1 µs within an hour, so TOAs of different pulsars taken within hours share the same clock. Each pulsar also has a constant offset between our profile's reference point and its timing model. A switch chooses how it is handled: either the observer starts from a calibrated position and time, for example in port, or it estimates the offsets itself. Without calibration the polar component of a fixed site stays unobservable.

10.4 navFilter: a moving receiver validated

fit = navFilter(toa, psrs, trajDR, ...)
Archived, and a dated record

The work now aims at a static position, so on 5 Nov 2026 navFilter, runShip and testNavFilter moved to old/moving_platform/. This section describes them as they stood. They return if a moving platform is developed later. Every ship result below is from 22 Oct 2026, for the receiver planned until 23 November 2026 (0.61 m², 400 MHz, two pulsars followed in simultaneous digital beams): "nominal flux" is an SEFD of 3.2·10⁵ Jy and "worst-case flux" 7.4·10⁵ Jy. The ship was not rerun at the array's design point.

A fixed site is the simple case, because the position does not change. On a ship it does, and the observer knows its track only from dead reckoning: speed log and compass. The dead-reckoning error grows; an unknown current of 0.3 m/s is 26 km a day. navFilter estimates that error over time from the TOA residuals, together with the clock. It is a Kalman filter around the dead-reckoning track, followed by a smoother that uses all TOAs for every moment.

  1. The state: the position error and the velocity error in the ship's own east and north directions; the clock's offset and rate; with "self" one constant per pulsar. With "calibrated" the constants are known, and the clock starts from the port calibration, with that calibration's uncertainty ('ClockPrior'). Everything is in metres, the clock as the speed of light times its offset, as in GNSS receivers.
  2. Between TOAs: the position error grows with the velocity error. The velocity error is an unknown current that changes slowly (a random walk). The clock follows the two-state model of makeClock.
  3. At each TOA: the residual is the position error projected onto the pulsar's direction, plus the clock and the pulsar's constant. That is linear in the state, so an ordinary Kalman filter suffices.
  4. Afterwards: a smoother runs back through the TOAs, so that every moment gets the best estimate from all of them.

A ship stays at sea level, so the filter has no up component. That lets one pulsar find north as well as east: the equatorial component that rotates with the Earth then fixes north. For a fixed site the filter and navFit are the same estimator: test 1 shows they agree to 10⁻⁴ of the errors. The filter adds what the batch fit cannot do: a state that changes in time, and an estimate at any moment that can be fed back to the predictor.

The errors are kept in the ship's own frame, because a current pushes the ship along its own east and north, and those directions turn as the ship moves. Over 35° of longitude a fixed Earth-fixed east vector gets a 37 % vertical part. The first version kept the errors in Earth-fixed coordinates: the height constraint then forbade the east error, and the filter was 30 times overconfident in east. In the ship's own frame a constant current is a constant state. The port clock enters with its uncertainty, because a port calibration is never exact: an offset error of 1 ms is 300 km in the filter's units, more than one TOA's error. Test 6 shows what an exact prior does: the clock's error bars become 2.6 times too small. The position hardly suffers, because the clock's own wander takes up the offset within a day.

The equations

State x = [dE, dN, vE, vN, b, ḃ, Jk]. Over Δ: d += Δv; v a random walk of spectral density qv (default 1.2·10⁻⁷ m²/s³, about 0.1 m/s per day); the clock with c²q₁ and c²q₂. Measurement: c r = a(t)·M(t)ᵀd + b + Jk, with M(t) the local frame of the dead-reckoning track. The smoother is Rauch-Tung-Striebel. Outliers: the same mixture as navFit, applied to each TOA's innovation in one pass. On the real surface a horizontal error d lies d²/2R below the tangent plane (7 km at 300 km); the closed loop keeps d small.

The ship scenario

A simulated ship sails for 14 days from 50° N, 5° W at 5 knots and knows its track only from dead reckoning. An unknown current pushes it off that track: after 14 days by 640 km (the median of eight ships). It follows B0329+54 and B0950+08 in two beams, and its SDR clock drifts. Each evening it runs navFilter on all its TOAs so far and folds the next day with the corrected track and clock (runShip; Figure 10.5).

Over eight ships with different currents, clocks and noise, the smoothed track stays within about 70 km rms of the truth with a calibrated start, against 380 km for dead reckoning; a self-calibrated start gives about 100 km (Figure 10.6).

The error bars are checked against a twin: the same smoother on ideal TOAs at the same times, so that only the TOA chain differs (validation). With a calibrated start the two agree. With a self-calibrated start the north error is larger than the twin's in four of the eight ships; the cause is still open (Section 10.5).

A ship with a strong current, a 2.5σ draw of the filter's prior, is pulled towards zero even with ideal data. The calibrated start, the mean-reverting current and these eight ships followed the same day.

At worst-case flux Claude's run

The ship results above are at nominal flux. The design point of that time was the worst case, an SEFD of 7.4·105 Jy instead of 3.2·105 Jy. The same eight ships were sailed again at worst flux, with the same tracks, currents and clocks. At worst flux the ship barely beats dead reckoning.

The cause is the number of TOAs. B0329+54 gives 18–23 TOAs in 14 days instead of about 115, and B0950+08 gives 1–3 instead of about 12. Each B0329+54 TOA then spans 13–19 hours of data. The daily filter needs ten TOAs before it corrects the track, so the ship sails on dead reckoning until day 6–9. The smoothed track is worse than dead reckoning in four of the eight ships, and the real-time track at day 14 in four to five of the eight. The stated errors are too small at worst flux: the pull spread at days 3, 7, 10 and 14 is 1.6 east and 1.2–1.5 north, against 0.85–1.1 for the twin, so most of the excess is in the TOA chain. The per-run pulls that runShip prints are larger still, because navFilter puts the port's 1 km position prior at the first TOA. At worst flux that TOA comes 9–14 hours after departure, when the ship has already drifted 14–41 km.

Summary
  • A site offset shows up as a daily sine in the TOA residuals: 193 µs for 100 km east, from B0329+54.
  • The array's fixed panel times B0329+54 once a day per beam (SNR 11.5 per pass, 459 µs). One beam on the pulsar gives no position; both give ±108 km along one axis in 30 days.
  • navFit fits the sine with a robust mixture model, the clock as correlated noise, and rows averaged over each TOA's data. In the what-if of a tracking mount, with B0329+54 followed through its pass at the array's design point, 30 days give ±9.4 km east and ±11.9 km radial.
  • Extra pulsars at other declinations separate the polar component from the clock; B0950+08 brings most of the gain.
  • navFilter tracks a ship's dead-reckoning error (archived). At the design points before 23 November 2026 it reached about 70 km rms against 380 km for dead reckoning at nominal flux, and was barely better than dead reckoning at worst-case flux.
