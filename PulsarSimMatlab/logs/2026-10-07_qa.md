## 2.4: The LO and nBin

### Why a symmetric LO can hide a sign error

The receiver maps RF to baseband as f_bb = f_RF − f_LO. The classic mistake is a **spectral inversion**: f_bb = f_LO − f_RF, i.e. I and Q swapped or the stream conjugated. That mirrors the spectrum, f → −f.

- **With f_LO = 1.4 GHz in the middle of the band**, 1.2–1.6 GHz maps to −200…+200 MHz. The mirror image of −200…+200 is −200…+200, so the band sits exactly where it should. Every check that only asks where the power is passes, whether the mapping is right or mirrored:
  - band inside the filter;
  - total power;
  - noise bandwidth;
  - the IQ filter checks.
- **The dangerous case is two compensating errors.** If both the IQ stage and the dedispersion stage had the sign flipped, the simulation would be self-consistent and every test would pass. Only real hardware, using the standard convention, would expose it.
- **With f_LO = 1.3 GHz the band sits at −100…+300 MHz.** Its mirror image would be −300…+100, a different place. A mirrored mapping would put half the band outside the dedispersion filter (H = 0 there), so the sweep plots and the SNR would visibly fail.

The asymmetric LO makes a mirror error impossible to miss.

### Is nBin = 2048 enough, and would changing it help?

It's more than enough, and changing it won't improve performance.

Binning only costs information if the bins are coarse compared with the pulse. Each phase bin smooths the profile: the effective pulse width grows as σ_eff² ≈ σ_t² + Δ²/12 for a boxcar bin of width Δ. Linear assignment adds a bit more, of the same order.

| Pulsar | Period | σ_t (pulse) | Δ at 2048 bins | Δ²/12 ÷ σ_t² |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Simulated | 10 ms | 212 µs | 4.9 µs | 4e-5 |
| J0437 | 5.76 ms | ~60 µs | 2.8 µs | 2e-4 |
| Vela | 89 ms | ~720 µs | 44 µs | 3e-4 |

All of these are far below any measurable loss. A rough rule is Δ ≲ σ_t/3, i.e. NBin ≳ 10·P/W50. For J0437 that gives ~400 bins, so 2048 is about 5× more than needed.

Two things don't depend on nBin once the bins are fine:
- **Detection:** the Rice factor 5.51 depends on the template's smoothness (λ₂), not on the number of bins.
- **Matched-filter SNR:** splitting a bin into two doesn't change the total information.

Where nBin *does* matter is cost. A per-channel fold stores weight, weight2 and weightX as [NBin × nSub × nChan × D]. With 2048 bins, 128 channels and 1000 sub-ints, that's ~10 GB. For long observations in step C you'll probably **lower** nBin (e.g. 256–512 for J0437) or group channels into sub-bands.

The knobs that actually improve performance are SEFD (area and T_sys), bandwidth, integration time, dual polarization, channel weighting under scintillation, and an accurate template.

## 2.7: Q-Q plots, the ratio, self-noise

### What a Q-Q plot shows

Take the n normalized errors z_i = (TOA_i − truth)/σ_i and sort them. Plot the k-th smallest against the value N(0,1) predicts for the k-th smallest out of n, which is Φ⁻¹((k − ½)/n).

- If the z's really are N(0,1), the points fall on the line y = x.
- **Steeper than 1:** errors larger than claimed, i.e. error bars too small.
- **Shifted up or down:** bias.
- **Straight in the middle but bending away at the ends (an S shape):** heavy tails, i.e. outliers. This is exactly what the low-SNR sweep points would show.

It beats a histogram because it uses no binning and shows the tails clearly. The tails are where outliers live.

### Is the ratio "empirical spread / theoretical σ"?

Yes. **rms/pred = (rms of the actual errors TOA − truth) / (rms of the error bars the estimator claimed).**

- 1 means honest error bars.
- Below 1 means the error bars are too pessimistic; above 1, too optimistic.
- With n TOAs its own uncertainty is about 1/√(2n): 90 TOAs → ±0.075, which is the ± you saw.
- The reduced χ² (mean of z²) is essentially the same number squared.

### What is self-noise?

It's not that the pulsar's noise fails to follow the envelope. On average it follows it exactly: E[x²] = A²·G(t).

Self-noise is the **estimation scatter** of measuring a variance from a finite number of samples. A detected power bin averages |z|² over B·dt independent samples, so even with zero receiver noise its relative scatter is 1/√(B·dt). When the pulsar is bright, it's the pulsar's own noise that you're averaging, so the scatter is **proportional to the pulse itself**: largest at the peak, zero where there's no pulse.

In `expectedPowerModel`'s variance, css·Ps² + 2·csn·Ps·Pn + cnn·Pn², the three terms are:
- the self-noise term (Ps²);
- the signal × receiver-noise cross term;
- the pure receiver-noise term (Pn²).

**Consequences:**
- SNR per pulse saturates: you can't beat √(B·duration of the pulse), however strong the pulsar.
- FFTFIT stops being optimal, because the flanks it relies on get noisier with the signal.

At −5 dB self-noise is ~4 % of the variance; at −54 dB it's zero for practical purposes. That's why you decided to ignore it.

## 3: Fisher information, the factor 2, Isserlis, weights

### How do you get the (1 + ρp)² Fisher information?

Model each short detected bin as Gaussian:
- **mean** m(t) = P_n·(1 + ρp(t)), i.e. noise power plus pulsar power;
- **variance** m(t)²/(B·dt), the radiometer equation. The variance is the **total** power squared, pulsar included.

For independent Gaussian measurements with mean m_k(θ) and variance v_k, the Fisher information about a parameter θ is I = Σ (∂m_k/∂θ)² / v_k. (There's a second term from v depending on θ; it's smaller by a factor ~2/(B·dt), so it's negligible.)

**For the amplitude (gives the SNR):**
- ∂m/∂(amplitude) = P_n·ρp, so SNR² = Σ (P_n·ρp)²·B·dt / (P_n²(1 + ρp)²).
- Turning the sum into an integral (dt cancels): **SNR² = B·∫ (ρp)²/(1 + ρp)² dt**.

**For the arrival time τ:**
- ∂m/∂τ = −P_n·ρp′, so **1/σ_TOA² = B·∫ (ρp′)²/(1 + ρp)² dt**. This is the Cramér–Rao bound, the best any unbiased estimator can do.

**The two limits:**
- **Weak signal (ρp ≪ 1):** the denominator is 1, so SNR = ρ·√(B·∫p²dt) = ρ·√(B·σ_t·√π) ≈ 388·ρ.
- **Strong signal:** the fraction tends to 1 inside the pulse, so SNR² → B × the time the pulse dominates. That's self-noise saturation.

`expectedPowerModel` does the same with the real tapers; (1 + ρp)²/B is the flat-band special case of css·Ps² + 2csn·Ps·Pn + cnn·Pn².

### Is the factor 2 realistic?

You're right that it doesn't matter physically. It multiplies signal and noise identically, so SNR, TOAs, error bars and detection are all scale-invariant. The pipeline never assumes an absolute gain: `detectPulsar` normalizes by the fitted baseline and `estimateTOA` uses the fitted model m.

It's a **bookkeeping convention**, the standard complex-envelope definition x = Re{z·e^{iωt}}. With it, a tone A·cos keeps |z| = A. It matters only for absolute numbers:
- the baseline 1.233;
- `expectedPowerModel`'s sigScale;
- the plot checks.

Real hardware has an arbitrary gain anyway (amplifiers, ADC scaling).

### What is Isserlis' theorem, and how does it connect to the radiometer equation?

Isserlis' theorem (Wick's theorem in physics): for zero-mean jointly Gaussian variables, a fourth moment splits into products of second moments:

E[x₁x₂x₃x₄] = E[x₁x₂]E[x₃x₄] + E[x₁x₃]E[x₂x₄] + E[x₁x₄]E[x₂x₃].

For complex circular Gaussian voltages this becomes **cov(|z_a|², |z_b|²) = |R(a−b)|²**: the covariance of two power samples is the squared magnitude of the voltage correlation between them.

So yes, it's the **variance of the variance estimate**. A detected bin P̂ = (1/n)·Σ|z_a|² estimates the voltage variance, and

var(P̂) = (1/n²)·Σ_a Σ_b |R(a−b)|².

By Parseval, Σ|R|² ↔ ∫S(f)²df. For a filter W the mean is ∝ ∫W² and the variance ∝ ∫W⁴/dt, so

var/mean² = ∫W⁴ / ((∫W²)²·dt) = 1/(B_n·dt), with **B_n = (∫W²)²/∫W⁴**.

For a flat band that's the familiar 1/(B·dt). The same formula is what `powerCovariance` computes for narrow channels (C(L) = (1/n²)·Σ|R(a−b+Ln)|²), and what `blankingWeights` computes with blanking. It's the mathematical root of the whole noise model.

### What do `weight` and `weightX` do?

For phase bin j of one sub-int, the fold computes sum_j = Σ_k a_kj·P_k, where a_kj is how much of time bin k went into phase bin j (linear assignment). The profile is prof_j = sum_j / weight_j.

| Field | Formula | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| `weight` | Σ_k a_kj (A1b: Σ a·W) | normalizer: how much data is in bin j |
| `weight2` | Σ_k a_kj² (× V) | var(prof_j) = σ²·weight2/weight² (variance of a weighted mean) |
| `weightX(:,:,:,d)` | Σ a_kj·a_k,j+d (× correlations) | cov(prof_j, prof_j+d) = σ²·weightX/(weight_j·weight_j+d) |

**Example:** a time bin that lands 30 % of the way from bin j to j+1 gives 0.7 to j and 0.3 to j+1. It adds 0.49 and 0.09 to the two weight2 values and 0.21 to weightX. Both bins contain part of the same noise, which is why they're correlated.

In the long run the ratios settle at W : W2 : WX = 1 : 2/3 : 1/6, which gives a neighbour correlation of 0.25. That's exactly the 0.2484 measured in `runH0`.

## 4: Spectrum, missing effects, scintillation, bandwidth, model, scripts, SNR, χ², the detector, TOA errors

### Does the f^−1.6 spectrum matter if the envelope is known?

The envelope is a **time** shape; the spectrum is a **frequency** shape, and knowing one doesn't give you the other. You're right that it's mainly about weighting.

1. **Optimal channel weighting.** The best way to combine channels weights each by its pulsar signal-to-noise (a matched filter in frequency). An equal-weight sum loses SNR by a factor √((ΣS_c)²/(N·ΣS_c²)). For f^−1.6 across 1.2–1.6 GHz (a factor 1.58 from bottom to top) that's ≈ 0.99, about **1 %**. Small.
   Scintillation is a different story: some channels can be 3× brighter than others, and SNR weighting gains much more. The uncommitted `'optimal'` weighting with `ChannelGain` s_c in `estimateTOA` is exactly this.
2. **TOA bias.** The TOA of a summed profile is a flux-weighted average over channels. If the DM is slightly wrong (each channel's pulse sits at a slightly different time) or the profile changes shape with frequency (real pulsars do), the spectral weighting shifts the TOA. Scintillation makes those weights random over time, which turns the bias into jitter.
3. **The noise model is unaffected at weak signal.** The variance is dominated by receiver noise, and on the channel path each channel uses its own fitted mean anyway.

### Do the four missing effects need rescheduling?

No reordering is needed, but two points are worth adding.

| Effect | Where it lives | Remark |
|---|---|---|
| Earth's motion / Doppler | D | Already a hard requirement. C's design has a Δφ(t) hook, so D slots in. |
| Scintillation | C (FluxScale(t) hook) + adaptive sub-int length | Already planned. |
| DM variations | E / Block 2 (sub-band TOAs → DM fit, which the channel front end enables) | For J0437, δDM gives ~0.2–2 µs per year, small next to 12 µs TOAs. More relevant for Vela. |
| **Clock** | **not explicitly planned** | **Worth an item in D** (below). |

**The clock point:** a 20 h fold needs the receiver clock to stay coherent across days. A clock whose rate is off by 1e-9 accumulates 72 µs over 20 h, about 6× the J0437 TOA precision. A constant rate offset can be fitted (it looks like a spin-frequency error); random wander can't.

So:
- the clock model belongs in the phase predictor (D);
- "what oscillator does the hardware have?" is a good question for the project.

### Scintillation: what that sentence means, and how it works

Yes, it's **multipath**, exactly like fading in mobile radio.

1. **Mechanism.** The interstellar plasma is turbulent, and its electron density varies from place to place, so the refractive index varies too. It acts like a random phase screen. The signal reaches us along many slightly different paths with slightly different delays. The spread of those delays is the **scattering time τ_d**.
2. **In the time domain**, the pulse is blurred by an exponential tail of length τ_d. At DM 5, τ_d ≈ 0.4 ns against a 0.5 ms pulse, so it's irrelevant for timing.
3. **In the frequency domain**, the paths interfere. Constructive or destructive interference depends on frequency, and the pattern decorrelates over Δν_d ≈ 1/(2π·τ_d) ≈ 0.4 GHz. In comms terms that's the **coherence bandwidth**. The spectrum gets random bright and dark patches ("scintles") 0.1–1 GHz wide.
4. **Why the flux fades ~100 %:** your band is 400 MHz, so you see about one scintle. You can't average many independent patches, and the whole band brightens and dims together. That's **flat fading**, with roughly exponential intensity statistics.
5. **Over time:** the pulsar (often ~100 km/s), Earth and the screen move, so the pattern drifts past you. The **coherence time** is minutes to hours.
6. **At high DM** it flips. τ_d grows roughly as DM^2.2 and f^−4.4, so τ_d reaches µs and Δν_d drops to ~50 kHz. That's frequency-selective fading: thousands of scintles average out, so the flux barely fades, but the µs smearing changes the pulse shape and causes timing noise.

### Why does the small W² vs W⁴ difference matter?

The 1.5 % itself doesn't matter. What matters is using the right integral in the right place:
- **∫W²** for the mean power;
- **(∫W²)²/∫W⁴** for the variance.

1. **The validation precision is high enough to see it.** Several tests resolve ±0.1–2 %, so a 1 % systematic in the error bars would eventually show up as a puzzle.
2. **On real data it can be 10–30 %.** Bandpass ripple, slopes, notch filters and flagged channels all change B_n, and error bars and detection thresholds scale with √B_n.
3. **It was the prototype for A1–A3.** The narrow-channel V and X(L), and the blanking weights, are this same W⁴ calculation, generalized to time lags and masks.
4. **It answered a real question.** Without the exact optimum you couldn't tell whether FFTFIT was 2 % or 0.5 % from optimal (it was 0.5 %).

### How does `expectedPowerModel` work, step by step?

It's ground truth only, used by the plot checks and to print the best possible σ.

1. **Frequency grid** across the band (200,001 points). It builds the simulator's dispersion taper W_f and the observer's dedispersion taper W_i (sin² edges).
2. **Five integrals:**
   - Js = ∫(W_f·W_i)², the pulsar power passed;
   - Jn = ∫W_i², the noise power passed (noise never saw W_f);
   - Iss = ∫(W_f·W_i)⁴, Isn = ∫W_f²·W_i⁴ and Inn = ∫W_i⁴, the fourth-power integrals for the variance.
3. **Mean powers:**
   - pulsar Ps(t) = sigScale·p(t), with sigScale = g²·A²·Js/fsIn;
   - noise Pn = g²·σ_n²·Jn/fsIn.
4. **Variance of a bin of length dt:** var = (css·Ps² + 2·csn·Ps·Pn + cnn·Pn²)/dt, with css = Iss/Js², csn = Isn/(Js·Jn) and cnn = Inn/Jn².
   This is Isserlis: var ∝ ∫S²df with S = pulsar PSD + noise PSD, and squaring the sum gives the three terms. With a flat band all c = 1/B and var = (Ps + Pn)²/(B·dt).
5. **Best achievable** (`snrPulse`, `toaErrPulse`): the Fisher integrals above with that v, over ±8σ of the pulse.

### What exactly is the SNR per sub-int?

It's the **matched-filter SNR of the pulse in one folded sub-int profile**. Correlate the profile with the template: by how many noise standard deviations does the pulse stand out?

Three numbers estimate it, and they agree in the weak regime:
- the theoretical **SNRopt** (from `expectedPowerModel`, × √turns);
- `estimateTOA`'s **b/σ_b** (fitted amplitude over its error);
- `detectPulsar`'s **T0**.

For this pulsar it's ≈ 373·ρ·√N for N turns. It is **not** ρ (per-Hz power ratio) and **not** the SNR of a single bin.

### What does χ² do?

It's a **goodness-of-fit** test, not a general test that the distribution is wrong.

1. After fitting a + b·template(φ − τ), take the residuals r_j = p_j − m_j on the on-pulse bins.
2. Divide each by its predicted noise std √v_j.
3. Square, sum, and divide by (n − 3) (three fitted parameters).

If the data really are template + noise with the predicted variance, each normalized residual is N(0,1), and the average of the squares is 1 ± √(2/n) ≈ 1 ± 0.09.

So it tests the **shape model and the noise model together**. It goes ≫ 1 if:
- the profile isn't template-shaped (a radar spike: χ² ~1000);
- or the noise is larger than modelled.

Neighbour correlations don't change its mean (E[z_j²] = 1 regardless); they only widen its scatter a little.

### How is the detector threshold η calculated?

**Known phase:** T0 = cᵀ(p − a)/√(cᵀ·Cov·c), a weighted sum of Gaussian bins divided by its exact standard deviation. Under H0 it's exactly N(0,1), so

P_FA = P(T0 > η) = 1 − Φ(η) → **η = Φ⁻¹(1 − P_FA) = √2·erfcinv(2·P_FA)** = 3.09 for 1e-3.

Under H1, T0 has mean SNR, so **P_D = 1 − Φ(η − SNR)**.

The Neyman–Pearson lemma says the likelihood-ratio test gives the highest P_D for a given P_FA. For a known shape in Gaussian noise that's a matched filter. Strictly the optimal filter is Cov⁻¹·c, but the pulse spans ~100 bins while the noise correlation only acts at one-bin scale. At the template's low harmonics Cov is effectively a constant, so c is effectively optimal and P_FA is exact either way.

**Unknown phase:** `fzero` solves Q(η) + 5.51·e^{−η²/2} = P_FA, giving 4.15 (Rice, below).

### Known vs unknown phase: what are they and why both?

- **Known phase (tracking).** You already know your position and clock well enough to predict where the pulse is in the profile. You test only at that phase, one test, threshold 3.09.
- **Unknown phase (acquisition, cold start).** Your position is uncertain by more than a pulse width, so the pulse could be anywhere. 1 ms of timing is 300 km; one J0437 turn (5.76 ms) is ~1700 km. You search every phase and take the maximum. More chances for noise means a higher threshold (4.15) and more SNR needed.

`main.m` defines good TOAs with the **unknown-phase** detection, because FFTFIT itself searches all phases for the highest peak. The fair false-alarm question is "is the best peak anywhere real?"

### What is Rice's formula?

The question is the chance that the **maximum** over phase of T(τ) exceeds η when there's only noise. It's not simply "number of bins × Q(η)", because neighbouring shifts are almost identical (a smooth template moved by one bin gives nearly the same T). The trials are strongly dependent.

Rice (1944) gave the expected number of **upcrossings** of level η per unit length for a smooth stationary Gaussian process with unit variance: (√λ₂/2π)·e^{−η²/2}. Here λ₂ is "how fast the process wiggles": the second spectral moment, Σ(2πk)²|S_k|²/Σ|S_k|².

The maximum exceeds η if T is already above η at the start, or crosses it somewhere:

**P(max > η) ≤ Q(η) + (√λ₂/2π)·e^{−η²/2}.**

In the tail, crossings are rare and at most one, so the bound is tight.

- **Intuition:** √λ₂/2π ≈ the **effective number of independent phase trials per turn**.
- **Check against the template:** a Gaussian template with σ = 0.0212 turns gives √λ₂/2π ≈ 1/(2π·σ·√2) ≈ 5.3, close to the 5.51 in the code (the difference comes from mean subtraction and the finite bins). Narrow pulse, more independent trials, higher threshold.
- **Measured:** `runH0` gave Tmax exceedances of 88/13/1 against 90/9/0.9 predicted.

### Why does FFTFIT give TOA errors directly, and is there a more physical alternative?

**What FFTFIT does:**
1. τ̂ is where the cross-correlation C(τ) = Σ_j p_j·s(φ_j − τ) peaks, so C′(τ̂) = 0.
2. C′ is a **linear** combination of the data: C′ = Σ d_j·p_j, with d_j ∝ the template slope.
3. Noise changes C′ by δC′, and the peak moves until C′ = 0 again: C′ + C″·δτ = 0, so δτ = −δC′/C″.
4. Therefore **var(τ) = var(δC′)/C″² = dᵀ·Cov·d / C″²**.

That's standard first-order error propagation (the delta method) for an estimator defined implicitly by "the peak". It's logical, and for white noise it equals the least-squares/Fisher error.

**The physical picture:** timing information lives on the **flanks**, where the profile changes fastest. Shifting the pulse by δt changes each flank bin by s′·δt, and noise in those bins can fake a shift.
- For a Gaussian pulse this gives **σ_TOA = √2·σ_t/SNR**.
- At SNR 7 with σ_t = 212 µs: ~43 µs.
- Sharp pulse and high SNR mean a curved peak (large |C″|) and a small error.

**What it assumes:**
- you're on the **right** peak (hence outliers at low SNR, and detection);
- the template is right (hence χ²).

**Alternatives, more physical or more general:**

1. **Likelihood scan (Δχ²), my recommendation as the most transparent.** For every τ, refit a and b and compute χ²(τ) = Σ(p_j − a − b·s(φ_j − τ))²/v_j. The FFT gives this for all τ at once, almost for free.
   - The 1σ interval is where χ² rises by 1 above its minimum.
   - At high SNR it agrees with the curvature formula (it *is* the curvature formula, locally).
   - At low SNR it **shows** the second minima, the noise peaks that become outliers.
   - Read as a posterior, p(τ | data) ∝ e^{−χ²/2} is exactly what a navigation filter (Kalman, particle) would want: a full, possibly multi-peaked distribution instead of one σ.
2. **Monte Carlo or bootstrap:** draw many noise realizations from the noise model, refit, take the spread. It captures nonlinearity, but it's expensive. Your validation scripts do this, using the ground truth.
3. **Empirical noise:** estimate Cov from off-pulse residuals (`'offpulse'` model) instead of the radiometer theory.
4. **Sub-int scatter:** fit the TOAs of many sub-ints and use their scatter (the classic pulsar-timing "EFAC" correction).
5. **Sandwich covariance A⁻¹BA⁻¹:** what the uncommitted `'optimal'` weighted fit uses. It's honest even when the fit weights aren't the true inverse covariance.

## 5: Detection vs χ², T0 vs Tmax, the centroid

### How is detection different from χ²?

They ask different questions:
- **detection:** "Is there significant pulse-shaped power?" One number: the template-weighted sum in noise units.
- **χ²:** "Given the best fit, is what's left just noise?"

| Situation | Detection (T) | χ² | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| noise only | small → not detected | ≈ 1 | rejected by detection |
| real pulse | large | ≈ 1 | good |
| radar spike | large (it correlates) | ≫ 1 | rejected by χ² |
| pulse + carrier | large | ~1.3 | kept (by design) |

Detection catches **"nothing there"** (noise-peak outliers); χ² catches **"something wrong there"**.

### What's the difference between T0 and Tmax?

- **T0:** the matched-filter value at the **predicted** phase. One test, N(0,1) under H0.
- **Tmax:** the **largest** value over all phase shifts, with `phaseMax` where it occurs. Under H0 it's the max of ~5.5 effective independent N(0,1)s, so it's typically 1.5–2 even with no pulsar.

With a pulsar present and a correct ephemeris, T0 ≈ Tmax ≈ SNR, and `phaseMax` ≈ the pulse phase.

### 5.4: The centroid, window wings, lever arm

The plot functions cross-check FFTFIT with a **centre of mass**: c = Σ t·Q(t) / Σ Q(t), where Q = power minus baseline, over a window of ±4σ around the expected pulse.

- **Lever arm:** a noise blip δQ in a bin at time t shifts the centroid by (t − c)·δQ/ΣQ. Bins far from the centre move it more, like torque on a beam.
- **Window wings:** the outer parts of the window (2–4σ from the centre) contain almost no pulse, because the Gaussian is tiny there. They still contain full noise, and they have the biggest lever arms, so they dominate the centroid's noise.
- **FFTFIT** weights by the slope s′, which goes to zero in the wings, so it ignores them. That's why it's ~2× less noisy: 2.88 µs vs 6.2 µs per pulse.

The +3.7 µs was a 1.8σ fluctuation in this noisier estimator, from one noise seed.

## 7: A statistical model for folded profiles, plus RFI and excision

**The core:** for sub-int s, channel c, phase bin j:

prof_csj = μ_csj + ε_csj, with ε_cs ~ N(0, Σ_cs), independent across channels (and, to a good approximation, across sub-ints).

**Why it's valid:**
1. Each phase bin sums ~10⁶+ detected samples, so by the central limit theorem it's Gaussian.
2. The mean and covariance are known exactly (validated in Block 1, A1a and A1b).
3. The estimators are linear or quadratic in the data, so higher moments don't matter.

**Mean** (in SEFD units, per channel):

μ_cj = 1 + (S_c(t)/SEFD_c)·⟨f(φ_j − Δφ(t))⟩, with:
- **S_c(t)** = S_mean·(f_c/f₀)^α·g_scint(c, t): spectrum and scintillation;
- **f** = the true profile shape, with mean 1 over a turn;
- **⟨⟩** = smoothing by the phase-bin kernel and by the drift of Δφ(t) = φ_true − φ_ephem within the sub-int. That drift is where Doppler, clock and position errors enter (D).

**Covariance** (from the fold weights):
- var_j = σ_c²·W2_j/W_j²;
- cov(j, j+d) = σ_c²·WX_jd/(W_j·W_j+d);
- σ_c² = μ_c² / (n_pol·B_c·binDt), times V/X for narrow channels.

μ contains the pulsar, so self-noise is included automatically. Without RFI, the asymptotic weights are W = n, W2 = (2/3)n and WX = n/6, plus the A1a corrections.

**Sampling:** ε = diag(σ_j)·L·z with z ~ N(0, I) and L the Cholesky factor of the banded circular correlation matrix (or an FFT, since it's nearly stationary). 128 channels × 2048 bins × 1000 sub-ints takes seconds.

**RFI, excision and the consequences, in three layers.** All are calibrated from step B on real voltage-level simulations:

1. **Mask statistics (what gets blanked).** Per RFI source, a generative model of the blanking:
   - radar: pulses at its PRF in a few channels, each blanking pulse + guard, gated by antenna rotation;
   - GNSS: whole channels flagged permanently;
   - impulses: short blanks in all channels;
   - false flags on clean data, at the detector's false-flag rate.
2. **Effect on the weights.** From the mask follow W, V and X per time bin (`blankingWeights`, exact). At fold level there are two cases:
   - *Not phase-locked* (the usual case: topocentric RFI periods don't match the pulsar's, and the barycentric phase drift smears them further). Blanking spreads evenly over phase: W_j ≈ n·ν_cs with valid fraction ν_cs, and V(ν), X(ν) from lookup tables per RFI type. Experiment 3a showed no simple rule works, so the tables come from `blankingWeights` on representative masks.
   - *Phase-locked* (a harmonic PRF, e.g. 400 Hz). Phase-dependent W_j must be modelled explicitly. Because the fold divides by Σa·W (A1b), **the mean profile stays unbiased** and only the noise grows: var ≈ σ²·W2/W².
3. **Residual (missed) RFI.** Excision isn't perfect:
   - an **excess-noise factor** κ_cs ≥ 1 multiplies Σ. The observer's model doesn't know κ, so it shows up as `noiseRatio` > 1, exactly as in reality;
   - with probability p_corrupt per sub-int, add a radar-like spike at a random phase. This tests the χ² flag and outlier rejection.

**SNR consequence:** per channel SNR_cs ∝ √(valid data) (V/X refine that), and SNR² = Σ_c SNR_c². Whole flagged channels remove their share of the band:
- GNSS L1 C/A (~2 MHz): ~1 channel;
- L2 P-code (~20 MHz): ~7 channels.

**The ground-truth split stays clean:** the simulator knows ν, κ and the true phase; the observer receives only the fold struct with its W, V and X (what excision would have reported). `estimateTOA` and `detectPulsar` then run unchanged.

## 8.8: What are data weights?

For every detected time bin k and channel c, the number **W_kc ∈ [0, 1]** is the **fraction of valid (non-blanked) data in that bin**.

**Why it's a fraction and not 0 or 1:** blanking happens **before** dedispersion, and each dedispersed sample is a mix of ~ms of input samples. If some of those were blanked, the output is "partly real".

**How the fold uses it:**
- The bin's expected power is W_kc·m_c.
- The fold computes prof_j = Σ a·P / **Σ a·W**, instead of dividing by Σ a.
- A bin with W = 0.5 contributes half the power to the numerator and 0.5 to the denominator. It counts as half a sample, with the **right** mean.
- A fully blanked bin contributes 0/0, i.e. nothing.
- So the profile stays unbiased even when blanking prefers certain pulse phases.

Each bin also carries **V** and **X(L)**: its own variance and its covariances with later bins, which feed weight2 and weightX. Together, W, V and X form "the data weights" (the weight file [nChan × (2+Lmax) × N]).

When combining channels (A2), a channel that is **completely** empty in some phase bins of a sub-int, while other channels have data there, is **left out of that sub-int**. Otherwise the sum would have a dip that pulls the TOA. Channels with partial data (0 < W) stay in.

## Every test, in detail

There are three kinds of check, and most tests use all three:
- **Regression:** a changed function reproduces old outputs **bit for bit** where nothing should change.
- **Algebra:** the code equals the formula, computed independently (often a slow explicit matrix), to machine precision (1e-15).
- **Physics/statistics:** measured noisy quantities match predictions within n·σ, with σ predicted, not guessed.

### `tests/testChannelizeIQ.m` (~10 s): the filterbank

1. **Tones.** Four pure tones are placed at, beside and far from channel centres. Every output sample of the relevant channels must equal the exact prediction A·e^{iφ}·Hc(Δf)·e^{i2πΔf·t}. It passes to 2.7e-7, which is single-precision rounding.
   One test pins down four things at once: frequency mapping (which channel), LO phase (the φ), timing (the t of each sample, group delay removed) and gain (|Hc| = 1 ± 1.2e-4 in the passband). The far tone checks the stopband (−80.2 dB).
2. **White noise** (2^23 samples). Output power per channel / Σh² must be 1 (1.0007), and the passband PSD flat (1.0004). This checks the filter's noise gain, which matters because the noise model depends on it.
3. **Real data** (seed 43). The sum of channel band powers / IQ band power = 1.00007. That's Parseval, and it shows the 128 channels tile the band with no gaps or double counting.

### `tests/testDedisperseChannels.m` (~15 s): per-channel dedispersion

1. **Regression:** the full-band `applyInverseDispersion` (with the new `AllowRefOutsideBand` option off) reproduces `test_IQ_dedispersed.dat` bit for bit.
2. **Commutation (the precise test).** Channelize the full-band dedispersed data, and compare with dedispersing each channel of the channelized data. Since both are linear filters, the order shouldn't matter. Result: correlation ≥ 0.999993, phase ≤ 1.2e-5 rad, power 1 ± 3e-5. That proves the chirp per channel, the inter-channel delays to 1.6 GHz and the phases are all exact.
3. **End to end:** TOAs from the channel path vs the full-band path on the same data. The differences must scatter like σ·√(2(1−ρ)) with ρ = 0.979, the predicted correlation between the two paths' noise. Tested as a χ² within the 99.8 % range, with the mean within 3.29/√n.
4. **Time-bin noise:** measured variance and lag-1 correlation of detected time bins vs the prediction from the channel spectrum: 0.884 vs 0.888 ± 0.005, and 0.054 vs 0.052. This showed the narrow-channel effect is real.

### `tests/expBlankingVariance.m` (~15 s): experiment 3a, what blanking does to noise

Noise only, the bottom channel, DM 5 and DM 100, and three masks:
- radar: 4 µs at 373 Hz;
- random 50 µs blanks (10 %);
- 1 ms gaps every 5 ms (20 %).

Checks:
- **Mean:** P/W = 1 in every valid-fraction group.
- **Variance:** measured/exact = 1 ± 0.002 over ~1e6 bins. "Exact" comes from the mask convolutions, cross-checked against a direct 2n×2n covariance matrix to 7e-8.
- **Models:** the w² and w models are shown for comparison. They're off by up to ±45 %, which is the result that killed the "g(w)" idea.

### `tests/testDetectChannels.m` (~1 s): power per channel and its noise numbers

1. `powerCovariance` (from the spectrum formula) vs the same statistics computed from the **actual** dedispersion impulse response of channel 1: identical to 6e-10.
2. The interleaved [128 × nBins] file vs `detectPower` run per channel: rows bit-identical.
3. **Fold compatibility:** folding the 128-channel file and summing channels equals folding the summed power (1.2e-8).
4. **Measured on real data** (68,803 off-pulse bins × 128 channels): V 0.8866 vs 0.8877 ± 0.0006; X₁₋₃ match; the variance of 5-bin sums is 0.9685 vs 0.9692. (The old independent-bin model says 1.)

### `tests/makeFoldReference.m`: not a test

It froze four folds from the **pre-A1** `foldProfile`: full band linear, nearest, 3 turns per sub-int, and 128 channels. They're the reference for bit-identity.

### `tests/testFoldWeights.m` (~7 s): A1a and A1b

1. **Regression:** without new options, the four reference folds are reproduced bit for bit.
2. **Algebra:** synthetic data with a deliberately awkward f0, F1, chunking and exaggerated correlation coefficients. weight2/weightX equal Aᵀ·C·A, built explicitly from the assignment matrix A, to 6e-16. Nothing beyond lag D; zero coefficients give the default bit for bit.
3. **Physics:** on the 128-channel data, measured phase-bin variance and lag-1/2 covariances vs the new and old models: new −1.4σ / +0.6σ / −0.1σ, old **−13.1σ** / +5.9σ.
4. **Constant weight file** (W = 1, constant V/X) gives exactly the `NoiseCoeffs` fold: two code paths, same answer.
5. **Random weight streams** (3 channels, 10 % zeros): weight = AᵀW and weight2/weightX = AᵀCA per channel to 7e-16.
6. **Fake blanking** (15 % random, plus phase-correlated blanks at phase 0.30–0.36):
   - the weighted mean is unbiased (−0.0019 ± 0.0009), while the naive fold is −0.574 off exactly as predicted;
   - the noise matches the model.

### `tests/makeToaReference.m`: not a test

It froze `estimateTOA`/`detectPulsar` outputs **before** A2 on the frozen folds, in five cases: linear, off-pulse noise model, partial coverage 0.3 with gap filling, nearest, and 3 turns. Saved to `data/mc/toaRef_preA2.mat`.

### `tests/testChannelTOA.m` (~5 s, committed by the other session): A2a and A2b

1. **`estimateTOA` regression:** single-channel folds give the pre-A2 outputs bit for bit, in all five cases.
2. **`estimateTOA` algebra:** phaseErr = √(dᵀΣd)/|C″| and ampErr = √(cᵀΣc)/Σ|S|², with Σ the **full** covariance of the combined profile, built explicitly channel by channel. Two cases:
   - 128 channels with shared weights and 3 lags;
   - per-channel fake blanking, where channels 1–10 are blanked in every other turn so they're **excluded** from those sub-ints by the exclusion rule.
   Also, Bnoise per channel = scalar Bnoise.
3. **`estimateTOA` physics:**
   - (a) 128-channel fold vs the old summed-power path: same profile, so same TOAs to < 1e-4σ;
   - (b) blanked vs unblanked TOA differences consistent with zero.
4. **`detectPulsar` regression:** bit-identical to pre-A2.
5. **`detectPulsar` algebra:** T0, normProfile and Tmax equal the explicit formulas with the full H0 covariance (each channel at its own baseline a_c).
6. **`detectPulsar` physics:** `noiseRatio` = 1 within 4σ on the channel path, blanked and unblanked. Per-channel and summed paths give the same best phase and detections.

### `tests/testBlankingWeights.m` (in progress, uncommitted, A3a)

1. **Exactness:** W, V and X(1..7) from `blankingWeights` vs a direct per-bin calculation with the full covariance matrix H·K·H′. Bottom, middle and top channels; blanks of 1–3001 samples, including overlapping ones.
2. **Consistency:** an empty mask gives the A1a fold; far from blanks, the unblanked constants; a messy mask gives the same result as the equivalent clean one.
3. **End to end:** blank the channel voltages **before** dedispersion with a whole zoo of RFI types, and run the full chain. The zoo includes a 400 Hz radar locked to the pulsar and blanks right on the pulse. Then check: means unbiased per W group; noise vs weight2/weightX per valid-fraction group; channel exclusion as the rule says; TOAs consistent; noise ratio 1.
